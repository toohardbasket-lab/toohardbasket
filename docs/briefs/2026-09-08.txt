AUSTRALIAN PUBLIC INTEREST ALLIANCE
Closed, not answered
One template sentence has closed 260 parliamentary inquiries since May 2024, 39 of them on a
single day
Evidence brief · background for media · figures as at 8 September 2026 · not for publication without the contact below
Every figure is reproducible from the published dataset. See the last page.
At a glance
• 260 of the 624 government responses to committee reports tabled since April 2022, two in five, dispose of
the report with one repeated template sentence and take a position on nothing.
• 39 of them were tabled on one day, 13 August 2024. Every response the government tabled that day was
the form letter.
• Read recommendation by recommendation, the responses state a position on 1,030 of the 3,504
recommendations they cover: 29 per cent.
The finding
When a parliamentary committee reports, the government is expected to say what it will do about the
recommendations. Since May 2024 the government has often done that with a sentence that says nothing. We
machine-read every government response to a committee report tabled since April 2022: 624 documents. 260
of them, two in five, dispose of the committee's report with one repeated template sentence, accepting
nothing. That sentence, in its most common form:
“The Government notes this recommendation. However, given the passage of time since this report was
tabled, a substantive Government response is no longer appropriate.”
It has been written 2,266 times across those 260 documents. That is a count of sentences, not of
recommendations: 102 of the 260 use it once, closing a committee's entire report in a single line, and the rest
repeat it recommendation by recommendation. Where a response quotes each recommendation and answers
each with the sentence exactly once, the arithmetic can be checked on the face of the document. On that test,
81 of the 260, at least 886 individual recommendations were disposed of this way. It is a floor and a
conservative one: it sets aside the other 179 closures entirely, including 72 that close a report without naming
a single recommendation.
The day it was used 39 times. On 13 August 2024, a Tuesday in a sitting week, the government tabled 39
responses to committee reports. All 39 were the form letter: 392 copies of the sentence in a single day's
tabling. They closed reports of the Senate's economics and corporations committees tabled between May 2016
and March 2022: among them the Senate's three corporate tax avoidance reports of 2015 to 2018 (the
sentence 44 times between them), Superbad, the 2017 report on wage theft and unpaid superannuation (32
times), and the 2018 report on the life insurance industry (49). Governments table many documents on a
sitting day. We have found no other day on which 39 responses said the same thing, and no one who has
counted it.
It is not only old reports. The sentence is a judgment that the report is too old to answer, and it has been
applied without regard to age. Lessons to be learned in relation to the Australian bushfire season 2019–20, the
Senate Finance and Public Administration References Committee's final report, was tabled on 2 December
2021 and closed in May 2024, 894 days later, with the sentence 18 times, once under each recommendation
the response lists. A National Disgrace, the 2016 report on the exploitation of temporary work visa holders (33
recommendations), was closed the same week. Out-of-pocket costs in Australian healthcare (2014) and
Financial and tax practices of for-profit aged care providers (2018) were each closed in October 2024 in a single
line.

Recommendation by recommendation
The form letter is the most visible way of not answering. To see the rest, we read every response
recommendation by recommendation: 3,504 recommendations across 445 responses, each matched to the
government's own words about it. A recommendation counts as answered only where those words state a
position: supported, agreed, accepted, endorsed, implemented, rejected, declined or not supported, in full, in
part or in principle. Nothing is inferred. A response that says only that a recommendation is “noted” has stated
no position, and is counted that way.
1,030 of the 3,504, 29 per cent, received a stated position. Of those, 504 were accepted outright, 445 in part
or in principle, and 81 were not accepted. The other 2,474 were noted without a position (567), closed with the
form letter (1,427), or passed over by a response that addressed the report as a whole and never reached the
recommendations (480). Of the 445 responses, 250 took a position on nothing the committee asked; 58 took
one on everything.

What the government will say, and what the record says
Four replies are available to a press office, and three of them are true. We would rather give them than have
them put to us.
“We inherited a backlog of hundreds of reports.” True. The Senate's outstanding count climbs from 2015 and
peaks in March 2024, under governments of both persuasions. What is particular to this government is the
method of clearing it: every one of the 260 form letters was tabled under it, 205 in 2024, 4 in 2025 and 51 so
far in 2026, and the reports closed on 13 August 2024 were all tabled between 2016 and March 2022.
“This year we have tabled more substantive responses than any government on record.” Also true: of the
206 responses read so far in 2026, 155 take a position on at least some recommendations, more substantive
responses in eight months than any full year in the Senate's 26-year record. Substantive is a low bar, though:
read recommendation by recommendation, this year's responses stated a position on 373 of the 990
recommendations put to them, 38 per cent. The 51 form letters tabled alongside them are still more than in
the whole of 2025 (4), and a report closed by a form letter has left the backlog exactly as if it had been
answered.
“The Senate's outstanding count is the lowest in two decades.” True: a report leaves the count whichever
kind of response it gets. The number that shows the difference is how many responses were tabled and what
they said.
“Reports on bills do not require a separate response.” Partly true: by title, 59 of the 260 closures answer
reports on bills, which the Senate does not require a separate response to. Set every one of them aside and the
share rises: 201 of the remaining 439 responses, 46 per cent, are form letters.
What it looks like when it reaches the oldest file
On 1 April 2026 the government tabled its response to the Senate's children-overboard inquiry, the Select
Committee on A Certain Maritime Incident, whose report was tabled on 23 October 2002. The response arrived
8,561 days, 23 years and 5 months, after the report, against the Senate's three-month standard. It is the
longest gap between a committee report and a government response anywhere in the Senate's register.
It uses the sentence above. Eighteen entries, the committee's seventeen recommendations and the one
recommendation from the minority report, and the same words eighteen times. Not one is accepted,
supported or agreed to. The response was authored by the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, and
its own introduction concedes: “The then Howard government did not provide a response within the
mandated timeframe.”
Two of the eighteen, quoted in full from the response document, where each recommendation is reproduced
immediately above the sentence closing it. The response numbers them 6 and 10:
“The Committee recommends that the Australian Public Service Commission, in consultation with the
Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet, prepare guidelines addressing the responsibilities of agency
heads in circumstances where a minister fails to act on advice which corrects factual misinformation of
public importance. The guidelines should give particular consideration to ensuring that the Prime Minister
is provided with the correcting information, if the Minister refuses to correct the public record.”
“The Committee recommends that an appropriate parliamentary committee develop recommendations
concerning suitable frameworks, mechanisms and procedures by which ministerial advisers may be
rendered directly accountable to parliament in ways commensurate with those which currently apply to
public servants.”
Both are about what happens when a minister will not correct the public record, and who answers for the staff
around a minister. Both were closed in the same words as the other sixteen. The report went unanswered
under seven prime ministers of both persuasions; the seventh closed it with a form letter.

What is still waiting
Two registers, one per chamber, each following its presiding officer's own record. As at today the President of
the Senate's report leaves 81 reports awaiting a government response: the report recorded 98 at 30 June, and
17 have been answered since and taken off. The Speaker's schedule counts 45 as awaiting a response; one of
those carries a 2022 response date that the government's own status report also records, so we treat it as
answered, and 7 more have been answered since, leaving 37. The two counts are never added: most of what
is on both is joint committee reports, listed by both officers, and 23 reports appear on both registers at once.
The two lists do not hold the same joint reports. The two officers exclude different categories, and the House
removed everything presented before July 2019 while the Senate did not.
The government publishes its own status report for each chamber. Of the 81 Senate reports outstanding today,
45 were recorded as “being considered” in the government's status report as at 31 March 2026, the most
recent it has published, which says nothing more about them than that. The oldest of them was tabled on 25
November 2014, 11 years and 9 months ago, a Senate inquiry into a bill to abolish the Office of the Australian
Information Commissioner. The bill lapsed when Parliament was prorogued on 17 April 2016; the inquiry into it
is still recorded as awaiting a response. The President has tabled 9 of these status reports since June 2022 and
every one is on the public record. We read all 9: that report is marked “being considered” in each of them, and
so are 4 others. The government has been saying the same thing about those 5, twice a year, for four years.
What it said before June 2022 we cannot show, because the Tabled Documents register does not go back
further.
Two limits on that figure. The government's House status report has only three things it can say about a
report: that a response was tabled, that a full response has been provided, or that the response is being
considered. So “all 37 House reports are being considered” is close to a restatement of “37 are outstanding”.
And the Senate flag comes from the government's status report as at 31 March 2026, not 30 June.
One thing the House register does not show. Its backlog reaches back only to 2020, and that is not the House
performing better. Footnote 3 of the Speaker's schedule records that in June 2023 the Liaison Committee of
Chairs and Deputy Chairs agreed to recommend removing every outstanding government response for a report
presented before 2 July 2019, unless the committee's successor resolved to keep it. Seven of those reports are
still on the Senate register, where the President kept them.
Whether the deadline was met
The Senate has asked for a response within three months since 1973; the House within six months since 2010.
Of the 1,458 responses since 2000 whose report date the Senate's own register records, 74 arrived within
three months: 5.1 per cent. The median took 416 days, and 55 per cent took more than a year. No government
in 26 years reaches one response in ten inside the deadline, on either way of attributing them, by who tabled
the response or by who was in office when the report was tabled. The best any government manages, on
either reading, is 8.9 per cent.
What these measures do not catch
The 260 is a floor, not a ceiling. The form-letter test detects one form of non-answer: the repeated template
sentence disposing of every recommendation. The recommendation-by-recommendation reading catches two
more, a response that notes a recommendation and says nothing about it, and one that addresses the report
as a whole and never reaches the recommendations, and counts both as no position. What neither catches is a
response that states a position and then describes existing policy, or one that answers a different question
from the one asked. We name those forms on the methods page, with a document to read for each, because
we would rather publish the limit than a larger number we cannot defend. And both measure answers, not
outcomes: a government can act on a recommendation without answering the committee, and can accept one
and do nothing. This site records only what the government said, and when. Everything it has had to correct is
in the corrections log, dated.
Questions the record supports
The same sentence appears in responses from different departments, which implies a central template.
Questions a journalist could put, none of which we can answer from the record:
• Who drafted the passage-of-time response, when was the decision taken to use it, and what guidance
went to departments? (The drafting and the guidance are documents, and are the subject of a Freedom of
Information request to the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet.)
• Who decided that 39 responses would be tabled on 13 August 2024, and on what criteria were reports
from 2016 to 2022 judged too old to answer?
• Does the government consider that a passage-of-time response discharges the Senate's resolution of 14
March 1973?
• For the five reports recorded as “being considered” in every status report since June 2022, including a
2014 freedom-of-information inquiry, does a draft response exist, and which minister holds it?
• Of the recommendations the government has “noted” without a position since 2022, 563 so far, how many
has it since acted on, and where is that recorded?
Supplementary Budget Estimates sit from 26 to 29 October. Each of these can be asked there, of the
department that authored the response.
For your round
Every response and every register row on the site links to the Parliament's own document. Tell us your round
(health, veterans, housing, family violence, the environment, workplace) and we will send you the committee
recommendations on it that are still awaiting a response, were closed with the sentence, or were noted
without a position, each one checked by hand against the document before it goes to you.
Or search it yourself. The site's recommendations page indexes 5,259 recommendations from 575 documents,
1,193 of them awaiting a response right now. Each is shown with the words the government wrote about it, or
the fact that it wrote none, and where those words state a position, the verdict: accepted, in part or in
principle, or not accepted. Where a response answered a report as a whole without taking its
recommendations one by one, each of those recommendations is shown with that fact beside it. Every result
links to the report and to the response. Type a topic; the rows still waiting come first, and the results can be
narrowed to recommendations that got no position, or were not accepted. Where the site could not separate
the government's words from the committee's it says so rather than guessing, and where the document names
a dissenting or minority author for a recommendation, that is shown rather than the committee's name. It
cannot tell you whether a recommendation was implemented; only the record of the answer.
