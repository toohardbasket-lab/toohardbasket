Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 On 4 September 2025, the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability
Amendment (Ban Unethical Contractors) Bill 2025 (the bill) was introduced into
the Senate by Senator Barbara Pocock.1
1.2 On 30 October 2025, the Senate referred the bill to the Senate Finance and Public
Administration Committee for inquiry and report by 17 March 2026.2 On
23 March 2026, the Senate agreed to extend the reporting date to 26 March 2026.3
Purpose and provisions of the bill
1.3 The bill would amend the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act
2013 (PGPA Act) to require that government procurement contracts cannot be
entered into with potential suppliers or tenderers that have engaged in
'unethical conduct' for a period of five years.4
1.4 According to the Explanatory Memorandum, the bill would define 'unethical
conduct' to include:
 a conviction or pecuniary penalty within the previous three years;
 a proven record of poor labour practices or tax avoidance; or
 other conduct that has or is likely to have an adverse impact on the integrity
of or public confidence in the Commonwealth.5
1.5 The bill would also provide for the discretionary exclusion for up to five years of
such suppliers or tenderers and their directors, senior officers or employees
(their 'personnel') or related entities.6 Further, the bill would require unethical
conduct of such related entities or their personnel to be considered during
procurement processes.7
1.6 Under the provisions of the bill, a public register of excluded entities would be
established, setting out, among other matters, the period of exclusion, and each
decision to enter into a procurement contract with an entity despite its personnel

Background to the bill
1.7 The bill sits within a broader discussion regarding the engagement of consulting
services within the Australian Public Service (APS), following the 'PwC tax leaks
scandal' that came to light in 2023.10
1.8 Moreover, it is also informed by the adoption of 'debarment regimes' by some
Australian and international governments, where governments set grounds,
processes and governance standards to assist and encourage business to
improve their practices, and to prevent suppliers accessing government
procurement processes in cases where wrongdoing has been found.11
1.9 In the 47th Parliament, the Senate Finance and Public Administration References
Committee (References Committee) looked into the misuse of Commonwealth
information by PwC in its Inquiry into management and assurance of integrity by
consulting services (Consulting Inquiry), which tabled its final report in June
2024.12 It summed up the PwC matter as follows:

and consulting firm PwC and one of its partners, Mr Peter Collins, had been

Treasury consultations and through his engagement with the Board of

others both in Australia and overseas.

and potential new clients of PwC to avoid the anti-avoidance tax laws to be

Commonwealth Procurement Rules
1.19 The Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) are issued by the Minister for
Finance under the PGPA Act,24 and apply to all non-Corporate Commonwealth
entities and prescribed corporate Commonwealth entities listed in section 30 of
the PGPA Rule.25
1.20 The CPRs require officials responsible for a procurement to be satisfied that the
procurement achieves a value for money outcome, complies with the CPRs and
allows for the exclusion of suppliers in certain circumstances. For example,
officials must make reasonable inquiries that the procurement is carried out
considering relevant regulations or regulatory frameworks, including tenderers'
practices regarding labour regulations and ethical employment practices and
workplace health and safety. Tenderers may also be excluded on grounds such
as bankruptcy, insolvency, false declarations or significant deficiencies in
performance under a prior contract.26
AusTender
1.21 AusTender is the Australian Government's centralised procurement
information system. It provides a single portal to connect government entities
with suppliers, through the publication of business opportunities; and provides
transparency of contracts awarded following a procurement process.27

Commonwealth Supplier Code of Conduct and Notification of the Significant
Event clauses
1.24 The Commonwealth Supplier Code of Conduct (Code) was introduced in July
2024 and must be incorporated into all Commonwealth contracts (unless
otherwise determined by an accountable authority) entered into from 1 July
2024.30 Finance explained in its submission that the Code requires suppliers to
government:
… to uphold business integrity, demonstrate high standards of professional
conduct and corporate citizenship, meet taxation obligations, undertake and
cooperate with audits and assessments, act to prevent involuntary labour
and human rights abuse, and respect employee rights [and]
… places a positive duty on suppliers to take proactive action to prevent and
discourage breaches of the Code. Where requested by the Commonwealth,
tenderers and suppliers must be able to demonstrate they have appropriate
policies, frameworks, or similar, in place regarding ethics, governance and
accountability to comply with the expectations in the Code. Failure to
adhere to the Code may result in remedial action and/or termination in
accordance with contractual provisions.31
1.25 The Notification of the Significant Events (NOSE) clauses, introduced in May
2023, require service providers to notify the entity managing the contract
immediately, upon becoming aware of a 'significant event'.32 A 'significant
event' includes adverse comments or findings by a court, commission, tribunal
or other relevant body regarding conduct or performance of the supplier, its
employees or related entities that could be reasonably perceived to impact on
their professional capacity, capability, fitness or reputation; or other significant
matters, including commencement of legal or disciplinary action.33
1.26 Where the NOSE clauses are incorporated into the standing offer head
agreement or deed, the entity managing the standing offer and entities that may
have contracts with the supplier under the standing offer are subject to the
notification requirement.34 Following notification, the Commonwealth can
request a remediation plan from the supplier and, where the contract manager

1.27 The Contractor Reporting, Integrity Information Solution (CRIIS) was

1.28 In September 2025, Finance introduced guidance titled 'Managing conflicts of

1.29 Under the Modern Slavery Act 2018, large businesses and other entities (including

1.30 The Australian Government must submit an annual Commonwealth Modern
Slavery Statement detailing its actions to identify, assess and address modern
slavery risks in its operations and supply chains.41

Debarment regimes in other jurisdictions
1.32 Some jurisdictions currently have debarment processes in place or under
development, as noted in the Explanatory Memorandum and submissions to
this inquiry, which this section discusses in turn.
1.33 Evidence noted that Western Australia, New South Wales and Queensland have
noteworthy approaches to managing integrity in procurement. Similarly, some
international examples were briefly canvassed, namely Canada, the United
States of America and the United Kingdom.
Western Australia
1.34 Western Australia's debarment law is established under the Procurement Act
2020, which sets out the broad parameters of the regime, and the Procurement
(Debarment of Suppliers) Regulations 2021, which set the processes for the
regime.43 It was instigated following a 2018-19 Corruption and Crime
Commission investigation into the North Metropolitan Health Service.44
1.35 Ms Kate Ingham, the Western Australian Assistant Under Treasurer, described
its operation to the committee:
The debarment regime commenced on 1 January 2022 and establishes
processes and governance that allow government to work with suppliers to
improve business practices. In the worst case of wrongdoing, it provides
grounds for suppliers to be excluded from contracting with government.
Some examples are fraud and bribery. Conduct for which a supplier may be
debarred is categorised as either category A, category B or other debarment
conduct. Category A offences describe the most serious conduct.
The Department of Treasury and Finance's Under Treasurer may carry out
an investigation into a supplier and consider whether or not they should be
debarred if she reasonably suspects that the supplier or a senior officer of
that supplier has engaged in debarment conduct. Following an
investigation, the Under Treasurer may debar a supplier from supplying

Queensland
1.40 Queensland currently has a Queensland Government Supplier Code of
Conduct, which 'uses an incentive-based approach to foster accountability and
trust, and strengthen partnerships between suppliers and the government'. It
comprises the following three components:
 an incentive scheme, commencing on 1 January 2027, to encourage and

 proactive and reactive capability building, to support suppliers to

the Queensland Government to take decisive action such as applying
sanctions for egregious behaviour, only when necessary.49

and individuals inviting them to make a submission by 12 December 2025. The
committee published 13 submissions, which are listed at Appendix 1 and
available in full on the committee's website.
1.51 The committee held a public hearing in Canberra on 20 February 2026. The
witness list can be found at Appendix 2, and the Hansard transcript is available
on the committee's website.
1.52 The committee also wrote to a number of witnesses, asking them to provide
further evidence on the bill, in answer to questions on notice.
1.53 Further details of the inquiry, including links to the bill and the Explanatory
Memorandum, and answers to questions on notice, are also available on the
committee's website.
Structure of the report
1.54 The report comprises two chapters:
 This chapter provides background information relating to the bill, identifies
its key provisions, and notes consideration of the bill by other parliamentary
committees. It also looks at administration of the inquiry.
 Chapter 2 considers evidence provided to the committee, including support
for the bill, and matters raised in submissions and evidence at the hearing. It
also sets out the committee's views and recommendation.

Chapter 2
Matters raised in evidence and committee view
2.1 This chapter considers matters raised in evidence provided to the committee in
submissions and at the public hearing.
2.2 It first outlines the general support for the intent of the bill to establish a
debarment regime.
2.3 This chapter then considers concerns raised in evidence, including on a lack of
clarity in the bill's drafting, such as the definition of 'unethical conduct';
implementation and operation of the bill; potential duplication with existing
measures and frameworks; and the need for adequate guidance material for
stakeholders.
Support for the bill and its intentions
2.4 A number of stakeholders expressed support for the intention of the bill to
introduce a debarment regime, to increase the integrity of suppliers and as a
deterrent for unethical behaviour in government contracting.1 Others supported
the intentions of the bill, but considered the proposed regime was either not fit-
for-purpose, or that it could be amended to be more effective.
2.5 In outlining its support, the Australia Institute commented:
Major scandals, such as PwC's use of confidential tax information to solicit
work, have severely undermined public trust in government.
However, at present, the Commonwealth has no 'clear power' to ban a
consulting firm that monetised confidential Treasury information, which
meant that PwC was not banned from bidding for government contracts in
the wake of the tax leaks scandal.
This Bill fills that gap in the Commonwealth's powers and ensures that PwC,
alongside other firms that engage in unethical conduct in the future, can be
banned from receiving government contracts.2
2.6 The Tax Justice Network Australia (TJN-Aus) observed that the Commonwealth
'lags behind other governments in its failure to have a proper debarment system
for unethical contractors'.3 It contended that debarment regimes can drive more

'particularly broad, vague and subjective' and so recommended:

an unsuitable definition for applying a debarment regime and should not be
adopted.16
2.20 TJN-Aus urged the committee to pass the bill, but suggested that the 'definition
of "unethical conduct" needs amendment as the current drafting is too broad'.17

Proven record of tax avoidance
2.33 The committee received evidence commenting on the inclusion of tax avoidance

2.34 For example, the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) stated that 'tax avoidance' is

2.35 Similarly, the IMC submitted:
A 'proven record of tax avoidance' is not a breach of taxation laws, nor is it
necessarily a breach of ethical conduct. Tax minimisation and tax avoidance
are lawful practices that minimise tax payable by applying the tax laws to
the taxpayer's benefit. Tax evasion, which may involve underreporting
income, falsifying financial records, or failing to file tax returns, is illegal and
potentially criminal.32

Conduct of related entities and personnel
2.42 Some submitters and witnesses raised concerns about provisions of the bill
relating to the conduct of related entities and personnel. This included concerns
about how a debarment regime would interpret these terms in its decision-
making processes.
2.43 The bill does not provide a definition of a 'related entity'. However, the
proposed regime requires the unethical conduct engaged in by related entities
and the personnel of potential suppliers or tenderers be considered during any
procurement process, and also provides for the discretionary exclusion of these
suppliers and tenderers.38
2.44 Finance raised concerns that the bill requires the conduct of related entities to be
taken into consideration when applying the discretionary but not mandatory
exclusion period which, combined with lack of definition of 'related entity', is
likely to create confusion in the bill's operation.39

suppliers or tenderers where offending is not attributable to them.41
2.46 ACES Group recommended amending the 'responsible person provisions' of the

Natural justice and review processes
2.53 Further concerns were raised in relation to natural justice and review processes
under the bill's proposed debarment scheme. Finance submitted that:
Section 105BA of the Bill requires that the Finance Minister establish a
review process for decisions to place a supplier on the exclusion list. Finance
is concerned that the review process, as set out in this Bill is too broad in its
application. This lack of detail, combined with the broad definition of
unethical conduct, the lack of a power to compel information and the
ambiguity of who the decision-making body is, creates significant
implementation challenges.49
2.54 The IMC told the committee that if the bill's concepts of unethical conduct
remain, they would require: clear materiality thresholds; defined evidentiary
standards; decision criteria that are consistent across government, procedural
fairness safeguards, including a genuine right of reply, reasons for a decision
and a review pathway; and a fast and safe correction mechanism for any errors
on a public register.50
2.55 ASIAL also commented that before exclusion under the debarment regime,
suppliers should be provided with a right to respond, an opportunity to
demonstrate remediation and access to a review process. It stated that this
would ensure that temporary or minor breaches do not permanently exclude a
supplier.51
2.56 The IMC contended that the distinction between the 'explicit three-year limit'
for unethical conduct relating to convictions and penalties and the other 'highly
subjective' aspects of unethical conduct raises natural justice issues; uncertainty
about evidentiary standards; and potential for inconsistent application.52

2.65 The Digital Transformation Agency (DTA) raised concerns about 'significant
operational risks and unintended risks for the Australian Government' of
'outright bans' on suppliers. DTA commented:
In the digital market, where unique or highly specialised services are often
supplied by a limited number of providers, a permanent exclusion of a
supplier may jeopardise continuity of essential services and escalate costs
and risks. Furthermore, the dynamic nature of the technology sector—with
frequent mergers, acquisitions and changes in subsidiary and reseller
relationships—means that a blanket ban may inadvertently restrict access to
critical or innovative capabilities that subsequently become available
through previously debarred organisations.62
2.66 Citing a recent example where the Queensland Government had banned IBM
from government contracts following failure of the Queensland Health payroll
system, DTA suggested that:
While this action addressed immediate concerns about accountability, it also
reduced competition in the market for large-scale IT services and
contributed to a contraction of the local workforce. Even after the issues
were resolved and the ban was eventually lifted, the effects on competition
and capability persisted, highlighting the enduring impact of such measures
on government procurement and the broader technology sector.63
2.67 At the hearing, officials from Finance told the committee that 'the risks of getting
debarment regimes wrong are quite severe', as:
… if there are accusations made public too early, there is reputational

Commonwealth for reputational damage to those suppliers. So getting that

Committee view
2.88 The committee acknowledges the concerns raised in evidence to this inquiry that
unethical behaviour in government contracting and recent scandals, such as the
PwC matter, have the potential to undermine trust and reduce the capacity of
the public service.
2.89 Similar matters were outlined in the final report of the Consulting Inquiry
undertaken by the References Committee in the 47th Parliament, and remain of
concern.90
2.90 However, it is clear from the evidence received in this inquiry, that there are
substantial concerns with the bill as drafted and its effectiveness in achieving its
stated objectives.
2.91 Even where there was support for a Commonwealth debarment regime,
stakeholders raised concerns with the scope of the proposed regime and the
definitions contained in the bill, and suggested that substantial amendments
would be required for it to be effective.
2.92 In particular, the committee notes concerns that the breadth of the definition of
'unethical conduct' may result in confusion and a lack of clarity, as well as
concerns as to the operation and implementation of the bill.
2.93 The committee also received a significant amount of evidence detailing existing
measures in the Commonwealth procurement framework to support ethical
conduct in government contracting.
2.94 This includes measures such as the strengthened CPRs, and the Code and NOSE
clauses that were introduced subsequent to the PwC matter. The committee
notes the evidence from Finance that a significant amount of work has been
done in this area since the PwC matter, and that it now believes the
Commonwealth has appropriate guardrails to prevent another case like the
PwC scandal from arising.
2.95 The committee also notes that some submitters and witnesses argued in favour
of the Commonwealth considering adopting a scheme based on features of
debarment processes in other jurisdictions or proposed by the ALRC. However,
these propositions differ significantly from the bill's proposed framework that
this inquiry has examined.

2.96 On the proposed debarment regime put forward by the bill, the committee also
considered comments regarding the possible reputational impacts for potential
suppliers that faced allegations of unethical conduct later proved to be untrue,
as well as impacts on competition and innovation.
2.97 The committee notes Finance's comments that the current approach has a focus
on remediation, rather than punitive measures.
2.98 Further, the committee considers that the bill's debarment regime could overlap
and conflict with existing measures, which could create confusion and
potentially act as a deterrent for suppliers coming forward early to advise of
relevant issues they are facing.
2.99 Accordingly, while the committee considers that it is essential to uphold ethical
conduct in government contracting, the evidence received in this inquiry
outlines that there has been a substantial amount of work in this area since the
PwC matter. The committee therefore considers the existing, and enhanced
mechanisms, in the Commonwealth Procurement Framework are adequate to
achieve these aims.
