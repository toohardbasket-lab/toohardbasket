Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 On 25 November 2025, the Higher Education Support Amendment (Reverse
Job-Ready Graduates Fee Hikes and End 50k Arts Degrees) Bill 2025 (the bill)
was introduced into the Senate by Senator Mehreen Faruqi.1
1.2 On 5 February 2026, pursuant to the recommendations of Report No. 1 of 2026
of the Senate Selection of Bills Committee, the Senate referred the bill to the
Education and Employment Legislation (the committee) for inquiry and report
by 25 June 2026.2
Purpose of the bill
1.3 The bill seeks to amend the Higher Education Support Act 2003 to reduce student
contribution amounts for certain disciplines affected by the Job-Ready
Graduates (JRG) reforms, including law, accounting, administration, economics,
commerce, society and culture, and communications.3
1.4 In her second reading speech, Senator Faruqi said that 'under the Job-Ready
Graduates scheme, fees for humanities, arts, law and social science degrees
exploded, with students now paying over $50,000 for an arts degree'. Senator
Faruqi said the bill would reverse these 'punitive' fee hikes, which had 'left
hundreds of thousands of students saddled with higher and higher debts for
simply wanting to study what they love'.4
1.5 Further, the Explanatory Memorandum (EM) stated that the bill 'follows urgent
calls from students, academics and the higher education sector across Australia
to end the damaging legacy of the job-ready graduates'.5
Context of the bill
1.6 The JRG package was introduced by the former Coalition Government in 2020
and commenced on 1 January 2021. The reforms were intended to 'better align
Commonwealth Grant Scheme (CGS) funding to the cost of delivering higher

1.7 Under the CGS, the cost of a Commonwealth supported place (CSP) is shared
between the Australian Government and the student.7 The Australian
Government contributes funding to higher education providers through
Commonwealth contribution amounts, while students pay student contribution
amounts, which are commonly deferred through the Higher Education Loan
Program (HELP) using HECS-HELP loans.8 Together, Commonwealth and
student contributions make up the total funding received by a higher education
provider for a CSP.9
1.8 Among other things, JRG made substantial changes to both Commonwealth
contribution amounts and maximum student contribution amounts across
different fields of education. Student contribution amounts were increased for
some disciplines, including humanities, law, society and culture, and
communications, and reduced for others, including teaching, nursing,
agriculture and certain Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics
(STEM) disciplines. Some of the changes are set out below in Table 1.1.10

1.9 The operation of JRG was subsequently examined as part of the Australian
Universities Accord (the Accord), which reviewed Australia's higher education
system. In 2024, the final report found:
The JRG package needs to be replaced. Its purpose of providing price signals
to influence student subject choices has failed. Only 1.5% of students applied
to enrol in courses they would not have applied for under the pre JRG
student contribution arrangements. It has left some students facing
extremely high student contributions and large HELP debts that do not
reflect their future earning potential, and tilted the overall cost burden of
higher education further on to students and away from the Australian
Government.12
1.10 The Accord recommended a range of measures to address the impacts of JRG,
including 'urgent remediation' of the JRG package by:
 reducing student contribution rates in the highest charging fields
(humanities, human movement, society and culture, and communications)
(Recommendation 16a);
 progressively moving towards a student contribution system aligned with
lifetime earnings, with humanities courses moved into the middle of three
bands (Recommendations 16a and 40); and
 increasing government funding to support science, technology, engineering
and mathematics courses (STEM), to reduce the negative impacts of the JRG
package (Recommendation 41d).13
1.11 On 15 May 2024, the Australian Government announced that it would
implement a number of the Accord's recommendations, including development
of a new Managed Growth Funding System for Commonwealth supported
places.14

education pricing and funding arrangements would occur through the

Key provisions of the bill
1.13 The bill consists of one schedule with two items which would amend the Higher
Education Support Act 2003 and would commence the day after Royal Assent.17
1.14 Item 1 would repeal the existing table in section 93-10 of the Higher Education
Support Act 2003 and replace it with a new table setting out revised maximum
student contribution amounts for units of study in law, accounting,
administration, economics, commerce, society and culture, and
communications.18
1.15 The new table has been calculated using the 2026 indexed rates guide published
by the Department of Education, consistent with the indexation methodology
set out in Division 198 of the Higher Education Support Act 2003.19
1.16 Item 2 provides that the amendments made by Part 1 of Schedule 1 apply to a
unit of study with a census date that falls on or after the commencement of the
bill. This application applies regardless of whether the unit of study forms part
of a course of study that commenced before, on, or after that commencement.20
1.17 The bill does not amend Commonwealth contribution amounts payable to
higher education providers under the CGS. Section 53 of the Constitution states
'proposed laws appropriating revenue or moneys, or imposing taxation, shall
not originate in the Senate'.21

Chapter 2
Key Issues
2.1 This chapter discusses the evidence received by the committee relating to the
Higher Education Support Amendment (Reverse Job-Ready Graduates Fee
Hikes and End 50k Arts Degrees) Bill 2025 (the bill).
General views on the bill
2.2 There was broad support among academics, student representative bodies and
humanities organisations for the bill's intent to reduce student contribution
amounts for disciplines that were increased following the introduction of the
Job-Ready Graduates (JRG) framework.1
2.3 For example, Professor Michelle Arrow, President of the Australian Historical
Association, stated that the bill was a 'crucial first step towards sustainable
reform', explaining that students who had studied under JRG over the past five
years were accumulating 'immense' levels of debt that were 'likely, in many
cases, never to be repaid'.2
2.4 Similarly, Mr Felix Hughes, President of the National Union of Students,
described the bill as 'a starting point for a broader reset' to ensure 'that access to
university is not determined by cost'.3
2.5 However, many universities and peak bodies did not support the bill as drafted.
While supporting the intent of the bill, these submitters argued the bill would
reduce student contribution amounts without restoring the associated
reductions in Commonwealth contributions introduced under JRG. This would
result in an overall reduction of university funding and place additional
financial pressure on the higher education sector.4

Effectiveness and impact of the JRG framework
2.9 As outlined above, while views differed about the bill, witnesses and submitters
consistently raised concerns regarding the impacts of the current funding
settings on students, universities, and the higher education sector.
2.10 For example, Mr Luke Sheehy from Universities Australia told the committee
that JRG is 'not working':
It's a barrier to opportunity. It's pushed costs onto students, driving up debt.
It's failed in its core objective by not meaningfully changing student
behaviour. It's pulled around $4 billion out of the system since 2021, and it's
weakening the system. It needs to be fixed, and it needs to be fixed now.7
2.11 Universities Australia also argued that reform of the current funding settings is
necessary to support Australia’s long-term skills and attainment objectives,
including the government’s target of 80 per cent tertiary attainment by 2050.8

Failure to achieve policy objectives
2.14 As noted in Chapter One, JRG was introduced to encourage enrolment in
courses aligned with perceived market need. It was also expected to better align
university funding with the cost of delivery, and support growth in
Commonwealth Supported Places (CSPs).11 Evidence to the inquiry
overwhelmingly suggested that JRG had failed to achieve these objectives,
discussed below.

Growth in Commonwealth supported places and funding alignment
2.22 Evidence questioned whether JRG had achieved increased the number of CSPs
and better aligned university funding with the cost of delivering courses.
2.23 The University of Melbourne submitted that the policy assertion that JRG would
progressively increase the number of student places had 'proven unfounded'. It
estimated that JRG 'cut total revenue for domestic students by over 5 per cent',
university funding 'by around $750–800 million per year' and concluded that
'the Government did not provide sufficient subsidy to deliver these promised
additional places'.21
2.24 The committee heard that the methodology used to set JRG funding rates may
have contributed to the overall reduction. Funding settings under JRG were
based on a Deloitte review which assessed the cost of delivering university
education.22
2.25 Professor Andrew Norton explained that the review considered 'teaching and
scholarship costs only' and excluded broader research activity. He noted that
funding for science and engineering disciplines was subject to 'pretty heavy cuts'
under JRG. As a result, while lower student fees created 'slight incentives' to
enrol, universities faced 'reduced incentives on the supply side … to actually
increase supply'.23
2.26 Professor Alex Zelinsky, Vice-Chancellor and President of the University of
Newcastle, similarly argued that JRG 'tried to create more places without more
funding'. He stated that the Deloitte review underestimated the true cost of
delivering some disciplines, particularly STEM courses, explaining that in these
areas it 'completely underestimated the true cost of delivery'. 24

2.29 As outlined in Chapter One, JRG changed the level of Commonwealth funding
provided for different fields of education. Under the current funding
framework, Commonwealth support is allocated across eight 'funding clusters'.
Cluster 1 disciplines include law, accounting, administration, economics,
commerce, communications, and society and culture.27
2.30 Evidence to the committee suggested that one of the most significant
consequences of JRG has been a substantial increase in the debt burden carried
by students enrolled in Cluster 1 disciplines.28
2.31 Universities Australia submitted that fees in some Cluster 1 disciplines had
'more than double[d], rising by up to 117 per cent and pushing total course costs
beyond $50,000', describing this as 'one of the largest increases in student costs
in decades'.29

Coalition Senators' additional comments
Introduction
1.1 Coalition Senators thank all those who contributed to this inquiry, and
acknowledge the effort made by students, academics, universities, peak bodies
and members of the public to assist the committee's deliberations.
1.2 Coalition Senators support a higher education system that equips Australians
with the knowledge and skills a changing economy demands, sustains research
of international standing, and remains within reach of every student. The
strength of such a system rests not on any single fee or subsidy, but on the
coherence of its funding arrangements as a whole; settings that are fair to
students, sustainable for the institutions that rely on them, and defensible to the
taxpayers who fund them. Durable reform of those arrangements requires the
Job-Ready Graduates scheme (JRG or the scheme) to be assessed in full, and not
only in the parts that support a particular conclusion.
1.3 Coalition Senators support the committee's recommendation that the Senate not
pass the bill. They reach that conclusion, however, for reasons that differ from
those set out in the committee's report.
1.4 The bill cannot achieve what its supporters intend. It would result in significant
cuts to university funding due to reductions in student contributions. The
decision to introduce the bill in the Senate is fatal, because it guarantees that
these cuts cannot be offset by corresponding increases to Commonwealth
contributions. This is because bills that originate in the Senate are
constitutionally prohibited from appropriating money from the consolidated
revenue. On the evidence before the committee, the bill as presently drafted
would therefore result in a loss to the sector of around $1.3 billion each year.
Overview
1.5 Consistent with the Coalition’s longstanding policy position, Coalition Senators
consider that the student contribution settings established under the Job-Ready
Graduates scheme would benefit from review across the board. That view
informs the observations that follow.
1.6 The observations of Coalition Senators relate to five matters:
(a) The committee's report considers the disciplines in which student
contributions increased, and does not consider in the same way those in
which contributions were reduced;
(b) The evidence on whether student contributions influence student choices is
not readily reconciled with the conclusions drawn from it;
(c) The bill, on its own terms, would create a fee structure that has never
previously existed;

task. On this, Coalition Senators and the committee are agreed.
