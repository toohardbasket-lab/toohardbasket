3.14 The Committee recommends that, following passage of the Bill and before the
next sunsetting of the provisions in 2027, the Government consider reviewing

considering whether any additional exceptions should be prescribed by

Recommendation 2
3.21 The Committee recommends that, following passage of the Bill and before the
next sunsetting of the provisions in 2027, the Government consider an
additional exception to the offence of entering, or remaining in, an area that

would apply where:
• the person is already travelling to, or is already in, the area on the day on
which it becomes a declared area, and

period of one month beginning with that day.

1. Introduction
The Bill and its referral
1.1 The Counter-Terrorism Legislation Amendment (Declared Areas) Bill 2024 (the Bill)
was introduced into the House of Representatives by the Attorney-General, the Hon
Mark Dreyfus KC MP, on 27 March 2024. On 3 April 2024, the Bill was referred to
this Committee for review.
1.2 The ‘declared areas’ offence in section 119.2 of Schedule 1 of the Criminal Code Act
1995 (Criminal Code) is presently due to cease (‘sunset’) on 7 September 2024. The
Bill would ensure the continuation of the offence for a further three years, until
7 September 2027. In addition, the Bill provides that section 119.3—the provision
under which the Minister for Foreign Affairs can declare an area for the purpose of
the offence in section 119.2—ceases to have effect on 7 September 2027. Currently,
section 119.3 is not subject to a sunsetting provision.
1.3 In his second reading speech, the Attorney-General said:
The threat of terrorism is enduring and we must remain resolute in our response.
To this end, this bill ensures the continuation of a key component of our
counterterrorism legal framework: the declared areas provisions. …
Although there are currently no areas declared, these provisions remain a
necessary component of our framework in the current threat environment. The
offence plays a role in the disruption and prosecution of returning foreign fighters
and their associates. The limited number of areas that have been declared, and
the limited use of the offence to date, demonstrate its exceptional nature and
judicious use of this power.1
1.4 The Bill would also amend the Intelligence Services Act 2001 (IS Act) to remove
paragraph 29(1)(bbaa), which provides for the Committee to review the ‘operation,
effectiveness and proportionality’ of sections 119.2 and 119.3 (declared areas
provisions) before 7 January 2024. The Committee exercised its discretion not to do
so before 7 January 2024, making this paragraph redundant.
1.5 The Bill does not propose a replacement paragraph in the IS Act to re-vest the
Committee with that specific review power. The justification for this is that the
Intelligence Services Legislation Amendment Bill 2023 (ISLAB), which is currently
before the Parliament, proposes to empower the Committee to conduct own motion

Section 119.2: Entering, or remaining in, declared areas
1.12 A person commits the offence in section 119.2(1) of the Criminal Code, subject to the
exceptions in subsections 119.2(3) and (4), if each of the following physical elements
is satisfied:
a. The person enters, or remains in, an area in a foreign country;
b. The area is an area declared by the Minister for Foreign Affairs under
section 119.3; and
c. When the person enters the area, or at any time when the person is in the
area, one of the following applies—the person:
i. is an Australian citizen; or
ii. is a resident of Australia; or
iii. is a holder under the Migration Act 1958 of a visa; or
iv. has voluntarily put himself or herself under the protection of
Australia.6
1.13 In addition to demonstrating the above physical elements, a fault element must also
be established for each physical element, unless stated otherwise.7
1.14 No fault element is specified for the first two physical elements—entering, or
remaining in, an area in a foreign country (paragraph 119.2(1)(a)) and that the area is
a ‘declared area’ (paragraph 119.2(1)(b))—and therefore the default fault elements
apply for both.8 The fault elements are intention and recklessness, respectively.
1.15 For the third physical element—being an Australian citizen, resident, visa holder or
under Australia’s protection while present in the declared area—absolute liability
applies.9 This means there is no fault element that needs to be proven and the
mistake of fact general defence under section 9.2 of the Criminal Code is not
available (i.e. a person cannot claim they did not know that they had such ties to
Australia when committing the offence).

2. Consideration of the Bill
2.1 As outlined in Chapter 1, the effect of the Counter-Terrorism Legislation Amendment
(Declared Areas) Bill 2024 (the Bill) would be to:
• extend for a further three years the offence contained within section 119.2 of
Schedule 1 of the Criminal Code Act 1995 (Criminal Code), to 7 September 2027;
• insert a sunsetting provision on the power for the Minister for Foreign Affairs to
declare an area for the purpose of the offence in section 119.2 of the Criminal
Code, which would also cease to have effect on 7 September 2027; and
• amend the Intelligence Services Act 2001 (IS Act) to repeal the provision for
review by this Committee in paragraph 29(1)(bbaa).
2.2 The Bill does not propose to amend the scope or operation of the declared areas
provisions in any respect but it would continue their operation for a further three
years. As such, much of the evidence provided to the Committee focused on the
overall desirability of the declared areas provisions, including whether they should be
allowed to sunset or to be renewed. Some evidence offered views on how, if
continued, the provisions should be amended.
2.3 The other substantive change proposed in the Bill is the removal of the explicit
authority for the Committee to conduct a statutory review and report to Parliament on
the operation, effectiveness and proportionality of the declared areas provisions, prior
to their future sunsetting dates.
2.4 This chapter examines the evidence received in relation to each of the proposed
amendments contained in the Bill.
Section 119.2: the offence of entering, or remaining
in, declared areas
2.5 Currently, the declared areas offence in section 119.2 of the Criminal Code is due to
cease operation on 7 September 2024.1 Schedule 1, Item 1 of the Bill would amend
the sunset date contained in subsection 119.2(6) of the Criminal Code from
7 September 2024 to 7 September 2027. The effect of this would be to continue the
operation of the declared areas offence for another three years.
2.6 As detailed in Chapter 1, subsection 119.2(1) sets out the offence of entering, or
remaining in, declared areas, including the elements that must be proved to be found

Necessity of declared areas provisions
2.7 The Attorney-General’s Department, the Australian Federal Police (AFP), the
Australian Security Intelligence Organisation (ASIO) and the Department of Foreign
Affairs and Trade (DFAT) all submitted that the declared areas provisions remain a
necessary and proportionate part of Australia’s counter-terrorism laws. The Attorney-
General’s Department stated in its submission to this inquiry:
The declared areas provisions in the Criminal Code are an important part of the
Australian Government’s efforts to stop the flow of foreign fighters, and mitigate
the risk they pose to Australia on their return.2
2.8 The AFP submitted that there is still a need for the declared areas provisions and
that they ‘remain appropriate and proportionate in the current terrorism threat
environment’.3
The AFP continues to be concerned that individuals who travel, without any
legitimate purpose, to areas where terrorist organisations are engaging in hostile
activities, present a threat to Australia and Australians (both domestically and
overseas). In particular, there is a risk that such individuals may develop
enhanced capabilities, connections to terrorist organisations, and/or an ideology
and motivation to cause harm to Australians. The provisions at sections 119.2
and 119.3 of the Criminal Code Act 1995 (the declared area provisions) create a
mechanism that can be used to deter Australians from travelling to such areas.4
2.9 The AFP submitted that despite there being no areas declared at present, ‘the
existence of the declared area provisions ensures the Government is able to pivot
quickly to address emerging risks’.5 Similarly, ASIO submitted that the security
environment remains ‘complex, challenging and changing’:
The National Terrorism Threat Level is POSSIBLE and terrorism remains a real
and pervasive threat. In this regard, ASIO acknowledges that the declared areas
offence is a necessary and proportionate tool to manage the terrorist risk to
Australia in the current threat environment.6

Criminalising ‘mere presence’ in a place

declared areas provisions appear—establishes several other foreign incursion

Proportionality of the offence and the changed security context

necessary to advance a legitimate objective, and are reasonable, necessary, and
proportionate to achieving that legitimate objective.32 The legitimate objective of the
declared areas offence was described in the Bill’s Statement of Compatibility with

protecting Australia’s national security interests, deterring Australians from

and guardians from taking them to declared areas.33

restrictions on human rights are no longer proportionate to the pursuit of that
objective, owing to the changed security environment.

3. Committee comment
3.1 In its last review of sections 119.2 and 119.3 of the Criminal Code (the declared
areas provisions) in 2021, the Committee recommended the provisions be extended
for a further three-year period, to sunset on 7 September 2024.
3.2 The Committee considered at the time that it would be imprudent to repeal the
provisions during a period of great uncertainty but recommended that the Committee
be given the opportunity to review the provisions again before 7 January 2024. It was
considered that such a review would allow for the reassessment of the operation,
effectiveness and proportionality of the declared areas provisions after the
geopolitical, economic and political outcomes of COVID-19 had become apparent.1
3.3 The scheduled date for review has come and gone and, while COVID-19 may no
longer be dominating the public consciousness as it was three years ago, uncertainty
still abounds. Since 2021 international conflicts have broken out and the security
landscape has grown increasingly complex. But uncertainty is not the metric by which
the Committee should evaluate these provisions, instead it should consider whether
the law is still fulfilling its intended purpose effectively and proportionately.
3.4 However, the Director-General of Security has noted that terrorism remains an
enduring ‘real and pervasive’ threat to Australia and that the declared areas offence
is a necessary and proportionate tool to manage the terrorist risk to Australia in the
current environment.
3.5 The Committee’s view is that the declared areas provisions continue to serve an
important purpose within the broader framework of Australia’s robust counter-
terrorism laws. As evidence from the Australian Security Intelligence Organisation
and the Attorney-General’s Department stressed, the declared areas provisions do
not operate in a vacuum but as part of a much larger legislative scheme to achieve
Australia’s counter-terrorism objectives. This includes deterring, disrupting and
supporting successful investigations and prosecutions of individuals who pose a risk
to Australia, including foreign fighters.
3.6 While the Committee notes that no areas have been declared since the revocation of
the declaration of Mosul on 19 December 2019, criminal proceedings against
individuals charged with the offence of entering, or remaining in, a declared area
have progressed.
3.7 Further, the Committee acknowledges ASIO and AFP comments emphasising that
the global counter-terrorism environment is complex and changing. The Committee
1 Parliamentary Joint Committee on Intelligence and Security, Review of ‘Declared Areas’ Provisions: Sections
119.2 and 119.3 of the Criminal Code, Report, February 2021, p. 18.
27
observes that this is evident in recent events: since the October 7 Hamas-led attacks
on Israel, there has been significant deterioration in the security environment in Gaza
and southern Lebanon.
3.8 In light of the recent escalation in operations by the listed terrorist organisation
Hezbollah in the southern area of Lebanon, the Committee believes there would be
merit in the Government proactively seeking advice from relevant security agencies
regarding a declaration under section 119.3 of the Criminal Code of Hezbollah-
controlled areas of Lebanon south of the Litani River, consistent with United Nations
Security Council Resolution 1701.
3.9 Based on the evidence received during this inquiry, the Committee’s view is that the
declared areas provisions in the Criminal Code continue to serve an important
purpose.
3.10 There are already robust safeguards and limitations in place under the legislation.
However, the Committee considers that the necessity and scope of these safeguards
and limitations should be regularly reviewed and considered, to reflect the current
threat environment and in the context of Australia’s counter-terrorism legislative
framework.
3.11 The Committee, as well as the former Independent National Security Legislation
Monitor (INSLM) Dr James Renwick SC, had previously recommended amending the
Criminal Code to allow Australian citizens to request an exemption from the Minister
for Foreign Affairs in advance of travel to a declared area for reasons not listed in
section 119.2(3), but which would not otherwise be illegitimate under Australian law.
3.12 While the Committee stands by the principle underpinning this recommendation, the
Committee respects the objections raised in the Government’s 2021 rejection of this
recommendation and the evidence to this inquiry reiterating those objections.
3.13 The Committee notes that there is a regulation-making power in paragraph
119.2(3)(h) of the Criminal Code which enables additional purposes of travel to be
prescribed. The regulation-making power is still a valuable safeguard and, while it is
yet to be used, could be used as a vehicle to prescribe additional legitimate purpose
exemptions.
Recommendation 1
3.14 The Committee recommends that, following passage of the Bill and before the
next sunsetting of the provisions in 2027, the Government consider reviewing
the list of ‘legitimate purpose’ exceptions for entering or remaining in a
declared area in subsection 119.2(3) of the Criminal Code. This includes
considering whether any additional exceptions should be prescribed by
regulations.
3.15 The evidence also drew attention to the prominent roles ministerial and prosecutorial
discretion play in the operation of the declared areas provisions. The Committee
28
agrees that the discretion to declare an area and to proceed with prosecutions has
been exercised sensibly and judiciously; and the Committee has no expectation that
this approach would change. However, it is important to ensure that the current
safeguards and limitations on such discretionary powers are appropriate.
3.16 The declared areas provisions operate so that, as soon as an area is declared, it
becomes an offence to enter or remain in that area for any reason other than one of
the prescribed legitimate purposes in subsection 119.2(3). The punishment for
having been in that area for any other reason while it was declared is up to 10 years’
imprisonment. The fault element is that the person intended to enter or remain in the
area and that they were reckless as to whether it was subject to a declaration under
section 119.3 of the Criminal Code.
3.17 It is ultimately a matter for the Attorney-General’s discretion whether to consent to
the prosecution of such an individual.
3.18 Moreover, throughout this inquiry, it became apparent that, while information may be
provided to those in Australia at the time of a declaration such as via the distribution
of brochures to community groups and at airports, no specific mechanism currently
exists for notifying a person travelling in a country where an area is declared.
Persons already offshore must proactively check the Smartraveller.gov.au website;
and it is no longer possible for a person to register their overseas travel to receive
travel advice. In light of the extreme circumstances that would have likely precipitated
the declaration of an area, it cannot be assumed that a person in or near that area
would have regular access to reliable internet or methods of communication to learn
of such a declaration immediately.
3.19 As such, the Committee considers that the Government should review the
exemptions and consider whether a grace period needs to be explicitly provided for,
rather than relying solely on ministerial or prosecutorial discretion to not charge an
individual who found themselves in a declared area in the immediate aftermath of its
declaration. The Committee also notes the general defences in Part 2.3 of the
Criminal Code, including the defence of intervening conduct or event,2 or a sudden or
extraordinary emergency,3 available to an accused person as detailed in paragraph
1.21 herein.
3.20 The United Kingdom’s ‘designated area’ regime detailed in the Terrorism Act 2000
(UK) provides individuals with a grace period of one month provided that the person
was already travelling to, or already in, the area on the day on which it became
designated, and the person left the area before the end of the period of one month
beginning with the day of the designation.
2 Criminal Code Act 1995, Sch 1 (Criminal Code), s. 10.1.
3 Criminal Code, s. 19.3.

Recommendation 2
3.21 The Committee recommends that, following passage of the Bill and before the
next sunsetting of the provisions in 2027, the Government consider an
additional exception to the offence of entering, or remaining in, an area that
has been declared under section 119.3 of the Criminal Code. The exception
would apply where:
• the person is already travelling to, or is already in, the area on the day on
which it becomes a declared area, and
• the person leaves the area as soon as practicable, but no later than the
period of one month beginning with that day.
3.22 Section 119.3(1) empowers the Minister for Foreign Affairs to declare an area in a
foreign country provided the Minister is satisfied that a listed terrorist organisation is
engaging in a hostile activity in that area of the foreign country. As above, the
Committee notes that this discretion has been used appropriately and sparingly since
the law came into effect—however, that does not negate the case for ensuring there
are strong safeguards.
3.23 During the public hearing it was repeatedly stated that the law has only been used in

organisation.

talking … about an ungoverned space where a listed terrorist organisation is
undertaking activities … we are talking about extreme situations.’6
• The Explanatory Memorandum to the Bill stated the objective of the provisions is
in part to deter Australians from travelling to ‘dangerous conflict areas’ where
listed terrorist organisations are engaging in hostile activity.7

3.25 The Committee agrees this represents an area of potential ambiguity. To give effect

Committee therefore considers that additional legislative safeguards on the Minister
for Foreign Affairs’ discretion to declare an area should be considered following the
passage of the Bill but before its proposed sunsetting date in 2027.
3.26 A review of the process for declaring an area would also include a review of the
Minister’s protocol listing the non-legislative factors to be taken into account.
Reforming the protocol could include giving greater prominence to the extent of
territorial control exercised by a listed terrorist organisation.
Recommendation 3
3.27 The Committee recommends that, following passage of the Bill and before the
next sunsetting of the provisions in 2027, the Government consider reviewing
the existing safeguards on the Minister for Foreign Affairs’ discretion to
declare an area under section 119.3 of the Criminal Code. This would include
reviewing the Minister’s protocol and the list of factors to be taken into
consideration by the Minister for Foreign Affairs before declaring an area.
3.28 The Committee notes the Law Council’s view that the declaration of an area should
be a matter for parliamentary, not Executive, decision. Noting that the Committee is
empowered to review a declaration when made or at any time it is in effect, and that
the legislative instrument declaring an area is subject to disallowance by either
House of Parliament, the Committee considers current mechanisms for parliamentary
scrutiny are sufficient. The Committee will undoubtedly give close consideration to
any future declarations made and considers it highly likely that it would elect to
review any declaration, as has been its practice to date.
3.29 The Committee is also aware of the concerns of some participants in the inquiry
regarding the accretion and continuation of terrorism-related powers and offences
that were initially introduced as temporary or exceptional measures in response to a
specific set of circumstances.
3.30 While extenuating circumstances in recent decades have precipitated the significant
expansion of offences and powers, these should not be seen as permanent. The
limitations these laws place on rights is justified only so long as the law remains
necessary and proportionate to achieve the legitimate aim of protecting Australians
and their way of life. The retention of these laws for retention’s sake risks
undermining the faith Australians have in lawmakers.
3.31 The regular and considered review of these laws is a key mechanism by which
Australians can be assured of their ongoing necessity and proportionality. The
Committee considers following passage of this Bill, it will be an appropriate time to
31
take stock of Australia’s national counter-terrorism laws and whether they remain fit
for purpose, necessary and proportionate in the current security landscape. It has
been a decade since the passage of the Counter-Terrorism Legislation Amendment
(Foreign Fighters) Act 2014 and the country is facing a much-changed security
landscape.
3.32 As noted above, the declared areas provisions do not operate in a vacuum. Indeed,
evidence from the Australian Security Intelligence Organisation and the Attorney-
General’s Department stressed that these provisions operate as part of a framework.
Therefore, in considering the future of the declared areas provisions, it is necessary
to consider the broader legislative architecture that contributes to Australia’s counter-
terrorism objectives. The Committee considers that the INSLM is best placed to
conduct such an inquiry and report before the powers are due to sunset on 7
September 2027.
3.33 Noting that the Committee has the ability to refer matters to the INSLM under section
7A of the Independent National Security Legislation Monitor Act 2010, the Committee
considers the following recommendation will provide a basis for further discussion
with the INSLM about the form and scope such a review might take.
Recommendation 4
3.34 The Committee recommends that the Independent National Security
Legislation Monitor review Division 119 of the Criminal Code, with particular
attention to the declared areas provisions and their ongoing necessity within
the broader counter-terrorism legislative framework. The INSLM should report
its findings in relation to the declared areas by 7 January 2027.
3.35 This Committee is also integral to oversight of the terrorism powers within the
Criminal Code, including the declared areas provisions, and takes its responsibility
seriously. The Committee welcomes the proposed creation of an own-motion review
power as part of the Intelligence Services Legislation Amendment Bill 2023 that is
currently before the Parliament. Noting that these laws will remain subject to a
sunsetting provision which requires legislative amendment of the Criminal Code
before the declared areas provisions can continue beyond 2027, the Committee
would expect to conduct an inquiry into any Bill proposing to extend sunsetting at that
time, as has been the case to date. Findings and recommendations made by the
INSLM pursuant to the above recommendation would usefully inform the
Committee’s review in that regard.
3.36 In conclusion, the Committee supports the continuation of the declared areas
provisions in sections 119.2 and 119.3 of the Criminal Code for a further three years,
on the condition that additional safeguards be considered to account for the changed
security landscape. While the Committee agrees that the provisions have an ongoing
role to play in Australia’s suite of counter-terrorism measures at this time, the
Committee would urge the Government to maintain careful consideration of whether
these laws remain necessary and appropriate, and be ready to provide a convincing
32
rationale for any request to prolong the operation of the provisions beyond
September 2027.
Recommendation 5
3.37 The Committee recommends that the Counter-Terrorism Legislation
Amendment (Declared Areas) Bill 2024 be passed by the Parliament.
