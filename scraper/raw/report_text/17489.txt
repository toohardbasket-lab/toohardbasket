1. Introduction
The Bill and its referral
1.1 The Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Amendment Bill 2026
(the Bill) was introduced to the Parliament on 12 March 2026 and referred to the
Committee for inquiry and report. The Committee commenced its review on 30 March
2026.
1.2 The Bill intends to establish a new framework in the Anti-Money Laundering and
Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006 (AML/CTF Act) for the Australian Transaction
Reports and Analysis Centre (AUSTRAC) Chief Executive Officer (CEO) to restrict or
prohibit, via legislative instrument, a reporting entity from using a product, service,
delivery channel or thing (the high-risk mechanism) to provide a designated service.
1.3 In his second reading speech the Assistant Minister for Immigration and Assistant
Minister for Foreign Affairs and Trade said:
Transnational, serious and organised crime actors continue to adapt and develop
new ways to exploit Australian community members and target our economic
systems to generate illicit profits and launder the proceeds of crime.
Money laundering is not a victimless crime. Each year, billions of dollars of illicit
funds are generated from criminal activity such as drug trafficking, cybercrime,
child exploitation, tax evasion, and other illegal and corrupt practices. It's also
used by authoritarian regimes to fuel corruption and undermine the rule of law
across the world.
AUSTRAC has observed that crypto ATMs are one of these new mechanisms,
linked to everything from money laundering to scams and fraud to illicit
substances to child exploitation. When AUSTRAC looked at the top 90 most
prolific users of these ATMs in 2024 it found that 85 per cent of these users were
either scam victims or money mules who had been tricked or coerced into
moving money.
While not everyone who uses these ATMs is linked to illicit activity, the
observations of our law enforcement agencies is that, unfortunately, a
substantive amount of their use contributes to criminal gain.
The Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Amendment Bill
2026 responds to crypto ATMs and the broader modern financial crime
1
environment which facilitates serious crimes and harms both Australia's financial
systems and the community.1
Conduct of the inquiry
1.4 The Committee announced its inquiry on 1 April 2026 and invited submissions
addressing the terms of reference by 8 May 2026.
1.5 The Committee received 19 submissions including 1 confidential submission.
Appendix A sets out a list of submissions received. The Committee held a public
hearing on 9 June 2026. Appendix B sets out a list of witnesses who appeared at
the public hearing.
1.6 Copies of the submissions and links to the Bill and Explanatory Memorandum can be
accessed at the Committee’s website.2
Report structure
1.7 This report consists of three chapters:
• Chapter 1 provides an introduction to the Bill and the inquiry, and an outline of
the legislative and parliamentary history of Australia’s AML/CTF framework.
• Chapter 2 sets out the proposed amendments in the Bill in more detail, examines
the evidence received in the inquiry and also considers other matters presented in
evidence that are not contained in the Bill.
• Chapter 3 provides the Committee’s comments and recommendations.
Bill structure
1.8 The Bill consists of 3 Schedules.
1.9 Schedule 1 to the Bill contains amendments related to regulating the use of high-risk
mechanisms. Under the new framework, the AUSTRAC CEO will be empowered to
restrict or prohibit, by legislative instrument, a reporting entity from using a high-risk
mechanism to provide a designated service.
1.10 Schedule 2 would amend the meaning of financing of terrorism in section 5 of the
AML/CTF Act to reference new offences introduced into the Criminal Code Act 1995
(the Criminal Code) by the Criminal Code Amendment (State Sponsors of Terrorism)
Act 2025 for financing a state sponsor of terrorism.

Legislative and review history
2006: Introduction of the AML/CTF regime (Tranche 1)
1.13 The AML/CTF regime began with the introduction of the Anti-Money Laundering and
Counter-Terrorism Financing Bill 2006 to the Parliament. This was subsequently
referred to the Senate Standing Committee on Legal and Constitutional Affairs and
reported on in November 2006.3
1.14 That report noted that the committee was generally satisfied with the provisions of
the Bill (as they related to combatting money laundering and the financing of
terrorism), but held concerns regarding privacy, discrimination and industry transition.
1.15 Subsequently, the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act
2006 came into effect in December 2006.4
1.16 The legislation was intended to align Australia with the Financial Action Taskforce on
Money Laundering (FATF) Forty Recommendations on Anti-Money Laundering and
Nine Special Recommendations on Counter Terrorism Financing (which have since
been consolidated and revised further as a single set of 40 Recommendations,
known also as the FATF Standards5), ensuring that Australia met international best
practice for preventing money laundering and terrorist financing.6
1.17 The FATF is an intergovernmental group that sets and oversees the quality of
implementation of AML/CTF and proliferation standards internationally. Australia has
been a member of the FATF since its inception, established by the Group of Seven
(G7).7

1.20 In 2016, the Attorney-General’s Department completed a statutory review of the Anti-
Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006 (AGD Review).12 It
found that ‘the AML/CTF regime remains relevant and appropriate, but … that there
is scope to strengthen the regime and achieve greater regulatory efficiencies.’13
1.21 The AGD Review made 84 recommendations. Several recommendations concerned:
• minimising red tape, duplication and unnecessary regulatory burden;
• better supports for industry to understand and comply with AML/CTF obligations;
• making better use of the risk-based approach to provide regulatory relief; and
• capturing services that pose a high ML/TF risk and remaining responsive to new
and emerging threats.14

2022: Senate inquiry
1.22 In March 2022, the Senate Legal and Constitutional Affairs References Committee
reported on The adequacy and efficacy of Australia’s anti-money laundering and

1.23 It found that delay in implementing Tranche 2 reforms risked harms to Australia’s

2024: Amendment Bill and Senate inquiry
1.24 The Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Amendment Bill 2024
(2024 Bill) was introduced to Parliament in September 2024.18 The 2024 Bill had
three principal objectives:
• Progress Tranche 2 reforms – extend the AML/CTF regime to certain higher-risk
services provided by real estate professionals, professional service providers
including lawyers, accountants and trust and company service providers, and
dealers in precious stones and metals (also known as ‘Tranche 2 entities’);
• Improve effectiveness – make it simpler and clearer for businesses to comply
with their obligations; and
• Modernise – reflect changing business structures, technologies and illicit
financing methodologies in the AML/CTF regime. 19
1.25 The 2024 Bill was subsequently referred to the Senate Legal and Constitutional
Legislation Committee and reported on in November 2024. The report recommended
the passage of the 2024 Bill (subject to a range of amendments), noting that the
2024 Bill would ‘reduce complexity and close regulatory gaps in the current regime,
to ensure that Australia has an effective system to deter, detect and disrupt illegal
flows of money internationally, and protect Australian businesses from exploitation by
criminals’.20 The Committee also noted that the 2024 Bill would improve Australia’s
compliance with the FATF standards, which are regularly revised and reviewed.21

2. Proposed amendments and other
matters
Schedule 1: Regulating the use of high-risk
products
2.1 Schedule 1 to the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing
Amendment Bill 2026 (the Bill) contains amendments related to regulating the use of
high-risk mechanisms. It inserts a new Part 6B – Regulating use of high-risk products
and services etc – into the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter Terrorism Financing
Act 2006 (AML/CTF Act). Part 6B inserts proposed new section 77 which provides a
simplified outline of the part and proposed new sections 77A-77F which set out the
new powers of the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre (AUSTRAC)
Chief Executive Officer (CEO).
New powers for the AUSTRAC CEO
2.2 Proposed new section 77A creates a power for the AUSTRAC CEO, by legislative
instrument, to restrict or prohibit reporting entities from using a product, service,
delivery channel or thing (the high-risk mechanism) to provide a designated service.
2.3 Subsection 77A(1) provides that before making a decision, the AUSTRAC CEO must
be satisfied that using the high-risk mechanism to provide the designated service has
caused, will cause or is likely to cause, significant harm to either or both the financial
system or the Australian community (at paragraph 77A(1)(a)), and that the restriction
or prohibition is necessary in the public interest (at paragraph 77A(1)(b)).
Certain matters that must be considered
2.4 Proposed paragraphs 77A(2)(a) – (c) set out certain matters that the AUSTRAC CEO
must consider when determining that a restriction or prohibition is necessary in the
public interest for the purposes of paragraph 77A(1)(b). These are:
• the nature and extent of the harm that has been, will be, or is likely to be, caused
by a reporting entity using the high-risk mechanism to provide the designated
service
• the effect the proposed restriction or prohibition is likely to have on that harm
• whether there are any alternative products, services, delivery channels or things
that may be used to provide the designated service.
9
2.5 Proposed paragraph 77A(2)(d) provides a regulation-making power, allowing
regulations to prescribe any other matter that the AUSTRAC CEO must consider.
2.6 Proposed subsection 77A(4) sets out the kinds of restrictions the AUSTRAC CEO
may impose under the new power, at paragraphs 77A(4)(a)–(d). These restrictions
may relate to:
• the volume or value of funds remitted, virtual assets exchanged or physical
currency or property transferred
• the method used to remit, exchange or transfer, or
• the destination (however described) of the remittance, exchange or transfer.
2.7 Proposed paragraph 77A(4)(d) also provides that the AUSTRAC CEO may require
reporting entities to provide information to AUSTRAC or do certain things to mitigate
risk.
Requirement to consult
2.8 Proposed section 77B establishes consultation requirements that must generally be
satisfied before the AUSTRAC CEO may make a legislative instrument to restrict or
prohibit a high-risk mechanism.
2.9 Under proposed subsection 77B(1), the AUSTRAC CEO may not make such an
instrument unless consultation has been undertaken in accordance with paragraphs
(a) and (b). Paragraph 77B(1)(a) would require consultation with persons reasonably
likely to be affected by the proposed instrument. Paragraph 77B(1)(b) would further
require consultation with the relevant Commonwealth, State or Territory agency
where the instrument would restrict or prohibit the use of a high-risk mechanism that
is regulated under a law administered by that agency. Proposed subsection 77B(2)
would require that consultation be undertaken for a period of at least 30 days.
2.10 Proposed subsection 77B(3) would, however, provide an exception to the
consultation requirements where the AUSTRAC CEO is satisfied that exceptional or
urgent circumstances exist. In such circumstances, the AUSTRAC CEO may make
an instrument without prior consultation.
2.11 Proposed subsection 77B(4) sets out the manner in which consultation may be
undertaken, and the information that must be provided, for the AUSTRAC CEO to
have complied with the requirement under subsection 77B(1).
2.12 Proposed subsection 77B(5) provides that a failure to comply with the consultation
requirement in subsection 77B(1) does not invalidate a legislative instrument made
under subsection 77A(1). The Explanatory Memorandum (EM) states that the
‘consultation process is intended to be mandatory’ but adds that:
[…] an otherwise valid legislative instrument is effective despite any defect in the
relevant consultation process. The no-validity clause is appropriate in this
instance, noting that an exceptional or urgent circumstance must exist to justify
making an instrument without consultation. Further, any instrument made without
10
consultation will only be in force for a limited period, and any extension of that
instrument must be subject to the consultation process. This will preserve the
intent of the framework while enabling it to be used, in specific circumstances
and for short periods, to respond to high-risk mechanisms that have caused, will
cause or is likely to cause, significant harm to either or both of the financial
system and the Australian community, where the restriction or prohibition is
necessary in the public interest.1
Duration of restrictions or prohibitions
2.13 Proposed section 77C provides that the duration of a restriction or prohibition in the
first instance would depend on whether consultation was undertaken prior to making
the instrument—no more than 6 months if no consultation was undertaken and no
more than 3 years if consultation was undertaken.

2.15 Proposed sections 77E and 77F set out offence provisions. A person subject to a
restriction or prohibition commits an offence if:
• the person engages in conduct that breaches the restriction or prohibition
• the person offers to use a product, service, delivery channel or thing to provide a
designated service where that use would breach the restriction or prohibition.
2.16 For both offences, strict liability applies to the person’s conduct. The EM states that
this is appropriate because:
Requiring the prosecution to establish fault … would not be practical and would

whether the restriction or prohibition remains in place. The defence of mistake of
fact under section 9.2 of the Criminal Code would still be available in relation to
these elements.2

Schedule 2: Amending the definition of financing of
terrorism
2.20 Schedule 2 would amend the definition of financing of terrorism in section 5 of the
AML/CTF Act to reference new offences introduced into the Criminal Code by the
Criminal Code Amendment (State Sponsors of Terrorism) Act 2025 for financing a
state sponsor of terrorism. The definition would also be updated to allow it to reflect
developments in Australian sanction laws relevant to combatting terrorism and its
financing.
2.21 The Bill would update the existing section 5 paragraph (a) in the definition of
financing of terrorism under the AML/CTF Act to expressly include the offences of
getting funds to, from or for a state sponsor of terrorism (set out in section 112.5 of
the Criminal Code), and offences against financing a state terrorist act targeted at
Australia (set out in Division 113 of the Criminal Code).
2.22 The Bill would also insert new paragraphs (ba) and (bb) into the definition of
financing of terrorism. This would provide a regulation-making power to prescribe
offences against the Charter of the United Nations Act 1945 and the Autonomous
Sanctions Act 2011, or against regulations made under either of those Acts, for the
purposes of the definition in the AML/CTF Act.
Harmonisation with other frameworks
2.23 Two technical amendments are intended to ensure:
• State or Territory laws that correspond to offences prescribed under amended
paragraphs (a), (b), (ba) and (bb) of the definition of financing of terrorism are
appropriately captured; and
• the offences covered by the amended definition of financing of terrorism are
appropriately captured under the suspicious matter reporting framework at section
41 of the AML/CTF Act, by referencing paragraphs (a), (b), (ba), (bb) or (c) of the
amended definition of financing of terrorism.
Schedule 3: Technical amendments
2.24 Schedule 3 makes technical amendments to the AML/CTF Act which the EM states
were identified through implementation of the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-
Terrorism Financing Amendment Act 2024. This includes to:
• amend initial, ongoing and enhanced customer due diligence requirements to
make compliance easier for reporting entities
• amend existing definitions under the AML/CTF Act, and introduce new definitions,
to ensure that the definitions for domestic politically exposed person and foreign
politically exposed person apply appropriately when designated services are
provided at or through a permanent establishment in Australia or foreign countries

3. Committee comment and
recommendations
3.1 The Committee welcomes the breadth of views expressed in submissions and
acknowledges the diversity of perspectives on the proposed amendments to the
Anti‑Money Laundering and Counter‑Terrorism Financing Act 2006 (AML/CTF Act)
made by the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Amendment
Bill 2026 (Bill). The Committee notes that submitters shared a common recognition of
the seriousness of money laundering, terrorism financing, organised crime and
related financial threats, as well as the need for Australia’s AML/CTF regime to
remain effective in a rapidly evolving risk environment.
Restricting high-risk mechanisms
3.2 The Committee considers that the proposed new power relating to high-risk
mechanisms set out in Schedule 1 to the Bill responds to a well‑identified gap in the
existing legislative framework. Evidence provided by the Department of Home Affairs
(the Department) and the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre
(AUSTRAC) demonstrated that existing entity‑level supervisory and enforcement
powers are insufficient to address emerging, sector‑wide risks associated with
particular products, services or delivery channels. The Committee accepts that
technological change, new payment systems and innovative financial services have
increased the speed, scale and complexity of financial crime, reducing the
effectiveness of traditional, entity‑by‑entity regulatory responses.
3.3 While a number of submitters raised concerns regarding the breadth of the proposed
high‑risk mechanism power, the Committee is largely satisfied that the Bill contains
appropriate safeguards to ensure its use is targeted, proportionate and accountable.
In particular, the Committee notes that the power is subject to a public interest test,
time limits, and parliamentary scrutiny through the ability of either House to review
and disallow legislative instruments. The Committee considers these safeguards are
mostly sufficient to mitigate the risk of inappropriate or over‑broad application.
3.4 The Committee recognises the concerns expressed by some participants in the
inquiry that the new power must be used, and be seen to be used, in accordance
with objective and consistent criteria. To this end the Committee recommends that
the high-risk mechanism power be exercised by the Minister following advice from
the AUSTRAC CEO. In practice this means that the AUSTRAC CEO must still
undertake consultations as set out in the Bill but is doing so to provide the best
advice to the Minister exercising the power.
29
Recommendation 1
3.5 The Committee recommends that the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-
Terrorism Financing Amendment Bill 2026 be amended so that the proposed
new power relating to high-risk mechanisms set out in Schedule 1 is exercised
by the responsible Minister on advice from the AUSTRAC CEO.
3.6 The Committee strongly encourages the Minister, if and when any instrument is
made under the new regime, to provide the clearest and most detailed information
possible in the instrument itself and/or its explanatory material with regard to the
assessments made under subsections 77A(1) and (2); including the criteria used to
assess the significant harm being addressed, the public interest protected by the
restriction of the high-risk mechanism and the consideration of alternative measures.
This will ensure that the power is both used, and seen to be used, consistently and
appropriately.
3.7 The Committee has considered a range of evidence on the scale of harm caused by
cryptocurrency Automated Teller Machines (crypto ATMs). Referencing a nationwide
law enforcement operation, the Australian Baking Association submitted that “after
interviewing 90 of the 100 most prolific users of crypto ATMs … AUSTRAC estimated
that about 85 per cent were scam victims or money mules coerced into moving
suspected illicit funds.”1 Crypto ATM operator CoinFlip pointed out the uncertain
generalisability of this finding, noting the small and non-random sample. CoinFlip
submitted its own estimate that scam-related activity accounted for only 1.1 per cent
of total cryptocurrency ATM transactions by value across the 2024 dataset. This
figure only includes transactions related to scams and not other forms of money
laundering or terror financing. The Committee gave weight to the evidence of both
organisations when determining its position on the Bill.
3.8 The Committee has concluded that crypto ATMs contribute significant harm to scam
victims and those coerced into moving the proceeds of crime. Crypto ATMs also
increase ML/TF risk and complicate AUSTRAC’s work identifying and preventing
financial crime. The Committee therefore considers that the Minister, in consultation
with AUSTRAC, should prioritise consideration of whether the restriction or
prohibition of crypto ATMs is in the public interest.
3.9 The Committee also considered evidence that restricting or prohibiting established
high-risk mechanisms risks displacing criminal activity into less regulated
mechanisms. The Committee acknowledges this is a real risk inherent in any
mechanism-based regulatory power.
3.10 The Committee has considered the Law Council of Australia (Law Council) concerns
that, because the Explanatory Memorandum provides crypto ATMs as the only
worked example of a high-risk mechanism, the practical scope of the power under
proposed section 77A is difficult to assess. The Committee accepts the Department's
evidence that the power is deliberately framed by reference to the mechanism posing
1 Mr Chris Taylor, Chief of Policy, Australian Banking Association, Committee Hansard, 9 June 2026, p. 1.
30
significant harm, rather than by reference to any particular sector, and that this
design is intentional. Noting the Committee’s recommendation above, it allows the
Minister to respond to new high-risk mechanisms as they emerge, rather than
requiring further amendment of the Act each time. The Committee is nevertheless
conscious that a power of this breadth, illustrated by only a single example, provides
limited practical guidance to reporting entities.
3.11 The Committee therefore considers that the proposed guidance is not sufficient on its
own, and recommends the Explanatory Memorandum be updated prior to the Bill's
passage to include additional illustrative examples of the kinds of products, services,
delivery channels or things the power is, and is not, intended to capture including
express confirmation of whether the power is intended to extend to professional
services regulated under Table 6 of the AML/CTF Act. This would not narrow the
power itself, which the Committee otherwise considers appropriately targeted, but
would materially improve the ability of affected sectors to assess their own exposure.
3.12 The Committee has also considered the broader suite of additional safeguards
proposed by the Australian Finance Industry Association (AFIA), including mandatory
regulatory concurrence with prudential and market regulators, ministerial approval for
system-wide interventions, more structured statutory criteria and evidentiary
thresholds, mandatory impact assessments.
3.13 The Committee does not consider it necessary to require the AUSTRAC CEO to
obtain the concurrence of the Treasurer, Australian Prudential Regulation Authority
(APRA), Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) or the Reserve
Bank of Australia (RBA) before providing advice to the Minister. The Committee is
satisfied that vesting the power in the Minister, the existing disallowance mechanism,
combined with the consultation requirements addressed elsewhere in this chapter,
already subjects the exercise of the power to both expert and parliamentary scrutiny,
and that adding further concurrence requirements risks slowing the ability of the
AUSTRAC CEO and Minister to respond to fast-moving, cross-sector risks.
Recommendation 2
3.14 That, pending the passage of the Bill, the Minister, in consultation with
AUSTRAC, consider the merits of restricting or prohibiting cryptocurrency
ATMs as a matter of priority.
Consultation requirements and the no-invalidity clauses
3.15 Furthermore, the Committee shares the concerns raised by the Senate Scrutiny of
Bills Committee, in relation to the no-invalidity clauses that would preserve a
legislative instrument made under subsection 77A(1) even if the AUSTRAC CEO fails
to consult with affected stakeholders in accordance with the requirements in
proposed subsection 77B(1). The Committee welcomes the Government’s
reassurance that the consultation requirements are intended to be mandatory, and
that the no-invalidity clauses are only intended to protect against limited instances
such as inadvertent flaws in consultation. The Committee notes the Government’s
31
advice that instruments to regulate high-risk mechanisms are subject to
parliamentary oversight, including disallowance, and judicial review. To facilitate
complete parliamentary oversight, the Minister should publish details of consultation
undertaken when making an instrument.
Recommendation 3
3.16 The Committee recommends that the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-
Terrorism Financing Amendment Bill 2026 be amended to:
• require that as soon as practicable following the conclusion of the
minimum 30-day consultation period required by 77B(2) or 77D(3) when

Enhancing action against the financing of terrorism
3.17 The Committee notes that some submitters were concerned about the updated
definition of ‘Financing of terrorism’ in the Bill. The Committee supports the
amendments in Schedule 2 of the Bill expanding the definition of financing of
terrorism. The Committee considers that explicitly referencing offences relating to
state sponsors of terrorism and international sanctions laws strengthens regulatory
clarity and ensures alignment between the AML/CTF framework and Australia’s
broader counter‑terrorism and sanctions regimes.
3.18 The Committee notes that Australia currently recognises only a single state sponsor
of terrorism, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. The Committee considers that
the extension of CTF obligations to incorporate state sponsors of terrorism therefore
adds no more additional complexity than the listing of a new non-state terrorist
organisation covered by entities’ existing CTF obligations would. The committee
therefore supports Schedule 2 taking effect on the timeline proposed in the Bill.
Other issues
3.19 The Committee acknowledges evidence it received that there are some areas where
the Bill could be improved more broadly to help ensure compliance and give clarity to
affected entities. These are discussed further below.
32
Definitions
3.20 The Committee appreciates that whilst submitters were largely supportive of the
principles of the Bill, and the AML/CTF regime, their ability to ensure compliance with
the existing framework and the proposed amendments of the Bill can be stymied by
ambiguous terminology. At the highest level, the Committee is concerned that entities
may be impacted by the proposed changes in the Bill, not clearly knowing whether
they are captured under the Professional Services, Table 6, Section 6(6B) of the
AML/CTF Act. The Committee understands that there is a big difference between
small businesses and large corporations and who may be considered a ‘Senior
Manager’ across the spectrum of organisations. The Committee also notes that
technology is evolving at an ever-increasing rate. Terminology in the AML/CTF
regime should be technology neutral to ensure the longevity of the framework. As
such, the Committee makes a number of recommendations in relation to addressing
these issues, and improving clarity of terminology.
Recommendation 4
3.21 The Committee recommends that the Explanatory Memorandum for the Anti-
Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Amendment Bill 2026, or
the Bill itself, be updated to better explain key terms including:
• clarifying the scope of application of the Professional Services in Table 6,
section 6(6B) of the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism
Financing Act 2006
• updating the ‘pass on’ wording at proposed sections 66(3)(a) and 64(3) of
the Bill to become technology neutral and align with the provisions of
Financial Action Task Force Recommendation 16
• providing more detailed guidance as to who is a ‘Senior Manager’ under
section 5 of the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing
Act 2006
• providing additional worked examples in the Explanatory Memorandum of
the high-risk mechanisms proposed section 77A is intended to capture,
including whether professional services are intended to be captured.
The Committee further recommends that any consequent regulations arising
from the Bill, ensure clarity in definitions and terminology, to reduce the
compliance burden while maximising the effectiveness of the laws.

mitigating money laundering and financing terrorism. In particular, the Committee is
concerned about the Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS) which was intended to
be decommissioned, but remains active. The Committee considers that the New
Payments Platform (NPP) offers better security and is a preferable system to BECS
33
when it comes to supporting the transparency of transactions necessary for the

3.23 The Committee notes that the transition from BECS to the NPP was considered by

tabled on 29 June 2026, that committee made several recommendations relating to
the NPP, which the PJCIS endorses. This includes:
Recommendation 9
That Treasury, in consultation with stakeholders, re-establish a clear timeline for

Recommendation 10
That the Reserve Bank of Australia and Treasury continue to work closely with
