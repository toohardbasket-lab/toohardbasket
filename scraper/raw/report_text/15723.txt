1. Introduction
1.1 The Joint Standing Committee on Treaties (JSCOT) was appointed by the
Commonwealth Parliament to inquire into any treaty to which Australia has become a
signatory, upon the treaty being tabled in the Parliament or as referred by a Minister.1
1.2 The Committee’s resolution of appointment empowers it to inquire into and report on:
a. matters arising from treaties and related National Interest Analyses and
proposed treaty actions and related Explanatory Statements presented or
deemed to be presented to the Parliament;
b. any question relating to a treaty or other international instrument, whether or
not negotiated to completion, referred to the committee by:
i. either House of the Parliament; or
ii. a Minister; and
c. such other matters as may be referred to the committee by the Minister for
Foreign Affairs and on such conditions as the Minister may prescribe.
1.3 Prior to tabling, major treaty actions are subject to a National Interest Analysis (NIA),
prepared by the Australian Government. The NIA considers the treaty, outlines the
treaty obligations and any regulatory or financial implications, and reports the results
of consultations undertaken with state and territory governments, federal, state and
territory agencies, and with industry or non-government organisations.
1.4 The Committee takes account of the NIA in its examination of the treaty text, in
addition to other evidence taken during the inquiry.
1.5 The treaties, and matters arising from them, are evaluated to ensure ratification is in
the national interest, and that unintended or negative effects on Australia do not
arise.
1.6 The report contains the Committee’s review of two major treaty actions: the
Agreement to Establish the Pacific Resilience Facility and the Council of Europe
Convention on the Manipulation of Sports Competitions.
1.7 The Agreement to Establish the Pacific Resilience Facility establishes the Pacific
Resilience Facility as an international organisation. The organisation will be a
financial institution that manages and distributes funding with the purpose of helping

2. Agreement to Establish the
Pacific Resilience Facility
2.1 The Agreement to Establish the Pacific Resilience Facility was referred to the
Committee on 30 October 2025.
2.2 The Committee received three submissions and held a public hearing in Canberra on
19 February 2026. A list of submissions can be found in Appendix A and the details
of the hearing and witnesses can be found in Appendix B. The transcript of evidence
from the public hearing can be accessed through the Committee’s website.
Overview and background
2.3 The Agreement to Establish the Pacific Resilience Facility (the PRF Agreement)
establishes the Pacific Resilience Facility (PRF) as an international organisation.1
The intention is that the PRF will be a financial institution that manages and
distributes funding.2 The purpose of the PRF is to help its Members in the Pacific
build resilience to the impacts of climate change.3 The PRF aims to strengthen the
collective financial resilience of the region through:
• receiving and making climate and disaster grants for climate adaptation, disaster
preparedness, nature-based solutions, and loss and damage projects
• support rapid response to disasters
• receiving and making social and community grants for the Sustainable
Development Goals (SDGs) and to support resilient and sustainable
communities.4
2.4 Establishing the PRF has been an enduring priority for the Pacific Islands Forum
(PIF), the Pacific region’s platform for collaboration, pursuing shared goals and
addressing regional issues.5 The PRF was proposed at a (PIF) meeting for Economic
Ministers in 2018 and has seen continued consideration at subsequent meetings and

Justification
2.5 The climate crisis is a considerable threat to lives, culture and security in the Pacific
Islands.8 The PRF Agreement builds upon years of regional collaborative work
responding to this crisis.9 Australia has shown commitment to responding to the
needs of the Pacific region and its resilience through aid, agreements and
contributions to regional forums. This has included climate focused treaties such as
the Australia-Tuvalu Falepili Union10 and Australia’s ongoing participation as a
founding member of the PIF.11 Establishing the PRF supports Australia’s commitment
to this area by targeting climate financing through a collaborative entity.12 The
Agreement contributes to Australia’s national interests by strengthening the resilience
of the Pacific region and demonstrating our commitment to regional security,
prosperity and stability in the Pacific.13
2.6 Joining the PRF will allow Australia to meaningfully contribute to a Pacific-led
initiative with strong regional backing.14 Furthermore, the PRF provides a trusted,
accountable mechanism for delivering climate finance in a Pacific endorsed
manner.15 This bolster’s Australia’s standing as a regional climate leader,
underpinning our credibility and broader strategic efforts.16 A more resilient Pacific
region also eases stress on Australia’s humanitarian and defence systems.17

Governance
2.10 Articles 6, 7, 8 and 10 define and establish the structure and governing organs of the
PRF.27 The PRF will be governed by a Council, a Board and any other committees or
affiliate entities as may be decided by either body.28
2.11 The Council is the supreme governance organ and will be comprised by Ministers of
Finance (or similar) designated by each PRF Member.29 All powers of the PRF will be
vested in the Council who may then delegate certain powers and responsibilities.30
Responsibilities of the Council will include:
• approval of the strategic plan, annual budget, annual report and financial
statements31
• adopting and amending the PRF Rules of Procedure and Regulations32
• providing leadership, guidance, strategic direction and determining risk
tolerance.33
2.12 The Board sits under the Council and shall consist of seven to nine Directors
appointed by the Council.34 The Board will be responsible for the overall operation of
the PRF and will exercise any powers delegated by the Council.35

Operations and finance
2.14 The PRF shall operate in accordance with the Agreement, the PRF Rules of
Procedure and Regulations and any other policies established by the Council and/or
the Board.37 The PRF Rules of Procedure and Regulations, which shall be adopted
by the PRF, will outline the rules and regulations for governance, administration,
management and operation.38 Article 9(b) of the Agreement ensures that these rules
and regulations will not alter PRF Members’ rights under the Agreement or impose
any obligations, expenses or liabilities that are not within the Agreement.39
2.15 The PRF shall have three categories of funding sources:
• voluntary contributions from PRF Members
• investment income
• any other sources deemed acceptable under the PRF Rules of Procedure and
Regulations.40
2.16 The PRF shall not receive funding or assistance that prejudices or interferes with the
PRF’s purposes and functions; or that interferes in a Member’s political affairs.41 Any
investments or finance provided by the PRF shall be done so in accordance with the
Agreement and the PRF Rules of Procedure and Regulations.42 Having received
finance provided by the PRF, Members must use it exclusively to pursue the
Agreement’s purposes and report its use to the Board.43 Regarding membership, the
Council may decide that PRF member must make a financial contribution to be
eligible for funding applications and receiving Finance.44 Article 11(d) states that
payment is not required to become or remain a PRF member.45

Privileges and Immunities
2.18 PRF Members are obliged to provide such status, exemptions, privileges and
immunities that are necessary to enable the PRF to fulfil its objectives and carry out
its functions within a Member’s territory.47 Furthermore, Article 13(b) allows Members
to offer such exemptions, privileges and immunities to subsidiary entities and
affiliates of the PRF which carry out PRF functions within a Member’s territory.48 The
Agreement gives the Council the power to waive exemptions, privileges and
immunities of the PRF and its staff as appropriate in the best interests of the PRF.49
Consultation
2.19 The Agreement was negotiated between PIF members over online discussions, and
in-person workshops throughout December 2024 and January 2025.50 Negotiations
concluded in March 2025, and the Agreement text was endorsed by Forum
Members’ Foreign Ministers.51 The Agreement is not envisioned to have any impacts
on state and territory governments.52 Public consultation did not occur in Australia,
however, it is ongoing with communities in Pacific Island countries.53

Termination
Withdrawal
2.21 Any party may withdraw from the Agreement by written notification of its intentions to
withdraw from the Agreement. Such withdrawal shall become effective from the date
of receipt of the notice by the Council.57
2.22 A PRF Member shall cease to be a member of the PRF if:
• it withdraws from the Agreement, and the Council has received its written notice
• it ceases membership with the PIF
• the PRF is dissolved
• the Council so decides.58
2.23 In accordance with Article 22(b), any party which ceases to be a PRF member will
not be refunded the amount of any contributions made to the PRF.59
Dissolution
2.24 The Council may unanimously agree to dissolve the PRF.60 In such circumstances,
the Council will notify the Board and the Host Country, and the Board will cease all
activities except those necessary to affect the orderly liquidation of the PRF.61 Once
the Board has completed its work, the Council will remove all Directors from office,
distribute any surplus PRF assets according to allocation policies adopted by the
council, and the Agreement will terminate.62

Issues
A Pacific-designed, led and implemented agreement
2.30 The PRF Agreement is the result of years of planning and negotiations to create an
agreement by Pacific nations that directly targets Pacific climate needs.88 At the
public hearing, the Committee expressed interest in the unique nature of the
Agreement and how it addresses the Pacific climate priorities. The Committee heard
from the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT) that:
The Pacific region has long affirmed that climate change remains the single
greatest threat to livelihoods, security and the wellbeing of the peoples of the
Pacific. We've listened and we're working together on Pacific led, Australian
backed solutions. The Pacific Resilience Facility, or PRF, is one such solution.
It's a genuine Pacific offering to fill a critical gap in the climate finance

Overview and background
3.3 Australia plans to ratify the Council of Europe Convention on the Manipulation of
Sports Competitions (known as the Macolin Convention).1 The Convention was
created in Switzerland in 2014.2 Australia signed it in 2019 and is one of 43 countries
that have agreed to its aims.3
3.4 The Australian Government previously undertook a review of its sport integrity
systems (the Wood Review) and agreed that Australia should become a party to the
Macolin Convention as part of strengthening its approach to protecting sport from
corruption.4
3.5 The Macolin Convention is the only international agreement that specifically aims to
stop the manipulation of sports competitions.5 It requests governments to work with
sports organisations, betting companies, and event organisers to prevent, detect, and
punish sports manipulation.6 It also sets out a shared legal framework that helps
countries cooperate more effectively against a problem that increasingly crosses
borders.7

Justification
3.6 If Australia ratifies the Macolin Convention and introduces national criminal laws
against sports manipulation, it will help strengthen worldwide efforts to protect the
integrity of sport.8 This approach recognises that sports manipulation often occurs
across borders and that threats to Australian competitions can come from around the
world.9
3.7 Australia has a strong reputation for protecting sport integrity, however, the
international nature of corruption means that no single Australian organisation can
manage these threats alone.10 The Macolin Convention would support a stronger,
more coordinated national approach by addressing Australia’s lack of a unified
system to combat match-fixing and betting related corruption, creating a framework
for national laws and an information sharing platform.11
3.8 Countries that join the Convention gain access to expert networks that are preparing
monitoring systems and guidelines, and joining would allow Australia to contribute to
and influence these developments.12
3.9 Additionally, online and offshore wagering platforms have made it more difficult to
create a strong and trusted regulatory system for sports betting.13 Working closely
with other Convention members enables Australia to access international projects
that address issues such as illegal sports betting, emerging sports that attract
wagering but lack clear governance structures, and the use of cryptocurrency to
conceal corrupt activity.14
3.10 Australia’s involvement with the Convention shows its commitment to tackling global
sports manipulation.15

3.51 The Committee was concerned about the apparent delay in time between signing

Committee comment
3.52 The Committee acknowledges the significant role sport plays in Australia’s identity,
community and economy. The manipulation of sports competitions through match-
fixing and illegal offshore gambling poses a threat to the integrity of sports. The
Committee welcomes efforts to combat the manipulation of sports competitions and
to build Australia’s sport integrity.
3.53 The Committee recognises the work that has been done to ensure Australia is
aligned with the Convention’s obligations. The establishment of Sport Integrity
Australia has provided Australia with a national platform that coordinates an
approach to building sport integrity. The Committee looks forward to the adoption of
the National Sport Integrity Offences and the role they will play in pursuing
prosecution against sports manipulation.
3.54 The Committee understands that ratifying the Convention will provide Australia with
opportunities to combat the manipulation of sports competitions. The Committee
heard from Responsible Wagering Australia that under the Convention, Australia will
have the opportunity to address the advertisement of and accessibility to illegal
offshore betting providers. The Convention will also enable conversations about what
could be done to pursue financial restrictions such as blocking transactions to known
illegal providers.
3.55 The Committee sought to understand Australia’s delay in ratifying the Macolin
Convention. During the inquiry, the Committee’s concerns were adequately
addressed. The Committee acknowledges that factors of the delay such as the
establishment of Sport Integrity Australia were necessary efforts for Australia to meet
the Convention’s obligations.

4. Minor Treaty Action
4.1 Minor treaty actions are, in most instances, technical amendments to existing treaties
that do not significantly impact the national interest. They are presented to the
Committee with an explanatory statement and are published on the Committee’s
website unless a request for confidentiality is received and agreed to by the
Committee.
4.2 The Committee can choose to formally inquire into these treaty actions or accept
them without a formal inquiry. Once considered they are incorporated into a report of
the Committee at the next opportunity.

Background
4.5 The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO)’s
International Convention against Doping in Sport (The Convention), is a multilateral
treaty which entered into force on 1 February 2007.1 The Convention aims to prevent
and eliminate doping in sport through international cooperation and standardised
legislation, guidelines, regulation and rules.2 The Convention has 191 State Parties
making it the second most ratified UNESCO treaty.3
4.6 The Convention provides a legal framework for States Parties to cooperate
internationally for the protection of athletes and sporting ethics, restrict access to

4.7 Annex I of the Convention includes the Prohibited List - International Standard, which

consultation.6 States Parties, including Australia, commit to aligning with these
updates to maintain consistency in anti-doping measures worldwide. As such JCSOT
has considered the annual amendments to Annex 1 roughly every year since its entry

Proposed minor treaty action
4.8 The proposed minor treaty action is to accept amendments to Annex I of the
Convention to reflect the 2026 WADA Prohibited List, adopted on 11 September
2025 and effective 1 January 2026.8
4.9 On 1 October 2025, the Director-General of UNESCO notified States Parties of the
proposed amendments to Annex I.9 The amendments were deemed to be approved
by the Conference of Parties on 14 November 2025, 45 days after the Director-
General’s notification.10
4.10 The proposed amendments will then enter into force 45 days after notification by the
Director-General of the deemed approval: 1 January 2026.11 That is aside from any
state that notifies the Director-General that it does not accept the proposed
amendment.12
