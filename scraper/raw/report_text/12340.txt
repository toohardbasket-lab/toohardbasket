Chapter 1
Introduction and background
1.1 On 13 February 2025, the Senate referred the Whistleblower Protection
Authority Bill 2025 (No. 2) to the Legal and Constitutional Affairs Legislation
Committee (the committee) for inquiry and report by 29 August 2025.1
1.2 On 21 July 2025, the inquiry lapsed at the conclusion of the 47th Parliament.
On 23 July 2025, the Senate referred the Whistleblower Protection Authority Bill
2025 (Bill) to the committee for inquiry and report by 29 August 2025.2
1.3 The Bill would establish an independent statutory authority, the Whistleblower
Protection Authority (WPA), and provide for the appointment of the
Whistleblower Protection Commissioner (the Commissioner).3
1.4 According to the Bill, the Commissioner would have three broad functions:
(a) receiving and facilitating the investigation of disclosures of wrongdoing;
(b) providing assistance, advice, guidance and support to whistleblowers
and potential whistleblowers in relation to making a disclosure of
wrongdoing; and
(c) investigating any mistreatment of whistleblowers and undertaking
enforcement activities as necessary.4
Conduct of the inquiry and acknowledgement
1.5 In accordance with its usual practice, the committee advertised the inquiry on
its website and wrote to organisations and individuals inviting submissions by
30 June 2025. The committee extended that date to 7 July 2025. The committee
received 80 submissions, which are listed at Appendix 1, and held a public
hearing in Canberra on 13 August 2025.5 A list of the witnesses who appeared
at the hearing is at Appendix 2. The committee thanks those organisations and
individuals who made submissions and who gave evidence at the public
hearing.

Background and purpose of the Bill
1.8 In 1994, the Senate Select Committee on Public Interest Whistleblowing tabled
its report entitled In the Public Interest.6 This select committee recommended that
legislation be enacted to establish an independent agency, whose role would
include receiving and arranging for the investigation of public interest
disclosures (PIDs), and ensuring the protection of people making such
disclosures.7
1.9 In 2013, the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (PID Act) came into force. It aims
to 'promote the integrity and accountability of the Commonwealth public
sector'. The PID Act introduced a framework to encourage public officials to
make PIDs by ensuring they 'are supported and are protected from adverse
consequences'.8
1.10 On 15 July 2016, the independent statutory review of the whistleblower
framework conducted by Mr Philip Moss AM (Moss Review) was presented to
government. The Moss Review did not recommend the establishment of a
whistleblower protection authority. It found:
The experience of whistleblowers under the PID Act is not a happy one. Few
individuals who had made PIDs reported that they felt supported. Some felt
that their disclosure had not been adequately investigated or that their
agency had not adequately addressed the conduct reported. Many
disclosers reported experiencing reprisal as a result of bringing forward
their concerns.9

1.11 Agencies similarly reported difficulties in applying the PID Act:
Most agencies noted that the bulk of disclosures related to personal
employment-related grievances and were better addressed through other
processes. Agencies noted also that the PID Act's procedures and mandatory
obligations upon individuals are ill-adapted to addressing such
disclosures.10
1.12 In June 2023, the independent review of the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2010
(QLD) was presented to the Queensland Government. The Review of the Public
Interest Disclosure Act 2010 (review of the Queensland PID Act) did not
recommend the establishment of a standalone whistleblower protection
authority. It suggested:
The multiplicity of roles urged by advocates – support, advocacy, assisting
agencies in managing disclosures, legal advice, intervention in litigation and
investigating reprisals – bespeak an organisation of considerable size,
resource-heavy and, arguably, redolent of inherent conflicts of interest.11
1.13 The review of the Queensland PID Act found:
Given the relatively small number of whistleblower cases that result in
disputes in Queensland, it is not certain that the resources necessary to
establish a separate body to undertake [the whistleblower protection
authority role] would be justified.12
1.14 Senator David Pocock, who introduced the Bill into the Parliament, stated that,
essentially, the Senate Select Committee on Public Interest Whistleblowing had
recommended the creation of a whistleblower protection authority, a radical
idea for the time when whistleblowers were often considered 'traitors':
Fast-forward thirty years, and things have changed. Now, we better
understand the true value of whistleblowers. They are an important part of
accountability and a healthy democracy—risking their jobs, reputations,
and safety to shine a light on wrongdoing.
But for all that risk, we've fallen short. For decades, whistleblowers have had
to fend for themselves. And today, the need for a Whistleblower Protection
Authority is greater than ever.
It's time we finally gave whistleblowers the support they deserve—because
when they are protected, all of us benefit.13

The current public sector whistleblower framework
1.15 Within the current whistleblower framework, the Attorney-General's
Department (AGD) highlighted the role of whistleblower protections in
maintaining the integrity of public institutions:
Whistleblower protections play an important role in promoting the integrity
of public institutions and ensuring public trust and confidence in those
institutions by helping bring to light allegations of wrongdoing.14
1.16 The AGD administers the PID Act which aims:
…to encourage and facilitate reporting on wrongdoing by public officials by
providing protections to public officials who make public interest
disclosures and ensuring that Commonwealth agencies properly investigate
and respond to such disclosures.15
1.17 The AGD stated that the government is committed to a 'staged reform of the PID
Act'. It has implemented stage 1 reforms and is committed to further stage 2
reforms.16
Stage 1 reforms
1.18 On 1 July 2023, the amendments to the PID Act made by the Public Interest
Disclosure Amendment (Review) Act 2023 commenced. According to the AGD,
those amendments:
 strengthened protections for disclosers and witnesses
 focused the framework on integrity-related wrongdoing
 made the framework easier to administer, and
 enhanced oversight of the framework by the Ombudsman and the
[Inspector-General of Intelligence and Security].17
1.19 The AGD reported the stage 1 reforms implemented:
 21 of the 33 recommendations of the Moss Review;
 recommendations 6.1 and 6.3 of the 2017 Parliamentary Joint Committee on
Corporations and Financial Services Whistleblower Protections report; and
 recommendations 10 and 11 of the 2020 Parliamentary Joint Committee on
Intelligence and Security Press Freedom report.18
1.20 The department noted that the government is considering the remaining
recommendations as part of stage 2 reforms. Some of those 'recommendations

Stage 2 reforms
1.21 The government has committed to further reforms to the PID Act. In late 2023,
the AGD 'released a Consultation Paper, seeking views on what reforms are
needed to ensure the public sector whistleblowing framework remains
fit-for-purpose and accessible for public officials to report wrongdoing'.20
1.22 The Consultation Paper invited submissions that engaged with the following
areas of reform:
 making a disclosure within government;
 pathways to make disclosures outside of government;
 protections and remedies under the PID Act;
 oversight and integrity agencies, including consideration of an
independent body to support whistleblowers or a potential
Whistleblower Protection Authority or Commissioner; and
 clarity of the PID Act.21
1.23 The AGD received 56 submissions during the consultation process. The
submissions broadly supported:
 expanding the range of public officials who can receive protected
disclosures to better ensure disclosures are provided to an appropriate
agency for consideration;
 clarification of when disclosures can be made outside of government;
 increased access to professional assistance for whistleblowers and witnesses;
 increased consistency between whistleblower protection frameworks that
apply to the public, private and not-for-profit sectors; and
 the simplification and clarification of the PID Act.22

Part 3: Whistleblower protections
1.31 The Bill would provide for the making of a 'disclosure of wrongdoing' and set
out how the Commissioner would deal with that disclosure. The term
'disclosure of wrongdoing' is defined as:
disclosure of wrongdoing means:
(a) a public interest disclosure within the meaning of the Public Interest
Disclosure Act 2013; or
(b) a disclosure of information qualifying for protection under Part 9.4AAA
of the Corporations Act 2001; or
(c) a referral of an allegation, or information, that raises a corruption issue
under the National Anti-Corruption Commission Act 2022, where made or
provided by a current or former employee, volunteer or contractor of any
Commonwealth agency, corporation or other entity to whom the corruption
issue relates; or
(d) an alleged or suspected contravention of a code of conduct for
parliamentarians provided for in an Act of Parliament, where made by a
current or former employee, volunteer or contractor of an organisation to
whom the contravention also relates; or
(e) a disclosure about wrongdoing in accordance with any other
Commonwealth law, including the laws outlined at paragraph 4(1)(b),
where made by a current or former employee, volunteer or contractor of any
Commonwealth agency, corporation or other entity to whom the alleged or
suspected wrongdoing relates; or
(f) a disclosure of wrongdoing of a type prescribed by the rules for the
purposes of this paragraph.32
1.32 Another key definition in clause 8 of the Bill is 'whistleblower protection issue':
whistleblower protection issue means:
(a) an act or omission constituting reprisal or victimisation or which causes
detriment to any person, as a result of that person or any other person
making a disclosure of wrongdoing under a Commonwealth law to which
this Act applies; or

Disclosures, information or requests
1.33 A person would be able to make, or provide information about, a disclosure of
wrongdoing to the Commissioner. A person would also be able to request
information, advice, guidance or assistance in relation to a disclosure of
wrongdoing or a whistleblower protection issue from the Commissioner.34
1.34 The person would be able to make, or provide information about, a disclosure
of wrongdoing on behalf of another person, a Commonwealth agency, or a body
or association of persons. They would be able to do so either orally or in writing
and may choose to remain anonymous.35
1.35 The Commissioner would be required to ask the person making, or providing
information about, a disclosure to elect whether or not they want to be kept
informed about the action taken in relation to that disclosure.36
1.36 A 'public official' (as defined) would be required, as soon as practicable after
becoming aware of an allegation, or information, that raises a whistleblower
protection issue, to:
 refer the allegation or information to the Commissioner; or
 if the public official is an employee of a Commonwealth agency other than
the head of the agency, notify the head of the agency of the allegation or
information.37
1.37 The Bill would direct the Commissioner to provide general information, advice,
guidance and assistance to a range of people, which must be determined in
consultation with various agencies and organisations.38 The Commissioner must
ensure that the relevant staff of the WPA have the appropriate specialist training
and experience to provide advice, guidance and support.39

Dealing with disclosures of wrongdoing and whistleblower protection issues
1.38 The Commissioner would be able to deal with a disclosure of, or information
about, wrongdoing in any of the following ways:
(a) if the disclosure of wrongdoing relates to a Commonwealth agency—by
referring the disclosure or information to the agency for investigation;
(b) if the disclosure of wrongdoing relates to a matter that is within the
functions and powers of a government agency to investigate or
otherwise deal with—by referring the disclosure or information to the
agency for investigation or to be otherwise dealt with.40
1.39 If the disclosure or information is referred to another government agency, the
Commissioner would be able to fulfil their whistleblower protection
responsibilities by:
 monitoring how the agency investigates or deals with the disclosure or
information;
 providing advice, guidance or assistance to the person who made the
disclosure or provided the information; and
 providing advice, guidance or assistance to the agency in how it investigates
or deals with the disclosure or information.41
1.40 The Commissioner would be able to investigate a whistleblower protection
issue either alone or jointly with another government agency that has the
appropriate functions or powers if the issue relates to a Commonwealth
agency.42
1.41 If a whistleblower protection issue relates to a Commonwealth agency, the
Commissioner would be able to refer the issue to the agency for investigation.
After doing so, the Commissioner would be able to choose to:
 manage the investigation;
 oversee the investigation; or
 neither manage nor oversee the investigation.43
1.42 When deciding on how to deal with a disclosure of wrongdoing or
whistleblower protection issue, the Commissioner would be required to have
regard to the:
 need to ensure those matters are fully investigated;
 rights and obligations of other agencies to investigate those matters;

Remedial functions and powers
1.51 The Commissioner would have the authority to make recommendations in a
report that follows an investigation or public inquiry that they consider
necessary to protect a person from, or compensate them for, detrimental actions
or omissions caused by the disclosure of wrongdoing.59
1.52 If the Commissioner is satisfied there is a public interest in so doing, they would
have the power to commence proceedings in a court or make applications to an
industrial, civil or administrative body in respect of a whistleblower protection
issue.60
1.53 The Commissioner would be able to mediate or arbitrate a dispute between
parties involved in a whistleblower protection issue if they are satisfied that
doing so would be in the public interest and the interests of all parties involved.61
1.54 If the Commissioner reasonably believes that a person has contravened a civil
remedy provision related to a whistleblowing protection responsibility, they
would be able to accept a written undertaking by the person. If the
Commissioner believes that person has contravened the undertaking, they
would be able to apply for a court order.62

Part 5: Whistleblower Protection Advisory Council
1.63 The Bill would establish the Whistleblower Protection Advisory Council
(the Council). It would have the following three functions:
(a) on its own initiative or at the request of the Commissioner, to provide
advice to the Commissioner in relation to the Commissioner's functions;
(b) on its own initiative, to provide advice to the Commissioner in relation
to the Authority's function; and
(c) at the request of the Minister, to provide advice to the Minister about

Chapter 2
Key issues
2.1 This chapter outlines key issues raised in evidence to the committee about the
Whistleblower Protection Authority Bill 2025 (Bill). Those issues include the:
 experiences of Australian whistleblowers;
 limitations of the existing whistleblower framework;
 functions of the Whistleblower Protection Authority (WPA);
 interaction the WPA would have with the Public Interest Disclosure (PID)
framework; and
 costs associated with the establishment and operation of the WPA.
2.2 The chapter also articulates the committee's views and recommendations in
relation to the Bill.
Experiences of Australian whistleblowers
2.3 The committee received several accounts from whistleblowers who shared their
firsthand experience of the existing whistleblower framework. Not all
whistleblowers were comfortable publicly sharing their experiences which, for
the most part, were not positive.
2.4 One whistleblower who reported corporate wrongdoing informed the
committee that 'the decision to speak up is a profoundly lonely, terrifying, and
isolating ordeal'.1
2.5 The whistleblower identified one shortcoming with the Bill related to how a
private sector whistleblower may understand the protections afforded to them.
The whistleblower explained:
When a whistleblower reads this Bill, they will be making the hard decision
of whether they should speak up—and fearful of the consequences if they
do. Not only for themselves, but for their family. They are looking for
certainty and reassurance—and they need to see that support in writing.
They need the comfort of knowing they are explicitly included—not as a
secondary category, not as an afterthought, and certainly not as an "other
person," but as a full and equal participant in the national framework for
truth-telling and protection.2

Purpose of Whistleblower Laws
1.1 Public interest disclosures, commonly referred to as whistleblower laws, aim to:
 Promote integrity within institutions;
 Provide pathways for disclosures and appropriate responses;
 Protect individuals making disclosures from adverse consequences; and
 Ensure disclosures are made lawfully through established legal
mechanisms.
1.2 Existing whistleblower laws in Australia have come under significant criticism
in recent years.
Challenges in Public Sector
1.3 The Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (PID Act), which governs whistleblower
protections in the Commonwealth public sector, has been widely criticised for
its complexity and lack of clarity. Despite amendments introduced in 2023, the
legislation remains difficult to interpret and apply, both for legal professionals
and public servants.
1.4 Justice Griffiths of the Federal Court described the PID Act as containing
‘complex interlocking substantive provisions and definitions,’ and noted that it
is largely impenetrable even for experienced legal practitioners.1 This sentiment
is echoed by the Commonwealth Ombudsman, who reported that the PID Act
is still not well understood across the public sector.2
1.5 Key challenges include:
 Ambiguity in roles and responsibilities: There is confusion about whether
disclosures should be managed internally within agencies, by the
Ombudsman, or by other oversight bodies such as the Inspector-General of
Intelligence and Security.
 Unclear thresholds for valid disclosures: Many disclosures fail to meet the
legislative criteria required to trigger protections under the PID Act. In the
2023–24 financial year, nearly twice as many disclosures were rejected (444)
as were accepted (243).
 Resource intensity: Managing disclosures under the PID framework is time-
consuming and resource-intensive, adding administrative burden to
agencies.

2 Commonwealth Ombudsman, Submission 14, p. 2.

1.7 Whistleblower protections in the private sector are marked by fragmentation

Australians want a Whistleblower Protection Authority
1.49 Australians understand that whistleblowers protect the public interest. They
know that when corruption, abuse or misconduct goes unreported, it is ordinary
people who suffer, whether through failed services, wasted funds or lost trust
in government.
1.50 The overwhelming public support for a whistleblower protection authority
reflects this understanding. In polling conducted by the Australia Institute in
collaboration with the Human Rights Law Centre and the Whistleblower Justice
Fund, 84 per cent of Australians expressed support for the creation of a
whistleblower protection authority, with just five per cent opposed.27
1.51 Support is broad and not partisan. Voters across party lines, from Labor to
Liberal, from Greens to independents, want a system that protects people who
speak out for what's right.
1.52 This aligns with consistent public sentiment in Australia and abroad. Integrity
institutions enjoy strong and sustained public backing when their mandates are
clear and their operations are independent. Australians have supported anti-
corruption bodies, whistleblower protections and freedom of information laws
for decades.

A moment of opportunity
1.54 Presently, the Attorney-General's Department is reviewing the PID Act with a
view to making what has been described as 'Stage 2' reforms – more
comprehensive improvements to the public sector whistleblowing regime,
following initial, technical changes to coincide with the establishment of the
National Anti-Corruption Commission. This reform process has heard of
extensive support for a whistleblower protection authority. As the department
told the committee:
Of the 56 submissions received in response to the public consultation paper
on stage two of public sector whistleblowing reforms, 45 submissions
responded to the question about establishment of an independent
[whistleblower protection authority].28
1.55 Separately, from 1 July 2024, the Department of the Treasury (Treasury) has
been reviewing whistleblower protections in the private sector and the tax
sector, as required by the relevant legislation. The problem is that these
respective reviews are only required to consider their respective contexts. While
the Attorney-General's Department is reviewing public sector whistleblowing,
Treasury is reviewing private sector whistleblowing, and neither has the
mandate to consider whether, across the entirety of federal law, the nine
different whistleblowing regimes, a whistleblower protection authority is
needed. There is a risk the need for a whistleblower protection authority will fall
through this gap.
1.56 But these two reviews also provide a critical moment of opportunity. The most
effective model for a whistleblower protection authority would be one that is
established alongside comprehensive law reform. It is critical that the Attorney-
General's Department and Treasury work together to progress holistic rather
than siloed reform, with a whole-of-economy whistleblower protection
authority at the heart of reform.
Conclusion
1.57 For decades, Australia has failed its whistleblowers. We have passed laws that
look good on paper but provide little in practice. The result is that lives have
been ruined, corruption has been left unexposed, and public trust has been
eroded.

1.58 The evidence before this committee could not be clearer. Whistleblowers are
essential to the functioning of our democracy. They protect the public from
waste, abuse and corruption. Yet without real institutional support, they are left
isolated and punished for their courage.
1.59 Establishment of a whistleblower protection authority is not a luxury. It is the
missing piece in Australia's integrity framework. It would provide the
infrastructure of trust: a single, independent body with the power to support
whistleblowers, investigate reprisals, and enforce protections. It would return
more than it costs, both in financial savings and in public confidence.
1.60 This dissenting report has set out the moral, economic and democratic case for
a whistleblower protection authority. It is a case Parliament has known for
decades, but failed to act upon.
1.61 Yesterday, Australian Taxation Office (ATO) whistleblower Richard Boyle—
whose courageous whistleblowing led to reform to tax practices at the ATO—
was sentenced by the District Court in Adelaide, following an eight-year saga.
His case underscores the failings of Australia's current whistleblowing regime,
and the very real human toll of those shortcomings. In his speech at the 2024
Walkley Awards last year, Richard said:
What do we need to do to re-establish faith and confidence in our public
service, and our Commonwealth institutions? I proposed we need three vital
qualities: fairness, trust and transparency.29
1.62 The time for delay is over.
1.63 If the government is serious about integrity, about accountability, and about
restoring public trust, then it must support the establishment of a whistleblower
protection authority.
1.64 Anything less would be a betrayal of those who speak up, and of the Australian
public they seek to protect.
