Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 On 5 March 2026, the Senate referred the provisions of the Telecommunications
Legislation Amendment (Universal Outdoor Mobile Obligation) Bill 2025 (the
bill) to the Environment and Communications Legislation Committee (the
committee) for inquiry and report by 14 May 2026.1
1.2 The bill seeks to amend the Telecommunications (Consumer Protection and Service
Standards) Act 1999 (the Act) to establish a framework to create a Universal
Outdoor Mobile Obligation (UOMO). This framework would complement the
existing Universal Service Obligation (USO), which applies to fixed voice
services and payphones.2
Context and background to the bill
1.3 The Explanatory Memorandum states that the bill proposes ‘a significant and
important reform that will bring mobile services within the universal services
framework’. The Explanatory Memorandum explains:
Mobile telecommunications are essential to people in Australia, especially
in regional, rural and remote areas. Stakeholders have longstanding
concerns for public safety given gaps in terrestrial mobile coverage,
including on many regional and remote roads. In response, the UOMO will
require major mobile network operators, Telstra, Optus and TPG Telecom
(and any other providers designated in the future) to provide outdoor
baseline mobile coverage across Australia on an equitable basis. This reform
will benefit all Australians and in particular remote and regional
communities, by expanding baseline mobile coverage, which will in turn,
improve public safety by enabling connectivity to essential services and
Triple Zero.3
1.4 Mobile Network Operators—currently Telstra Limited (Telstra), Optus Mobile
(Optus) and TPG Telecom—will be expected to meet the UOMO using a mix of
their current terrestrial mobile coverage and Direct to Device (D2D)
technologies delivered over Low Earth Orbit Satellite platforms.4

1.5 The D2D technology is expected to enable delivery of baseline mobile services—
voice services and short message services (SMS)—where previously such
services were not commercially viable or, in some instances, feasible. The
Explanatory Memorandum acknowledges that the D2D technology and market
are still evolving and for these reasons the bill proposes a flexible framework:
D2D technology is evolving, with required infrastructure still being
deployed. At the same time, the market is developing quickly, with new
providers, and partnerships being forged around the world. Currently SMS
services are available in a number of countries on certain handsets,
including in Australia, and voice services are expected to follow.
Recognising this, the Bill creates a flexible framework which can be adjusted,
by disallowable legislative ministerial instrument, as the market develops
and satellite technology evolves.5
1.6 In 2023, the Commonwealth Government consulted on universal
telecommunications service delivery and funding arrangements given
emerging technologies and consumer preferences. The Department of
Infrastructure, Transport, Regional Development, Communications, Sport and
the Arts (the department) summarised the outcome of that consultation as
follows:
Overall, the consultation process demonstrated there is broad support for
change to universal service arrangements to better reflect evolving
consumer needs and the emergence of new alternative technologies. Many
stakeholders suggested a more flexible and technology-neutral approach
would be preferable, including to adopt modern networks and services that
are best suited to each premises, and to future-proof arrangements.6
1.7 In 2024, the triennial Regional Telecommunications Review also considered
reform options and recommended that, as a priority, the Australian
Government modernise the USO to reflect today’s digital realities.7

Communications (the minister), announced that the Commonwealth
Government would be establishing the UOMO.8 From September to October,
the department consulted on an Exposure Draft of the bill and reported:

Key provisions of the bill
1.9 The bill comprises one schedule of amendments: Part 1 sets out proposed
provisions in relation to the UOMO, and Part 2 would provide for mobile
telecommunications service standards, benchmarks and rules.
Establishment of the UOMO
1.10 Part 1 of Schedule 1 of the bill would amend three Commonwealth Acts, with
the key changes proposed in relation to the Act.10
1.11 This Part seeks to amend Part 2 of the Act, to incorporate the UOMO into the
existing universal service regime, to provide a range of new ministerial powers
and to designate Telstra, Optus and TPG Telecom as the primary universal
outdoor mobile providers (PUOMPs) from the ‘default date’ (currently, 1
December 2027).11
1.12 Proposed section 8BB of the Act defines the types of Designated Mobile
Telecommunications Services (DMTSs) that will be subject to the UOMO.
Proposed subsection 8BB(1) defines the initial DMTSs as voice and short
message service services that enable users to communicate with each other.
Proposed subsection 8BB(2) enables the minister to designate other services as
DMTS in the future, which would make these services subject to the UOMO
following consultation with existing PUOMPs as outlined in proposed
subsection 8BB(3).12
1.13 Proposed section 8D defines the area that will be covered by the UOMO as the
‘outdoor mobile coverage area’, which is defined as any geographical area
within Australia.13

Key issues
2.1 Most submitters and witnesses supported the intent of the Telecommunications
Legislation Amendment (Universal Outdoor Mobile Obligation) Bill 2025 (the
bill), with some concerns raised including:
 timing of implementation;
 standards, rules and benchmarks; and
 other matters
2.2 This chapter outlines support and the key issues raised before concluding with
the committee's views and recommendation.
Importance of establishing a Universal Outdoor Mobile Obligation
2.3 As explained in Chapter 1, proposed division 2A of the Telecommunications
(Consumer Protection and Service Standards) Act 1999 (the Act) would establish the
Universal Outdoor Mobile Obligation (UOMO) along with the ministerial
powers to set standards, rules and benchmarks relating to the UOMO.
2.4 The majority of inquiry participants supported the objective of the bill, to
establish a universal outdoor mobile obligation, with some individuals and
communities outlining their current and ongoing experiences with
telecommunications connectivity in regional, rural and remote areas.1 The
majority of submitters noted the need to improve mobile connectivity for
consumers.
2.5 As noted by the Department of Infrastructure, Transport, Regional
Development, Communications, Sport and the Arts (the department), there has
been broad support from stakeholders for the modernisation of universal
service obligations by using a technology neutral approach to better reflect the
evolving needs of rural Australia and the development of new
telecommunications technologies.2
2.6 Representatives from the National Rural Health Alliance stated their support
for the intent of the bill, and outlined the importance of mobile coverage in rural,
regional and remote communities and industries:
Outdoor mobile coverage in rural Australia is a lifeline. It underpins the
ability to call triple zero, to coordinate medical retrievals, to support

Comments on specific aspects of the bill
2.18 Information provided to the inquiry conveyed concerns about some aspects of
the bill. This section examines the following concerns:
 the timing of the commencement of the UOMO and concerns around
inadequate time for MNOs to prepare;
 standards, rules and benchmarks to be set by the minister; and
 other matters.
Timing of implementation
2.19 Proposed section 12M(1) of the bill would establish 1 December 2027 as the
‘default day’ on which the UOMO would commence.15
2.20 The explanatory memorandum to the bill states that Australia supports
international and domestic competition in both D2D and terrestrial mobile
service provision. However, it emphasised that the Australian Government’s
priority is to have the technology available, together with the necessary
safeguards, as quickly as possible:
While Australian MNOs had announced plans to commercially deploy D2D
services in partnership with LEOSat operators, this may not have delivered
baseline mobile coverage to all areas of Australia. The Bill will embed in law,
clear expectations that this significant technology should be as widely
available as possible, as quickly as possible and provide safeguard
mechanisms to ensure equitable and quality services are provided.16

Ministerial discretion
2.27 The Explanatory Memorandum highlighted that proposed subsection 12M(3)
would enable the minister to vary the ‘default day’ by postponing it by up to 12
months. This power can be exercised by the minister up to three times in order
to ‘provide flexibility to respond to the precise timing of D2D services becoming
available in Australia’.24 As such, the default day can be extended for a total of
period of up to 36 months (or three years).
2.28 In its submission, the department reiterated the flexibility of the default day as
outlined in proposed subsection 12M, noting that if the minister seeks to exercise
the power to vary the default day, that determination would have to follow
consultation with MNOs and consideration of technical and market issues.25
This would ensure that MNOs would be aware in advance of any changes to the
default day, as the minister would be in consultation with all of them prior to
any decision being made.
2.29 Additionally, the department noted the ability to split the UOMO into separate
voice and SMS obligations in order to ensure further flexibility to reflect the
readiness of technology and the telecommunications market, addressing the
concerns of several submitters who raised the issue of D2D voice services being
unfeasible to deliver by the current default day.26

Domestic mobile roaming
2.45 In evidence that was received by the committee, the issue of domestic mobile
roaming was raised by witnesses as an arrangement that could be an important
component of the UOMO. The Australian Competition and Consumer
Commission (ACCC) defines mobile roaming as a service that allows mobile
subscribers of one network to use their devices for calls, messaging and data
services when they are in areas that are outside the coverage area of the mobile
network area that they are subscribed to.41
2.46 In 2016, the ACCC reviewed the idea of mobile roaming arrangements,
presenting their final report on 23 October 2017. The ACCC concluded that it
would not declare a domestic mobile roaming service as they were not satisfied
that it would promote the long-term interests of consumers.42
2.47 The ACCC came to this conclusion for a number of reasons, including that
declaring a domestic mobile roaming service would not promote competition in
the retail mobile market. The ACCC argued that such a declaration would lessen
the competitive tension between MNOs and could possibly distort the long-
term competitive dynamics between the MNOs.43
2.48 On the other hand, the committee heard evidence that a domestic mobile
roaming arrangement could benefit rural, regional and remote communities,
especially in emergency or disaster situations. The NFF stated that there have
been instances where consumers from urban areas have visited remote
communities and found that they were unable to use mobile services as a
customer of a provider without local infrastructure.44
2.49 The ACCAN gave evidence of recent research that found there was widespread
support in communities for the introduction of a domestic roaming service, with
73 per cent of consumers supporting the proposal. ACCAN proposed that a
domestic mobile roaming arrangement could be an effective measure to meet
the UOMO obligations while the development of D2D technologies progresses.

Committee view
2.51 The committee acknowledges the overwhelming support from participants for
the establishment of the UOMO, and the importance of ensuring mobile
coverage is available across rural, regional and remote Australia.
2.52 The importance of modernising Australia’s universal service obligations by
using a technology neutral framework was recognised by many submitters. The
committee views the UOMO as an integral next step to deliver enhanced mobile
connectivity to the regions. However, the committee notes that the delivery of
this obligation is technology neutral, but submitters did indicate that it is only
likely to be deliverable if D2D and LEOSat technology is available.
2.53 The committee acknowledges the desire of some submitters for further
explanation and clarification surrounding some of the key definitions relating
to the UOMO, including the term ‘reasonably available’. The committee is
satisfied that definitions of reasonable availability and equitable access will be
outlined by the minister in legislative instruments following consultation with
MNOs.
2.54 The committee also recognises the concerns surrounding the default day on
which the UOMO would commence, noting that MNOs may not be in a position
to deliver these services by this time due to technological limitations and
availability of infrastructure. However, the committee is satisfied that the bill
provides the minister with the power to vary the default day by up to 12 months
on three occasions, with consultation being undertaken with MNOs before such
a decision would be made. The committee notes that the ability for the minister
to extend for up to 36 months or three years provides flexibility to industry and
stakeholders ensuring that the UOMO can commence at an appropriate time
with MNOs being ready to meet their obligations.
2.55 The committee notes that concerns were raised by some participants regarding
domestic mobile roaming services not being included as part of the UOMO. The
committee notes the reviews of this matter that have been undertaken by the
ACCC, and the conclusions that they drew regarding the decision to not make
a declaration on the matter. The committee also notes that the department is

coverage is extended to areas of rural, regional and remote Australia which
currently lack adequate coverage. The bill accounts for further development of
telecommunications technology to ensure that Australians can access effective
and reliable mobile services into the future.

Introduction
1.1 Improving mobile connectivity for Australians living, working and travelling in
regional, rural and remote areas is crucially important. Access to reliable
telecommunications is not a luxury but a critical service which underpins safety,
economic activity and social participation.
1.2 While the intention of the Telecommunications Legislation Amendment
(Universal Outdoor Mobile Obligation) Bill 2025 (the Bill) is commendable, the
Bill is fundamentally flawed in its design, lacks sufficient clarity, and fails to
provide a credible or timely pathway to achieving its stated objectives.
Labor’s shock axing of regional connectivity funding
1.3 In the 2026-27 Budget handed down just two days ago, the Albanese
Government has shockingly and cynically axed its Better Connectivity Plan for
Regional and Rural Australia (BCP). The BCP included future rounds of the
Mobile Black Spot Program, the Regional Connectivity Program, the On Farm
Connectivity Program, disaster and telecommunications resilience measures,
the Regional Tech Hub and the National Audit of Mobile Coverage.
1.4 The Government had allocated $116 million to the BCP in 2026-27 with $49.6
million allocated in the forward estimates.1 The ‘reprioritisation’ of this funding
shows the Government’s contempt for the communications infrastructure needs
of regional, rural, and remote Australia and is profoundly disappointing.
Lack of legislative clarity
1.5 The Bill establishes a broad and aspirational framework, rather than a clear and
enforceable regime.
1.6 At its core, the Bill requires that mobile services be made “reasonably available”
outdoors on an “equitable basis”, yet these key terms are not defined in primary
legislation. The absence of clear definitions creates significant uncertainty for
industry, consumers, and regional communities as to what level of mobile
connectivity they can expect.
Overreliance on delegated powers
1.7 The Bill confers broad powers on the Minister for Communications (Minister) to
determine critical elements of the universal outdoor mobile obligation,

1.9 There is the risk that this uncertainty will deter investment and innovation,

was switched off.
1.32 Mandatory domestic mobile roaming represents the most effective and
immediate step which can be taken to improve connectivity across regional

1.33 Without significant amendment, the Bill risks becoming an aspirational
framework full of motherhood statements which fails to deliver meaningful and
practical improvements in connectivity. Regional Australians deserve better.
