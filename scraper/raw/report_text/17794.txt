Chapter 1
Introduction
Purpose of the bill
1.1 The Universities Accord (Opening the Doors of Opportunity) Bill 2026 (the bill)
seeks to amend the Higher Education Support Act 2003 (HESA), the Australian
Tertiary Education Commission Act 2026 (ATEC Act), the Education Services for
Overseas Students Act 2000 (ESOS Act), the Tertiary Education Quality and
Standards Agency Act 2011 (TEQSA Act), and the Ombudsman Act 1976
(Ombudsman Act) to implement changes recommended by the Australian
Universities Accord Final Report by:
 embedding the new Managed Growth Funding System to support growth
in domestic students, including those from low socioeconomic and regional
and remote backgrounds;
 establishing demand driven Needs-based Funding to support students from
low socioeconomic backgrounds, First Nations students, and students
studying at regional campuses; and
 formalising the role of the Australian Tertiary Education Commission
(ATEC) in allocating Commonwealth supported places for higher education
courses to higher education providers, and international student places
where the Minister for Education (the Minister) makes a determination.1
1.2 In his second reading speech, the Hon Jason Clare MP, the Minister for
Education (Minister Clare), highlighted that this bill 'is about managing growth
and building a bigger and better and fairer system' and it will ensure that 'more
young people from poor families and from the regions and the bush get a crack
at university and that they get the support they need when they get there'.2

reference identified seven key areas for review:

 access and opportunity;
 investment and affordability;

Current university funding model
1.5 The Commonwealth Grants Scheme (CGS) is the largest higher education
funding program in terms of annual Australian Government (the government)
expenditure and subsidises course fees for eligible higher education students.
The subsidised place for eligible students is known as a Commonwealth
Support Place (CSP).7
1.6 Higher education providers receive two payments related to each projected
CSP—a Commonwealth contribution paid by the government through the CGS,
and a student contribution, paid for by the student, usually deferred through
HECS-HELP. Together, these payments make up 'base funding' for higher
education.8

components:
 specific allocations of CSPs for designated courses;
 uncapped funding for demand driven courses; and

Designated courses

1.10 As defined under subclause 1(1) of Schedule 1 of HESA, a demand driven course

bachelor's degree or bachelor's honours degree. There is no maximum funding

specifically determine how many CSPs are funded each year; rather, the Table
A provider determines the mix of courses offered up to the MBGA, and the
number of students funded under a given MBGA can vary considerably.13

Australian Tertiary Education Commission
1.12 The Australian Tertiary Education Commission (the ATEC), established under
the ATEC Act, is an independent steward for the higher education system and
is 'tasked with creating a better and fairer education system'.15 The ATEC's key
functions are to:
 enter into mission-based compacts;
 provide advice to government; and
 report on the state of the higher education system.16
1.13 The ATEC also has a key priority of supporting the government to reach its goal
of 80 per cent of working aged Australians completing a tertiary qualification
by 2050.17
1.14 Other work undertaken by the ATEC includes widening participation and
attainment of tertiary education for students from disadvantaged backgrounds
and regions, providing advice to the Minster for Education and the
Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency on the Higher Education
Standards Framework, and progressing work towards a joined-up tertiary
system between vocational education and training and higher education.18
1.15 Pending the passage of the bill, the ATEC will have responsibility for allocating
domestic student places under the Managed Growth Funding System, as well
as allocating international student places, subject to the Minister's
determination.19

Demand-driven Needs-based Funding
1.19 From 1 January 2026, Needs-based Funding has been provided to universities
to support students from low socio-economic backgrounds, First Nations
students and students studying at regional campuses to successfully complete
their university degrees.22
1.20 Under this program, universities are provided with additional per-student
funding contributions over and above core CSP funding. The funding is also
provided to help address the higher costs that regional higher education
providers face to deliver courses in regional Australia.23 Each university's
Needs-based Funding allocation is determined by a formula based on the
eligible volume of students from a low socio-economic background, First
Nations students and students studying at a regional campus in a CSP.24
1.21 The Needs-based Funding program is considered to be in a transition year for
2026, with plans to enshrine Needs-based Funding as a permanent feature of

Consultation on the Managed Growth Funding System
1.22 In June 2024, the government released a consultation paper setting out the
proposed new Managed Growth Funding System, which included the
following:
 a system-wide pool of Commonwealth supported places for the sector as a
whole to be set by the government;
 Managed Growth Targets (MGTs) for allocating places to Table A and
non-Table A providers;
 managed demand-driven funding for equity students from under-
represented backgrounds for Table A providers; and
 transition arrangements to smooth the impact of the end of the Higher
Education Continuity Guarantee from 1 January 2026 to support
institutional sustainability.26
Key provisions of the bill
1.23 The following section provides a summary of the key aspects of the bill
including the proposed implementation of a Managed Growth Funding System,
Needs-based Funding, and the allocation of international student places.
Managed Growth Funding System
1.24 Part 1 of Schedule 1 of the bill seeks to amend HESA to change the basis of
university funding from the setting of a MBGA to a managed growth funding
system based on the total number of equivalent full-time student load places.
According to the Explanatory Memorandum (EM), these amendments would
give the ATEC the ability to 'play a more active role' in managing how funding
is provided in relation to CSP students, including the ability to allocate CSPs to
each higher education provider from a total allocation pool set by the Minister.27
1.25 Part 1 of Schedule 1 of the bill also seeks to make amendments that would
introduce a disincentive for higher education providers that enrol students
above their domestic student profile.28
Determination of places available for allocation
1.26 The Minister, by legislative instrument, would be required to make a 'total
allocation pool determination' which would specify the total number of CSPs
that may be allocated by the ATEC for higher education courses, for a specified
year, to higher education providers.29

Allocation of places for higher education courses
1.28 Once the Minister has made their total allocation pool determination, the bill
provides for the ATEC to be able to allocate places for higher education courses
to higher education providers with the total number of CSPs allocated to a
provider to be known as their 'domestic student profile'.31 The domestic student
profile would be made up of up to two components — the core student load and
an additional growth allocation.32 The domestic student profiles set by the ATEC
would be required to be published on the ATEC's website to 'ensure
transparency across the system'.33
1.29 For 2027, the core student load for each provider would be set by an ATEC
determination.34 For 2028 and onwards, the core student load for each provider
would be the lower of:
(a) the provider's previous year's domestic student profile; or
(b) the number of CSPs in the most recent year for which there is verified
student data.35
1.30 On 20 August 2026, the House of Representatives passed government
amendments to the bill to clarify that the ATEC must allocate CSPs to Table A
providers that are equivalent to that provider's core student load.36
1.31 The bill also provides for the ATEC to allocate additional places to higher
education providers, which would be considered additional growth allocations.
According to the EM, the ATEC, when considering whether to allocate
additional places, would discuss and negotiate with the providers and would
also consider various factors including government strategic priorities, student
demand, providers' performance, goals and missions, and sector sustainability.37

Needs-based funding for equity and regional cohorts
1.39 Part 2 of Schedule 1 of the bill seeks to amend HESA for the provision of
Needs-based Funding grants from the Commonwealth to higher education
providers who enrol eligible equity students (students from low socioeconomic
backgrounds and First Nations students) and students studying at campuses in
regional or remote areas.47 Currently Needs-based Funding operates in a
transitional status, and the bill seeks to enshrine Needs-based Funding in
legislation.
Eligible equity students
1.40 The bill defines an eligible equity student, for the purposes of Needs-based
Funding, as either an Indigenous person, a person who is from a low
socioeconomic background at the time of enrolment, or a person who is included
in a class of persons specified in the Needs-based Funding Grants Guidelines.
The bill provides for the Minister to specify additional classes of eligible equity
students through the Needs-based Funding Grants Guidelines, which the EM
states is 'necessary and appropriate' to provide flexibility to add new cohorts
and impose additional requirements as the program evolves over time.48

Funding for eligible equity students
1.41 The bill sets out that the funding rate for eligible equity students, defined as the
'base equity amount', would be $1,535 and would be indexed annually to the
Consumer Price Index (CPI).49
1.42 On top of the base equity amount, the bill provides for a loading called an 'equity
modifier amount', which would be set by the Needs-based Funding Grants
Guidelines. The equity modifier amount would be based on a number of criteria,
including the academic preparedness of a non-Indigenous students. The EM
explains that differentiating the contribution amounts based on levels of
academic preparedness is intended to recognise its influence on success and
ensure that the funding received by the provider is commensurate with the
needs of the student. For Indigenous students, a distinction would be made
based on socioeconomic background.50
Regional component
1.43 The regional component of the Needs-based Funding is intended to provide
funding to higher education providers to assist them with offsetting the higher
cost of operating campuses located in regional or remote Australia.51
1.44 To be eligible, a campus must be located in a 'high priority' area. A high priority
area is defined in the bill as an area that meets the following criteria:

 an area specified in the Needs-based Funding Grants Guidelines.52

Overseas students
1.48 Schedule 2 of the bill seeks to amend the ATEC Act to establish a framework for
the ATEC to exercise its existing functions to allocate international student
places to ESOS-registered higher education providers, once the international
allocation pool determination has been made by the Minister.56
1.49 The bill seeks to establish the Minister's power to determine the number of
international students that may commence a course of education with
ESOS-registered higher education providers during a specified period, by
legislative instrument. This would be known as the 'international allocation pool
determination'.57
1.50 The bill outlines that, once the Minister has set the international allocation pool
determination, there are certain matters that the ATEC must comply with and
consider when making an allocation to an ESOS-registered higher education
provider. For example, the ATEC would be required to consult with the
ESOS-registered higher education provider before making an international
student allocation. In consulting with the provider, the ATEC would be required
to consider a number of factors, including the provider's performance against
its mission based compact.58 The ATEC could also be required to take into
account a provider's alignment to government priorities, such as source market
diversification, and contribution to skills needs.59
1.51 While the international allocation pool determination would be implemented by
legislative instrument, it would not be subject to disallowance by the Senate or
the House of Representatives.60 According to the EM, it would not be
appropriate for a determination to be subject to disallowance 'as it may cause
uncertainty for the operations and functions of ESOS registered higher
education providers, as the determination is to be relied upon from the date it
takes effect'.61

Varying an international student allocation
1.52 The bill includes provisions that would allow the ATEC to vary an international
student allocation at any time before the end of the period for which the
allocation relates to. According to the EM, this would provide the ATEC with
the ability to respond to changing circumstances throughout the year and to
ensure that allocations reflect student demand.62
Other minor amendments
1.53 The bill also includes minor amendments to the Ombudsman Act, the TEQSA
Act and the ESOS Act related to information sharing with the ATEC to inform
the ATEC's work as appropriate.63
Financial implications

Chapter 2
Views on the bill
2.1 The Australian Universities Accord (the Accord) review found that significant
changes are needed in Australian higher education to 'produce the skills,
knowledge and intellectual ambition' needed to meet Australia's current and
emerging social, economic and environmental challenges.1 The Accord
proposed ambitious targets such as recommending a tertiary attainment target
of 80 per cent by 2050. To achieve this Australia will need to lift the attainment
of historically underrepresented cohorts, such as those from low-socioeconomic
(low-SES) and regional and remote backgrounds and First Nations people.2
Consequently, the Accord recommended the introduction of a needs-based
funding model.3
2.2 The Accord also found that the current university funding model is providing
growth in unplanned, unmanaged and under-funded ways.4 To address this,
the Accord recommended adopting a managed growth funding model for
higher education that is planned and managed by the Australian Tertiary
Education Commission (the ATEC).5
2.3 The Universities Accord (Opening the Doors of Opportunity) Bill 2026 (the bill)
directly responds to the recommendations and findings of the Accord by
enshrining a Needs-based Funding model in legislation to help support students
from low-SES, First Nations students and those studying at regional campuses
to get into university and complete their studies. The bill also embeds the new
Managed Growth Funding System to support growth in domestic students and
formalises the role of the ATEC in allocating Commonwealth Supported Places
(CSPs) and international student places.6

General views on the bill
2.6 There was broad support among participants for the intent of the bill to ensure
that more Australians can access and succeed in higher education regardless of
where they live or their backgrounds.7 For example, James Cook University
(JCU) strongly supported the passage of the bill and welcomed the Australian
Government's (the government) commitment to expanding opportunity

likewise expressed strong support for the government's goal of increasing
tertiary attainment to at least 80 per cent by 2050.9

highlighted their support for the bill's intent to widen access to higher

broad support for the bill and its policy intent to align university provision and
Australia's workforce needs.11
2.8 Charles Sturt University (CSU), which is Australia's largest regional university,

living in regional areas and those in metropolitan centres'.12

educational disadvantage.47 Likewise, Western Sydney University (WSU)

Coalition Senators' additional comments
1.1 Coalition Senators thank all those who contributed to this inquiry, and
acknowledge the effort made by representative and peak bodies, higher
education providers, higher education experts, and others in assisting the
committee's deliberations.
1.2 Coalition Senators support a higher education system that rewards student
aspiration, respects student choice, and supports genuine regional provision of
higher education. The Coalition considers that Australian Government (the
government) should exercise clear and accountable control over migration and
deliver value for taxpayers. Universities should be encouraged to teach well,
retain their students and respond to demand. Independent providers should be
treated fairly.
1.3 Those principles inform our approach to this bill. There are aspects of this bill
which align well with these principles, and aspects which do not.
International students
1.4 One of the core features of this bill is to significantly expand the Minister for
Education's (the Minister) power to control the number and allocation of
international students in the higher education sector.
1.5 This attempt to bring international student migration under control through a
clear statutory framework is a constructive change.
1.6 It is not controversial to say that management of the rate of migration is now
one of the central challenges Australia is facing today. All major parties
recognise that the current levels of migration are unsustainably high, that net
overseas migration must come down, and that international education must
play its part.
1.7 That this bill in effect transitions the international education system from an
uncapped to a capped system—with significant ministerial powers to guide the
allocation and distribution of those students—has the potential to generate
benefits beyond the university sector.
1.8 The powers are extensive and give the government of the day a granular level
of control. The Minister may:
 determine the number of commencements for a specified period;
 prescribe matters with which the Australian Tertiary Education
Commission (the ATEC) must comply;
 identify the kinds of providers eligible for allocations;
 require consideration of specified matters;
 permits particular classes of overseas students to be excluded from the
count for particular classes of providers.
41
42
1.9 This creates considerable scope to differentiate the treatment of providers and
student cohorts within the national framework. These powers allow
provider-level differentiation, alignment of the student intake with Australia's
long-term needs and interest and adjustment of the cohort mix in a way that is
presently possible under the migration system.
1.10 Ministerial influence continues after initial allocations. Section 46E allows the
Minister to direct the ATEC to consider varying an allocation, specify matters it
must consider, and prescribe requirements it must comply with if it makes the
variation. The ATEC must keep total allocations within the ministerially
determined pool. The pool determination and its variation are exempt from
parliamentary disallowance. There is more that could be done. In particular,
independent providers could be put on the same footing as Table A and Table B
providers with respect to consultation on international student allocations.
However, taken together, the provisions establish a powerful and adaptable
instrument for shaping international education.
1.11 It is surprising that the government has downplayed this feature of the bill.
For Coalition Senators, the capacity to manage both the overall scale and the
distribution of international students is a substantive policy benefit.

Student choice
1.26 There is no real question that this legislation reduces student choice.
The evidence received by the Committee was clear and consistent on this point.
It has been echoed elsewhere: at the National Press Club on 26 August 2026,
Universities Australia Chair, Professor Carolyn Evans, stated that '[t]his current
legislation absolutely does reduce student choice.'5 The reasons why the bill
reduces student choice are worth briefly explaining.
1.27 The funding for each Commonwealth Supported Place (CSP) at an Australian
university or higher education provider is made up of two components. The first
is the Commonwealth contribution. The second is the student contribution.
1.28 At its simplest level, over-enrolment occurs where a provider offers more places
than its government funding allocation covers. 'Under-enrolment' is the
opposite.
1.29 There are good reasons for permitting over-enrolment:
 for the taxpayer, it means educating more students for the same amount of
taxpayer money;
 for the university, it means being able to respond to student demand, and
that there is flexibility to respond to factors beyond the university's control
(such as a sudden improvement in retention rates which drive up the size of
the student cohort); and
 for the student, it means they are more likely to be able to study at the
university of their first preference.
1.30 Providing a university can deliver an appropriate standard and quality of
instruction, over-enrolment can serve a valuable purpose.
1.31 However, this bill imposes financial penalties on providers who exceed their
allocated number of CSPs, beyond a small, and in some cases, arbitrary buffer.
These penalties are set out in new sections 33-38 to 33-39A of the HESA. The
Minister's stated reason for imposing these penalties is to end competition
between providers to attract students which, in his view, could put overall
system stability at risk.
1.32 Universities will respond to these penalties. They create an incentive to hold
places back. The obvious response will be to reduce and be more careful with
their admissions—particularly first-round offers—so as to avoid accidental
over-enrolments. Universities will increase ATAR thresholds and lift rejection
rates to avoid the financial penalties. This is contrary to the stated intention of
the bill, which is to grow the higher education system.
1.33 Worse, the penalties can actually penalise providers for improving their
performance. The evidence received by the Committee from the University of

a university could slip into 'over-enrolment' territory simply by improving its

1.34 For taxpayers, the situation raised genuine concerns about productivity and the
efficiency of government spending. Mr Andrew Norton made clear in oral
evidence that, as presently drafted, the over-enrolment penalties would require

1.36 It is students who will pay the price. Fewer students will be offered places at

then happily move to their second or third option, eventually trickling through
the system to meet the allocations determined by the ATEC.
1.37 These provisions of the bill should be amended. The over-enrolment caps
should be softened or wound back, and the government should give

altogether.

inappropriate moral and emotional connotations. It suggests that a university is
doing something wrong, rather than responding to demand. It carries
imputations about quality that are not necessarily borne out by the evidence.
The comparison between a 'fully funded' place and a 'marginally funded' place
appears to be designed to make that imputation.

Ministerial overreach
1.41 Coalition Senators are concerned by the obvious overreach in Ministerial
powers in proposed section 36-65 of the HESA.
1.42 As introduced, this was a power that would have allowed the Minister to
impose any condition on any provider that accepted CSPs, with which the
provider would then be required by law to comply. Such a condition could
relate to any condition whatsoever. It could relate to internal university policies,
industrial relations matters, or other matters affecting the provider's autonomy
and integrity. It could be imposed at any time, without consultation, in relation
to any matter whatsoever, and potentially retrospectively. It is not reviewable
and not disallowable, and as initially introduced was not even required to be
published. The provider would face potential funding consequences if it did not
comply.
1.43 This is not an appropriate power to be exercised outside of tight guardrails.
1.44 After introducing the bill, the government amended this provision to remove
industrial relations matters from scope, and otherwise to impose course
restrictions dealt with in other parts of the Act. It also requires the conditions to
be published.
1.45 Beyond this narrow qualification, the House of Representatives (the House)
amendments leave the Minister's power to impose provider-specific, non-
disallowable conditions intact.
1.46 The government now suggests the power (as amended) is broadly in line with
existing conditions in the HESA. This suggestion should not be accepted at face
value. At present, section 36-65 of the HESA simply requires providers to
comply with conditions in a funding agreement. That requirement—that there
be an agreement—puts the existing power in an entirely different category.
1.47 Agreement and unilateral imposition are not interchangeable concepts.
Describing the change as 'like-for-like', as the Department of Education did at
the hearing,8 obscures the fact that the government is removing the requirement
for an agreement. It is a substantive change in the legal relationship.

1.48 Agreement requires negotiation, consultation and warning. In practice, it
prevents conditions that are retrospective. It requires the provider actually to

Transparency
1.49 The bill requires the ATEC to allocate CSPs for domestic students to universities
and other higher education providers. It requires the ATEC to publish a number
for each such provider. What it does not require is for ATEC to publish the
criteria by which it arrived at that number.
1.50 The Coalition did not support the ATEC, noting that it was duplicative and
added to already excessive layers of bureaucracy. Regardless, in an environment
where places are allocated by bureaucratic decision, the Australian Parliament
(the Parliament) should be in a position to whether those places are being used
effectively, whether the allocations respond to unmet demand, and whether the
system as a whole is becoming more efficient—or simply more redistributive.
1.51 With those factors in mind, there are clear public policy benefits to transparency
measures that require the publication of criteria by which allocations are made.
Doing so increases scrutiny and limits the room for poor or perverse
decision-making. It is notable that the 2050 Alliance specifically proposed
amendments to s 30-50 of the HESA to require annual publication of the criteria
used to prioritise allocations.9 This would help clarify how institutional mission,
sustainability, and student demand influence the ATEC's decision. This is a
proposal that merits careful consideration.

1.83 The introduction of over enrolment penalties attracts serious concern across

prospective students.
