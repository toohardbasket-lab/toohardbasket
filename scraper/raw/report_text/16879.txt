Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 On 14 May 2026, the Senate referred the provisions of the Treasury Laws
Amendment (Business Registries Stabilisation and Uplift) Bill 2026 (the bill) to
the Senate Economics Legislation Committee (the committee) for inquiry and
report by 17 June 2026.
1.2 The first chapter of this report outlines the purpose and provisions of the bill.
Chapter 2 of the report outlines views on the bill, as well as providing the
committee view and recommendation.
Purpose and background of the bill
1.3 The bill makes changes to various pieces of legislation to strengthen Australian
business registers, including the operation and regulation of the Director ID
regime and the Australian Securities and Investments Commission’s (ASIC)
administration of those business registers.1
1.4 Schedules 2 and 3 of the bill (discussed further below), which relate to ASIC’s
powers and stabilising business registers, implement the government’s
response to the Review of the Modernising Business Registers Program (the
MBR Program) which was released on 28 August 2023.2 This review (also
discussed below) recommended the unwinding of the MBR Program due to a
variety of factors, including that the program’s costs would have outweighed its
economic benefits.3
Provisions of the bill
1.5 The bill contains three schedules which amend various pieces of legislation.
They are discussed in detail below.
Schedule 1
1.6 Schedule 1 amends the Corporations Act 2001 (the Corporations Act) to make
changes to the Director ID regime.
1.7 Director IDs are unique, lifelong identifiers which are issued to people
appointed as directors of companies or other relevant entities under the
Corporations Act or the Corporations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) Act
2001. The Director ID regime was introduced in 2021 as part of reforms to

1.8 Schedule 1 of the bill will link Director IDs to ASIC’s company register as part

Schedule 2
1.12 Schedule 2 amends various pieces of legislation so that ASIC can continue to
administer existing business registers effectively. Currently, there are over 30
Commonwealth business registers which are administered by ASIC.13
1.13 According to the Explanatory Memorandum:
The integrity of Australian business registers is fundamental to effective
regulation, market confidence, and public trust. ASIC’s role as the national
corporate regulator relies on the accuracy and completeness of information
provided by companies and their officers. Inaccurate, misleading, or
incomplete information can undermine regulatory processes, facilitate
misconduct, and erode confidence in the system.14
1.14 The changes in the bill do the following:
 Give ASIC the power to deregister a company if it believes that information
provided by the company is misleading or inaccurate;
 Make the following changes to providing address and contact information
to ASIC:
− allows any officer of a company to have an alternative address for
registry purposes (officers must continue to provide their residential
address to ASIC in the first instance);
− companies can provide ASIC with an electronic address to receive
communications and notices from ASIC, in addition to the company’s
physical registered office;
− officers of a company must also provide ASIC with an electronic address
to facilitate communication with ASIC;
 Gives ASIC discretion to approve what manner or format it can receive a
document or other information;
 In relation to the registries that ASIC administers:
− ASIC will have the discretion to correct the information on those registers
it administers under the Corporations Act, the Business Names Registration
Act 2011, the National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009 and the
Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993;
− ASIC will be able to publish information on those registers if ASIC
reasonably believes such a public disclosure is within the public interest
and the benefits outweigh the risks;
− Similarly, ASIC will also be able to restrict or redact information if the
benefits of such a restriction outweigh the risks; and

Schedule 3
1.16 Schedule 3 contains amendments to a variety of Acts and gives effect to the
government’s policy to cease the MBR Program.17
1.17 Funding for the MBR Program commenced in 2019, with legislation enacting it
in June 2020. The purpose of the program was to ‘transform the company,
business and professional registry services of the Australian Government.’18 The
intent of the program was to create a centralised platform for over 30 business
and corporate registries to be administered by the ABRS within the ATO and
headed by a Registrar.19
1.18 The ABRS website was launched in October 2021, and Director ID was
successfully implemented in March 2022. Aside from this, the MBR Program has
not implemented any changes to existing registries and is considered ‘relatively
early in its lifecycle, with most of the delivery, cost and risk still lying ahead.’20
1.19 In February 2023, the government commissioned a review into the MBR
Program, the final report of which was released in July 2023. This review found
that the program going forward would have costs outweighing its benefits and
recommended cessation of the program.21 The government adopted this
recommendation and announced the cessation of the MBR Program and the
return of administration of business registers to ASIC in August 2023.22

Human rights implications
1.30 As at the time of writing, the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Human Rights
has not commented on the bill.
1.31 The Explanatory Memorandum includes a discussion of the human rights
implications of the bill. According to the Explanatory Memorandum, the
following human rights are engaged by the bill:
 The right to a fair hearing and trial and the presumption of innocence
(Article 14 and 15 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights
(ICCPR));
 The right to protection from arbitrary and unlawful interference with
privacy (Article 17 of the ICCPR); and
 The right to freedom of expression (Article 19 of the ICCPR).
Right to a fair hearing and trial and presumption of innocence
1.32 Articles 14 and 15 of the ICCPR set out the right of individuals to a fair trial and
hearing, including the presumption of innocence. Several sections of the bill
interact with this right:
 Items in Schedules 2 and 3 allow for merits review of certain administrative
decisions, such as Registrar decisions under the Corporations Act (schedule
3, part 1) and Ministers decisions to allow the use of restricted language in a
business name (schedule 3, part 1);31 and
 The bill creates strict liability offences including an offence for failure of a
director or company secretary to provide an alternative address for service
(schedule 2, items 5 and 9).32.
1.33 In relation to merits review, the items which relate to business names positively
engage with the right to a fair trial by providing a merits review of the Minister’s
decision. In relation to Registrar decisions under the Corporations Act, this also
allows merits review of administrative decisions and thus positively engages
with the right to a fair trial.33
1.34 Regarding the strict liability offence contained in the bill, the Explanatory
Memorandum states that the application of strict liability is appropriate as it
encourages compliance with providing ASIC with the information this agency
requires. It also notes that the penalty for the offence (being 30 penalty units) is
within the maximum amount recommended by the Guide to Framing
Commonwealth Offenses.34

1.35 The Explanatory Memorandum also makes the point that the application of a
strict liability offence, rather than an absolute liability offence, allows for the
defence of honest and reasonable mistake of fact, which maintains adequate
checks and balances for persons who may be accused of an offence under these
parts of the bill.35
1.36 As such the application of a strict liability offence in the bill is reasonable,
necessary and proportionate, and is compatible with human rights.36
Right to privacy
1.37 Article 17 of the ICCPR prohibits the unlawful or arbitrary interference with a
person’s privacy, family, home or correspondence. Schedules 1 and 2 of the bill
engage the right to privacy through:
 allowing the collection of director’s personal information (addresses and
electronic addresses) and government related identifiers such as the
Director ID (schedule 1, part 2 and schedule 2);
 allows ASIC, in certain circumstances, to disclose or redact information
provided to its registries, included addresses (schedule 2, parts 5 and 6);
 allows the Registrar to share information it holds in its registry with ASIC,
including personal information (schedule 1, part 4 and schedule 2, part 7);
and
 enables the Registrar and ASIC to correct information in its registries
(schedule 1, part 4 and schedule 2, part 4).37
1.38 The parts of the bill which allow for the collection and use of personal
information engage positively with the right to privacy, as it allows the relevant
regulator to collect information and publish it in a form which does not easily
identify the individual from that information.38
1.39 Regarding the disclosure of personal information, the bill requires that ASIC
balance the public purposes of improving regulation with considerations of
privacy, as well as other risks, when exercising this power. Powers in the bill
also enable ASIC to take measures to protect privacy through, for example,
redacting public access to personal information.39
1.40 In relation to provisions of the bill which allow the Registrar to share
information with ASIC:

Right to freedom of expression and information.
1.43 Article 19 of the ICCPR provides all individuals with a right to freedom of
expression, including the freedom to impart ideas and information. This right
may be limited on grounds of protection of national security and public order
and respect for the rights of others.43
1.44 According to the Explanatory Memorandum, the bill enhances this right by
‘improving avenues of communication between persons and ASIC, in particular
through electronic means.’44
1.45 As mentioned above, ASIC is also given the power to redact and limit access to
registry information where it reasonably believes that the benefit of that
limitation would outweigh the risks of that information being public. This
power is provided in order to protect personal safety, as well as public order
and security.45
1.46 The limitations on the right to freedom of expression and information contained
in the bill are therefore necessary, proportionate and reasonable, and are
compatible with human rights.46

Chapter 2
Views on the bill
2.1 This chapter summarises submitter views on the bill, as well as the committee’s
view and recommendation.
Support for the bill
2.2 Submitters to the inquiry were broadly supportive of the bill, with much of the
commentary focused on the Director ID regime.
2.3 The Australian Institute of Company Directors (AICD) was supportive of
linking Director ID to ASIC’s Companies Register, describing the changes in the
bill as ‘key to achieving the policy aims of the Director ID regime and
strengthening Register integrity.’1 The AICD was also in favour of the linkage of
Director IDs to service addresses through the Companies Register to public and
authenticated users.2
2.4 The Australian Finance Industry Association (AFIA) was also supportive of
linking Director ID to the Companies Register:
Business registers play a central role in credit underwriting, due diligence,
fraud prevention and ongoing compliance. The value of these registers
depends on the accuracy, timeliness and consistency of data. Reforms that
improve data integrity are therefore strongly supported.3
2.5 Similar views were expressed by the Governance Institute, which was
supportive of changes to the bill from the Exposure Draft to increase flexibility
by adding new definitions to terms such as ‘giving’ and ‘lodging’, as well as the
inclusion of technology neutrality in the bill. Examples include using the term
‘electronic address’ rather than ‘email’ and ‘notification’ rather than ‘written
notice’.4
2.6 The Governance Institute also made mention of the introduction in the bill of
amendments which removed the two-stage process to supress home addresses
on ASIC’s registers. The Governance Institute was supportive of the ability to
nominate alternative addresses for service of documents and other notifications,
as well as the grace period in the bill for newly appointed directors to obtain
and provide a Director ID number to ASIC. They noted that this amendment

Committee view
2.32 The committee notes the support for the bill and welcomes the opportunity the
bill provides to fully implement the Director ID system while also modernising
ASIC’s current registry powers.
2.33 The committee further acknowledges that this bill implements the roll-back of
the MBR Program and commends the government for this. The blow-out in costs
at this early point in the implementation for the MBR Program, combined with
the limited administrative and economic benefits it would provide, are clear
evidence that this program was not feasible. The committee commends the
government for making this decision.
2.34 The Director ID regime changes in the bill are welcomed by the committee.
ASIC’s submission to the inquiry is unequivocal, that these changes will deliver
the full intent of the Director ID regime, helping ASIC to target misconduct and
illegal behaviour of company directors. This is a highly important policy aim,
and one which the committee is fully supportive of.
2.35 The committee is cognisant of concerns raised by stakeholders around the
amount of information published on the Companies Register, and notes the
differing calls for less information and more information to be published on
these registers. The committee also notes the concerns about ASIC’s new

Australian Greens senators' additional comments
1.1 This Bill represents a small step forward for corporate transparency as it links
Director IDs to ASIC’s companies register, which makes it easier to trace
corporate identities.
1.2 The Bill introduces a new power under section 1274AC of the Corporations Act
for ASIC to restrict access to personal information based on the class of
information or the class of persons whose access is to be restricted via a
legislative instrument.
1.3 The Explanatory Memorandum indicates that ASIC could use this power to put
in place tiered access for people seeking to inspect certain information on the
companies register.1
1.4 The Greens support the recommendation in the joint submission provided by
Transparency International Australia and the Uniting Church in Australia,
Synod of Victoria and Tasmania (the joint submission) that ‘civil society actors
and organisations, including whistleblowers, investigative journalists,
procurement authorities, and researchers, should be given special access to full
director information, and subsequently, beneficial ownership information’.2
These organisations and individuals play an essential role in exposing corporate
malpractice, corruption and financial crime.
1.5 We also support the joint submissions recommendation that the month and year
of birth, nationality, and country of residence should be made public for
directors, in addition to the Director ID, which aligns with international best
practice.3 It is essential that public researchers, journalists and civil society
organisations have enough information to distinguish between directors with
the same name. It is also important to require directors to provide the same
information as beneficial owners, as many directors are beneficial owners, and
aligning the two systems reduces the ability of individuals to take advantage of
any inconsistency in reporting requirements.
