Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 The National Emergency Declaration Act 2020 (the Act) commenced operation on
16 December 2020. The Act provides a legislative framework for the declaration
of a national emergency by the Governor-General of the Commonwealth of
Australia (the Governor-General), on the advice of the Prime Minister of the
Commonwealth of Australia (the Prime Minister).1
1.2 Section 18 of the Act requires the Senate Standing Committee on Legal and
Constitutional Affairs (the committee) to conduct a review of the operation of
the Act immediately after it commences, and again by the fifth anniversary of
the Act’s commencement. 2
1.3 On 30 June 2021, the committee tabled its first review of the Act in the Senate.
On 6 November 2025, the committee commenced its second review of the Act,
in accordance with section 18. This is the report of the committee’s second
review of the Act.
Conduct of the review and acknowledgement
1.4 The committee advertised the review on the committee's website and called for
submissions by 27 February 2026. The committee also wrote to a number of
individuals and organisations, inviting submissions. The committee received
11 submissions, which are listed at Appendix 1 and are available on the
committee’s website at
https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Legal_a
nd_Constitutional_Affairs/NED48/Submissions.
1.5 The committee thanks those organisations and individuals who made
submissions to assist with this review.
1.6 The committee did not hold a public hearing for this inquiry.

Background and overview of the Act
1.8 The National Emergency Declaration Bill 2020 (the bill) and the National
Emergency Declaration (Consequential Amendments) Bill 2020 were
introduced to establish a legislative framework for declaring national
emergencies by the Governor-General on the advice of the Prime Minister.3
1.9 This followed the 2020 Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster
Arrangements (the Royal Commission), which considered Australia’s current
disaster outlook and identified that the Australian Government 'has unique
capabilities, and is able to take a broader view of the national consequences of
extreme to catastrophic disasters.’4
1.10 Specifically, the Royal Commission called for the provision of a Commonwealth
declaration of emergency:
Recommendation 5.1 Make provision for a declaration of a state of
emergency
The Australian Government should make provision, in legislation, for a
declaration of a state of national emergency. The declaration should include
the following components:
(1) the ability for the Australian Government to make a public declaration
to communicate the seriousness of a natural disaster
(2) processes to mobilise and activate Australian Government agencies
quickly to support states and territories to respond to and recover from a
natural disaster, and
(3) the power to take action without a state or territory request for assistance
in clearly defined and limited circumstances.5
1.11 When the bill was introduced, the then Attorney-General, the Hon. Christian
Porter, MP, advised it would implement the Royal Commission’s above
recommendation to provide a ‘unified framework for the use of Commonwealth
powers during major emergencies’.6 The Attorney-General explained that this
framework would allow a national emergency declaration to be made, which

1.12 The Explanatory Memorandum (EM) detailed the purpose of the bill to:
 empower ministers to suspend, vary or substitute “red tape” requirements
in legislation that they administer in certain circumstances; and
 enable the Prime Minister to require accountable authorities of

prepare for, respond to or support the recovery from the national
emergency.8
1.13 The bill passed the House of Representatives on 9 December 2020 and the Senate
on 10 December 2020. The Act commenced operation on 16 December 2020.
Overview of the Act
1.14 The Act contains four parts:
 Part 1 provides a simplified outline of the Act and clarifies that the Act
extends to Australia's external territories and offshore areas. It also contains
certain key definitions relevant to the Act including 'national emergency
law' and 'nationally significant harm’.
 Part 2 sets out the conditions for making a National Emergency Declaration
(NED) and the requirements and parameters of those declarations.
This includes the periods for which NEDs may be in force and the
circumstances in which the Governor-General may extend, vary or revoke a
NED. It also provides that a NED is a legislative instrument that is not
subject to disallowance and sets out the requirement for committee review
of each NED made, within one year of the anniversary of its declaration.
 Part 3 empowers ministers to modify certain administrative requirements
during a declared national emergency and identifies the laws that are
exempt from such modifications.
 Part 4 empowers the Prime Minister to require the provision of emergency
management information and outlines reporting requirements on the
exercise of powers under national emergency laws. This part also includes
the requirements for committee oversight in relation to the Act, detailed
below.

Legislated requirements for committee oversight
1.15 As set out above, section 18 of the Act requires the Senate Standing Committee

a resolution of the Senate to:
(a) begin a review of the operation of this Act immediately after this Act
commences and report the committee’s findings to the Senate by

(a) begin a review of the operation of this Act by the fifth anniversary of the

1.16 Additionally, section 14A requires the committee to:
(a) begin a review of each national emergency declaration made under section
11 by the first anniversary of the day the declaration is made; and
(b) report the committee’s findings to the Senate as soon as practicable after

Operation of the Act
1.17 The Act enables the Australian Government to support states and territories to
respond to a National Emergency, and does not exclude, interfere with or
override the primary responsibilities that states and territories have for
emergency management within their jurisdictions.9
1.18 A National Emergency can only be declared if the emergency meets certain
legislated thresholds and requirements under the Act, including if the Prime
Minister is satisfied that:
(a) an emergency has recently occurred, is occurring or is likely to occur, and
(b) the emergency has caused, is causing or is likely to cause nationally
significant harm in Australia or in an Australian offshore area, and
(c) for reasons relating to emergency management, it is desirable for the
declaration to be made for the purposes of one or more national emergency
laws, and
(d) any of the following applies:
(i) the affected state(s) and/or territory(ies) has/have requested in writing
a National Emergency declaration be made, or
(ii) it is not practicable for such a request to be made because of the
emergency, or
(iii) the emergency has affected, is affecting or is likely to affect
Commonwealth interests, or
9 Joint Agency, Submission 11, p. 2.

(iv) the making of the declaration is appropriate, having regard to the
nature of the emergency and the nature and severity of the nationally
significant harm.10
1.19 NEDs may be made for an initial period of up to three months, with the
possibility to extend the period for three-month blocks, on the advice of the
Prime Minister. The declaration enlivens emergency powers for the Australian
Government, which are designated ‘national emergency laws’ under the Act.
These emergency powers allow for the streamlining procedural ‘red tape’
requirements, and allows the Prime Minister to require accountable
Commonwealth authorities to provide specified information relating to the
emergency.11

National Emergency Management Agency (NEMA) was established on
1 September 2022 to support the operationalisation of a NED.12
1.21 Policy and operational matters relevant to the Act are provided by the
Department of Home Affairs, the Department of the Prime Minister and
Cabinet, and NEMA.13

Statutory review of NEDs made under the Act
1.25 To date, one NED has been declared by the Governor-General since the Act
commenced. It was declared on 11 March 2022 in response to the 2022 NSW
Floods, which resulted in significant flooding events in northern NSW
(the Northern Rivers region) and south-eastern Queensland.
1.26 No extension was made beyond the initial three-month declaration.
1.27 At the time, the Department of Home Affairs, PM&C and the NEMA described
these events as ‘the most severe in Australia’s history’, as millions of Australians
were impacted, including through the tragic loss of 21 lives. Further, the floods
caused significant damage to critical infrastructure in the region (including rail,
roads, power and telecommunications) commercial and residential property,
the agricultural sector and critical supply chains.
1.28 On 25 August 2022, the committee commenced a review of the National
Emergency (2022 New South Wales Floods) Declaration 2022 (the NSW Floods
Declaration), under section 14A of the Act. The committee received two
submissions to this review.
1.29 During the review, the committee considered whether the conditions required
for declaring a NED under the Act were satisfied.
1.30 Ultimately, the committee found that, on balance, the NED was made in
accordance with the requirements of the NED Act. However, the committee
identified several matters that ‘should receive more attention in future,
particularly as regards the provision of information to the committee’.
The committee also reiterated the importance of parliamentary oversight and
executive accountability for decisions made under the Act.

Chapter 2
Key issues
Overview of key issues
2.1 On balance, submissions to the review expressed support for the National
Emergency Declaration Act 2020 (the Act) and highlighted the importance of the
Act and the committee’s 5-year review to ensure the Act is fit for purpose.
2.2 Some concerns and suggestions for improvements to the Act were provided by
submitters, and are discussed in this chapter, including:
 the need for greater awareness of the NED framework;
 the absence of key definitions in the Act, including ‘emergency’;
 consultation requirements with state and territory governments;
 oversight of NEDs, including the exemption from disallowance; and
 the changing threat environment.
2.3 The committee’s view and recommendations are at the end of the chapter.
Awareness of the NED Framework
2.4 Submitters acknowledged the importance of the Act and expressed a need for
greater awareness of the framework and clarity regarding the role of the
Commonwealth, state and territory governments and local governments during
a declared national emergency.
2.5 For instance, the Australian Local Government Association (ALGA) stated that
during their consultations with state and territory local governments about the
NED framework, it became clear that many of them were not aware of the Act
or its operation. The ALGA suggested that this may be due to the limited
operation of the Act so far.1
2.6 To build greater awareness of the Act and provide clarity surrounding the roles
and responsibilities, the ALGA proposed that an ‘operational interface’ be
developed which clarifies roles, responsibilities and expectations during a
national emergency declaration. The ALGA recommended that such an
interface include:
 clear delineation of responsibilities across Commonwealth, state, and local
government during the period of a declaration, including clarity on which
level of government communicates key decisions or information to the
community;

 guidance on how Commonwealth assistance will integrate with state
emergency-management frameworks and recovery arrangements and
associated local government emergency management plans;
 information and data-sharing protocols between the Commonwealth, state
government and local governments, with clear processes for sharing
situation reports, impact assessments, and resource updates, including
agreed frequency and format;
 further clarification by the Commonwealth as to what elements of the NED
might apply, interest, or benefit local governments; and
 expected timeframes for the provision of additional national resourcing,
along with transparent guidance on when and how this assistance may be
scaled back or withdrawn.2
2.7 A similar point was made by the South Australian Government, stating that:
The Act (and any subordinate national framework) would benefit from
greater clarity on what thresholds, if any, state and territory governments

instance. It is currently unclear how the national emergency declaration fits

2.8 The South Australian Government suggested that this additional degree of

‘clarity on relationships, roles and responsibilities, set out clear processes and
thresholds, and identify what Commonwealth support and resources will
become available to states and territories during a declaration’.4
2.9 While the Joint Agencies’ submission did not specifically detail the measures
being taken to build awareness of the NED Framework, particularly with local
government, it reiterated that the Act does not:
… exclude, interfere with or override the primary responsibilities that states
and territories have for emergency management within their jurisdictions,
or the constitutional, legal and regulatory frameworks governing that

of government. While the committee appreciates this may be due to the Act

Definitions of key terms
2.12 Part 1 of the Act provides certain definitions for the purpose of interpreting and
implementing the provisions of the Act.
2.13 Submitters to the first review raised concerns about the absence of key
definitions like ‘emergency’ and ‘Commonwealth interest’. In this review,
submitters were particularly concerned about the absence of the definition of
‘emergency.’
2.14 According to the Australian Human Rights Commission (AHRC), the terms
‘emergency’ and ‘Commonwealth interests’ are critical to the scope of the Act
and the powers that may be exercised pursuant to it, including the declaration
or extension of a national emergency.6 As such, the lack of these definitions ‘has
the effect of providing a very broad discretionary power to declare, extend and
vary national emergency declarations under this Act.’7
2.15 From a local government perspective, the ALGA submitted:
Without a clear statutory definition, local governments are exposed to
inconsistent interpretations and the risk of the Act being applied in ways
that exceed its intended scope. This ambiguity creates uncertainty for
councils about when the legislation may be triggered, what obligations may
follow, and how responsibilities interact with state and territory
arrangements. A lack of definition also undermines national consistency,
particularly in multi-jurisdictional events where clarity is most needed.8
2.16 The South Australian Government shared these concerns, stating:
The omission of a definition for what constitutes an emergency (or disaster)
in the Act is at odds with established emergency management legislation in
all other states and territories.9

2.17 To address this, some submitters called for an inclusive definition of

… a clear, contemporary definition of what constitutes an emergency—
including the nature, scale and duration of events, and how they are

2.18 For greater consistency, the AHRC stated that the term ‘emergency’ should be
defined in similar terms to the definitions of ‘emergency’ and ‘disaster’ in the
emergency management legislation enacted in each State and Territory.11
The South Australian Government added that ‘while flexibility is an important
feature of emergency management arrangements, failure to clearly define what
constitutes an emergency in the Act invites uncertainty and ambiguity’ when
considering the need for a NED.12
2.19 The Joint Agencies explained that the framework was designed to reflect an
‘all-hazards approach’ to provide flexibility, and ‘ensure it is not unduly limited
to specific hazards’.13 This means that a NED may be declared ‘regardless of the
cause of the emergency (for instance bushfires, floods, tropical cyclones,
cyberattacks, terrorism, sabotage, etc).’14
2.20 A revised EM to the bill, issued in 2020, stated the rationale for the absence of a
definition for ‘emergency’:
Emergency is not defined, and instead takes its natural and ordinary
meaning, which supports the ‘all hazards’ approach adopted in the national
emergency declaration framework. This is important so as not to limit the
circumstances in which a declaration can be made to certain types or kinds
of defined emergencies. The Macquarie Dictionary defines emergency as an
unforeseen occurrence; a sudden and urgent occasion for action. The term
‘emergency’ is not intended to include predictable, ongoing or recurring
events such as drought or the effect of long term coastal erosion. The term is
also not intended to include protests or industrial action. These matters will
not constitute an emergency for the purposes of the Act.15

Committee view
2.22 The committee acknowledges the concerns raised by some submitters, including
the AHRC and the ALGA, about the decision not to include the definition of the
term ‘emergency’ in the Act.
2.23 The committee appreciates this issue was addressed in the joint submission
made by the Attorney-General’s Department, the Department of Home Affairs
and Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet (the departments) in the
committee’s first review of the NED Act:
The framework’s ‘all hazards’ approach ensures that the framework can be
adapted to a variety of emergencies, including those that are beyond current
contemplation and experience, as well as compounding or overlapping
emergencies. This flexibility is necessary and appropriate so as not to limit
the circumstances in which a declaration can be made in relation to certain
types or kinds of defined emergencies.17
2.24 This view was reiterated in the Joint Agencies submission to this review, further
explaining that this approach was intentionally taken to ensure NEDs are not
limited to specific hazards and may be declared ‘regardless of the cause of the
emergency’.18
2.25 While the committee acknowledges the desire for clarity, in this circumstance, it
accepts that a high degree of flexibility is needed for an effective emergency
management framework to ensure certain hazards or risks are not inadvertently
excluded. This is particularly important in Australia, where we have faced a
changing threat environment, as highlighted by submitters to this review.

Greater transparency regarding the consultation process
2.32 Some submitters raised concerns about a perceived lack of transparency
required under the Act regarding consultation and the decision-making process
leading to a NED being declared (or not declared). For example, one submission
argued:
While the consultation requirement is sound in principle, the Act does not
require disclosure of whether consultation occurred or why it was
impracticable where it did not. Over time, this creates a risk that
consultation becomes opaque, particularly in prolonged or repeated
declarations.26
2.33 The submitter suggested that the Act could be improved by requiring the tabling
of ‘a brief, factual consultation statement identifying whether states and
territories were consulted, which jurisdictions were consulted, and, where
consultation was not practicable, a written explanation of why.’27
2.34 The Australian Red Cross Society (Red Cross) also called for greater
transparency, explaining:
When a disaster occurs, the decision to declare or not declare a national
emergency must be grounded in a holistic understanding of impacts,
including social, economic, environmental and psychosocial dimensions.
Ensuring that this decision-making process is consistent, evidence-based
and communicated with clarity is essential for public confidence and will
facilitate a fast and just recovery. Greater transparency around the criteria
used, the assessment undertaken, and the rationale for decisions, especially
when the decision is taken to not declare a national emergency, would
support accountability and enhance community trust in government
processes.28
2.35 For these reasons, the Red Cross recommended that NED decisions be more
transparent, suggesting that a National Emergency Advisory Panel be
established to assist to ‘provide independent advice, facilitate real-time national
coordination, and deliver accurate information to decision-makers.’29

Committee view
2.36 The committee notes concerns raised by submitters about the risk of a NED
being declared without the relevant states and/or territories being consulted,
where the Prime Minister is satisfied that it is not practicable to do so.’30
2.37 In considering this issue, the committee recognises the primacy of the role that
states and territories have to respond to an emergency in their jurisdiction, and
the importance of consultation before actions to assist the situation are taken
under the Act.
2.38 However, the committee accepts the wording of the EM that the Act is intended
to require consultation with states and territories as its default position.
The committee considers given that future disasters are unforeseeable, this level
of flexibility is required.31
2.39 The committee is of the view that greater transparency, through the legislated
committee review process following a NED being declared, is a key step in this
process and may address the concerns highlighted by submitters in this regard.
Parliamentary oversight
2.40 Section 11(6) of the Act provides that a NED made pursuant to s 11(1) of the Act
is a legislative instrument, however it is not subject to disallowance under s 42
of the Legislation Act 2003 (Cth) (Legislation Act). Additionally, the Act provides
that any instruments varying or revoking a national emergency declaration are
also exempt from disallowance.
2.41 Submitters had previously raised concerns about the exemptions from
disallowance and the impact this would have on reducing parliamentary
oversight and scrutiny. Consequently, this issue was considered in detail in the
committee’s first review of the Act. At that time the committee recommended:
…that the Commonwealth government considers amending the National
Emergency Declaration Act 2020, through the removal of subsection 12(5),
so that any extension of a declaration beyond the three-month period is
subject to disallowance.32
2.42 As the exemptions from disallowance remain in the Act, the AHRC reiterated
its concern about the exemption applying to such significant matters of national
consequence in their submission to this review.

‘understand these concerns and recognise the importance of appropriate
oversight mechanisms in the NED Act’. The Joint Agency response set out that

Committee view
2.44 The committee acknowledges the longstanding concerns about the exemption
of national emergency declarations from disallowance.
2.45 The committee reiterates that declarations of a nationally significant emergency
must be accountable to the Parliament. The committee acknowledges that in the
absence of disallowance as a tool of parliamentary scrutiny, the Act requires the
committee to review the operation of the Act, and each NED made.
The committee takes this responsibility seriously and as such reiterates the
initial committee finding that national emergency declaration made under

Committee view
2.55 The National Emergency Declaration Act 2020 (the Act) was enacted in the wake
of significant emergencies which affected a large number of Australians,
including the COVID-19 pandemic and the 2019-20 Black Summer bushfires.
Since then, the nation has been impacted by other emergencies, including the
disastrous flooding event in 2022 which impacted New South Wales and
Queensland.
2.56 The changing threat environment in Australia has been at the forefront of the
committee’s mind during this review. It acknowledges the variety of actions
being taken by the Australian government to uplift emergency response
arrangements, in addition to the NED Framework.
2.57 The committee believes the flexibility built into the Act, and the ‘all hazards’
approach, should support a changing risk environment but nevertheless will
monitor this issue in future committee reviews to ensure the Act remains fit for
purpose.
Recommendations
2.58 As part of its second review of the Act and its operation, the committee makes
the following recommendations:
Recommendation 1
2.59 The committee recommends that the Australian Government continue to
build awareness of the National Emergency Declaration Act and Framework
with state, territory and local governments to ensure effective coordination
between all levels of government during an emergency.
Recommendation 2
2.60 The committee recommends that the Australian Government considers
amending the National Emergency Declaration Act 2020 so that national
emergency declarations made under section (11)(1) and extensions under
section 12(1) are subject to the usual parliamentary process.
