Chapter 1
Introduction
Referral of the inquiry
1.1 On 13 May 2026, the Senate agreed that the provisions of all time critical bills
introduced into the House of Representatives after 14 May 2026 and up to and
including 4 June 2026 that contain substantive provisions commencing on or
before 1 July 2026 (together with the provisions of any related bill), are referred
to standing committees for inquiry and report by 22 June 2026.1
1.2 On 28 May 2026, the provisions of the Treasury Laws Amendment (Tax Reform
No. 1) Bill 2026 (Treasury Laws Bill) and Income Tax Rates Amendment (Tax
Reform No. 1) Bill 2026 (Tax Rates Bill) (collectively, the bills) were referred to
the Economics Legislation Committee (the committee) for inquiry and report by
22 June 2026.
Purpose of the Treasury Laws Bill
1.3 The Treasurer, the Hon Dr Jim Chalmers MP, advised that the Treasury Laws
Bill delivers on three key objectives: it cuts taxes for every Australian worker; it
makes it easier for people to buy their first home; and it better aligns the tax
treatment of labour income and asset income.2
1.4 Dr Chalmers noted that the bills comprise the first tranche of legislation to
implement the significant tax reform package announced in the Federal Budget
2026–27. He foreshadowed further legislation on specific implementation details
and other parts of the tax reform package, subject to more detailed consideration
and stakeholder consultations.3
Key provisions of the Treasury Laws Bill
1.5 The Treasury Laws Bill comprises four schedules, each of which contains key
provisions as follows:
 Schedule 1—Capital Gains Tax (CGT) adjustments;
 Schedule 2—Limit negative gearing (NG) for residential property to new
builds;
 Schedule 3—Working Australians tax offset; and
 Schedule 4—Standard deduction for work-related expenses.

Schedule 1—CGT adjustments
1.6 Schedule 1 to the bills would amend the income tax law to:
 replace the 50 per cent CGT discount (CGTD) for individuals, trusts and
partnerships with cost base indexation to ensure only real gains are subject
to taxation;
 introduce a 30 per cent minimum tax on capital gains, with an exemption
for certain income support recipients, to ensure gains are subject to a tax
rate closer to the tax rate individuals faced during their working life and
commensurate with the tax rate paid by most workers; and
 apply the new arrangements prospectively to all capital gains accruing on
and after 1 July 2027, including gains accruing on pre-CGT assets, while
retaining access to the CGT discount to maintain support for new and
affordable housing, and maintaining existing CGT concessions for small
business.4
1.7 Schedule 1 to the bills partially implements the Tax reform—Boosting Home
Ownership—reforming negative gearing and capital gains tax measure in the 2026–
27 budget.5
Background and broader context for the amendments
1.8 Capital gains are the income earned from an increase in asset values over time
and, under the current rules, this form of income earned by individuals
(including individual partners in a partnership) and trusts attracts a 50 per cent
CGTD when the asset is sold—if the asset has been held for at least 12 months.6
1.9 The CGTD was introduced in 1999 following the Review of Business Taxation,
which said that the introduction of a generally more generous tax treatment of
capital gains would encourage investment in the Australian share market, spur
investors to buy and sell assets more frequently, and increase tax revenues. Prior
to this, capital gains were adjusted for inflation so that only the real component
was taxed.7
1.10 Since the introduction of the CGTD over 25 years ago, the share of Australians
receiving dividend income has fallen, while the share with investments in
property has grown significantly. The low effective tax rates on property
investments, caused by the interaction between the CGTD and negative

1.11 These incentives have led to higher house prices as investors bid up the price to

1.12 The EM suggests that the flat CGTD is 'arbitrary' and does not accurately

the EM highlights that, unlike most income, capital gains are taxed on
realisation—when the asset is sold—not when the gains accrue, and that this
creates opportunities for taxpayers to time the sale of assets for tax reasons.12
Who are the main beneficiaries of the CGTD?
1.13 The benefits of the CGTD predominantly flow to Australians who earn among
the highest incomes over their lives. The EM states that one-third of all capital
gains are realised by individuals who earn incomes in the highest one per cent
during their working lives and more than half of all gains are realised by the
highest 10 per cent.13
1.14 In its 2025–26 Tax Expenditures and Insights Statement (TEIS), the Treasury stated
that over 1.1 million individual tax filers realised a net capital gain in 2022–23
and that, of those, 71 per cent benefitted from the capital gains discount for
individuals and trusts. The Treasury reported that approximately 95 per cent of
the benefit was received by people with above median incomes, and that
83 per cent of the benefit was received by those in the top 10 per cent.14

Key provisions
1.15 Schedule 1 to the bills would amend the income tax law to make a range of
changes to CGT with the stated aim of improving the efficiency and fairness of
the tax system.15 These key changes are discussed below.
Cost base indexation for Australian resident individuals and trusts
1.16 Schedule 1 would amend the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (ITAA 1997) to
provide for the indexation of capital gains for individuals and trusts and the
removal of the CGTD for individuals and trusts from 1 July 2027.16 For CGT
events that occur on or after 1 July 2027:
 cost base indexation can be used for gains that accrue from 1 July 2027,
provided the asset is held for at least 12 months;
 the CGTD will continue to be available to discount capital gains accrued
prior to 1 July 2027; and
 for CGT assets acquired before 21 September 1999, the choice between cost
base indexation and the CGTD is removed.17
1.17 For CGT events that occur before 1 July 2027, there is no change in the
application of cost base indexation or the CGTD. Further, there is no change in
the availability of cost base indexation for other entities.18
1.18 Currently, cost base indexation is only available for assets that are acquired
between 20 September 1985 and 21 September 1999, and held for at least 12
months. Indexation only takes into account inflation up to 30 September 1999,
when it was frozen.19
1.19 The Treasury Laws Bill does not alter the methodology for calculating capital
losses—which has largely remained unamended since the introduction of CGT
in 1985. Capital losses are calculated using the reduced cost base of a CGT asset,
rather than the asset’s cost base that is used when calculating a gain.20
1.20 Similar to requirements for the previous CGTD, there are also residency
requirements that must be satisfied for indexation of the cost base of a CGT asset
to be available for an individual in relation to CGT events that happen on, or

1.21 The utilisation of cost base indexation ensures that only the real (that is,

Minimum tax on capital gains
1.22 A minimum 30 per cent income tax on capital gains that are realised on or after
1 July 2027 is introduced by new Division 119 of the ITAA 1997 and amendments
to the Income Tax Rates Act 1986 (Rates Act).23
1.23 The introduction of the minimum tax reduces the benefit of taxpayers deferring
the realisation of capital gains to years where their marginal tax rates are low.
The EM states that this approach ensures gains are subject to a tax rate closer to
the rate an individual faced during their working life and is commensurate with
the tax rate paid by most workers.24
1.24 The minimum tax will not apply to recipients of certain payments of a kind
prescribed in a legislative instrument by the Minister, if these recipients receive
those payments at any time during the income year. The EM notes that it is
intended that such payments include those relied upon for basic living expenses,
such as the Age Pension or JobSeeker.25
1.25 Although the minimum tax applies to all CGT assets, it does not apply in
relation to new residential dwellings or affordable housing—unless the relevant
entity with these asset classes chooses indexation and the minimum tax over
applying the CGTD.26

Pre-CGT assets
1.27 Schedule 1 amends the ITAA 1997 to bring pre-CGT assets into the CGT regime
for capital gains that arise on, or after, 1 July 2027, and apply to all entities that
hold pre-CGT assets—including individuals, trusts, and companies.27
1.28 The amendments apply to pre-CGT assets held on 30 June 2027. From 1 July
2027 these assets are subject to the CGT regime; however, CGT only applies in
respect of capital gains that arise on, or after, 1 July 2027, and only once a
realisation event happens in relation to the asset on, or after, 1 July 2027.28
1.29 In calculating their capital gains, an entity that is entitled to apply indexation
under the new CGT regime can do so to the cost base of the CGT asset, which is
no longer a pre-CGT asset, after 1 July 2027.29
1.30 The amendments remove the pre-CGT status for assets by deeming them to be
sold on 30 June 2027 and reacquired on 1 July 2027 for their market value, or
equivalent. Consequently, from 1 July 2027 there will no longer be any
remaining pre-CGT assets.30

New residential dwellings and affordable housing
1.32 Individuals and trusts that hold new residential dwellings or affordable housing
after 1 July 2027 will have a choice between applying relevant CGT discounts or
cost base indexation in respect of the period after 1 July 2027 and the minimum
tax. The chosen treatment will apply to the whole holding period, even if the
asset is purchased before and sold after 1 July 2027.32
1.33 The EM states that this policy for new residential dwellings and affordable
housing reflects the intent of the tax reforms to maintain incentives for new
residential dwelling and affordable housing supply in Australia.33 Existing
arrangements make no distinction between new and existing residential
dwellings for CGT.34

Structure of the changes to the ITAA 1997
1.40 Schedule 1 to the Treasury Laws Bill makes extensive amendments to the ITAA
1997. A summary of the purpose of each key division being amended is
provided below:
 Division 102—provides that a net capital gain is included in assessable
income for an income year and sets the method to work out a net capital
gain;
 Division 110—sets out how to work out the cost base and reduced cost base
of a CGT asset, which are used in calculating whether you make a capital
gain or loss from most CGT events. Indexation is relevant to calculating the
cost base, as provided by section 110-36;
 Division 112—explains how to modify the cost base in certain situations;
 Division 114—provides how to index expenditure and when indexation is
relevant or not relevant to expenditure in each element of the cost base. The
Division contains a number of other rules which affect how indexation
works;
 Division 115—deals with discount capital gains and trusts’ net capital gains;
and
 Subdivision 960-M—provides the indexation rules. The indexation factor is
calculated using formula in subsection 960-275.42
1.41 Schedule 1 also introduces the new Division 119 which implements a new
minimum tax on individuals for certain capital gains.43

for ‘residential dwellings’ to new builds.44 The schedule partially implements
the Tax reform—Boosting Home Ownership—reforming negative gearing and capital

2.39 A few submitters described how the CGTD and NG have benefitted investors at
public expense. The Grattan Institute, for example, submitted that the two
measures in combination allow investors to reduce and defer personal income
tax at a substantial annual cost to the public purse.35 The institute explained:
Capital gains…are taxed on sale rather than as they accrue. This allows
investors to defer paying tax on the capital gains that they accrue, which is
akin to the government providing the investor with an interest-free loan.
It reduces the effective tax rate paid on gains, with the tax benefit increasing
if the asset is held for longer. The benefits of waiting can be substantial.
A person paying the top rate of income tax of 47 cents in the dollar
(including the Medicare levy) who times the sale of an investment after 15
years for when they are in the lower (30 cents) tax bracket would reduce the
annual nominal tax rate on that investment from 28 per cent to 25 per cent.
Investors are also able to choose the time of an asset’s sale to minimise taxes
on capital gains. They can reduce their tax by selling assets when their
income is low, such as after they retire, so they are taxed at a lower marginal
rate. The flip side of the benefits to waiting is that taxing capital gains can
lead to asset lock-in.36

buyers, improving productivity by reducing distortions so that investment
decisions are based on economic outcomes rather than tax outcomes, and
supporting a more sustainable tax system.

good. The evidence is that these bills do the opposite, and that the Government
proceeded without understanding the harm. The damage operates through two

otherwise-deductible rule for salary-packaged benefits, undermines salary-
packaged giving. Philanthropy Australia has described the combined effect as a
"donation tax".
