Chair's Foreword
Australians have never had more ways to pay.
A purchase that once involved handing over cash can now be completed with a card, a
phone, a smartwatch, an account-to-account transfer, a digital wallet, a Buy Now Pay Later
service or an increasingly diverse range of online payment platforms.
The speed with which Australians have embraced these technologies reflects not only the
success of innovation in the payments sector, but also Australians’ longstanding reputation
as enthusiastic and early adopters of new technology.
As a result, consumers expect payments to be instant, secure and convenient. Businesses
increasingly depend on sophisticated digital systems to receive payments, manage
transactions and engage with customers.
While technology ushers in new ways of doing things, some things remain the same. Global
technology companies are playing an increasingly prominent role in how Australians access
and use payment services. As always, bigger players call the shots on how their tech gets
used and the margins they get to make off it.
There is no doubt that developments in payment systems have delivered substantial
benefits. However, it’s important we take stock of where we have got to, and that we take the
opportunity to raise important questions.
Who benefits most from innovation in the payments sector?
Are consumers and small businesses receiving the full benefits of competition?
Are new entrants able to compete on fair terms?
Are payment costs transparent and reasonable?
And does Australia's regulatory framework remain fit for purpose in a payments system that
is changing rapidly?
These questions sit at the heart of this report.
Throughout the course of the inquiry, the committee heard evidence from regulators,
financial institutions, payment service providers, technology companies, and merchants.
The committee heard concerns regarding the growing complexity of card-related payment
fees and the impact those costs can have on small businesses. It examined evidence
regarding the market power of major card schemes and the increasing influence of digital
iii
wallet providers. It also considered the opportunities presented by account-to-account
payment systems, emerging payment technologies and new forms of digital money.
Strong competition is often what drives innovation. Equally, innovation delivers its greatest
benefits when markets remain open, transparent and contestable.
However, the evidence received at this inquiry revealed concerning features of the payments
sector in Australia – namely, that aspects of its market structure are anticompetitive, opaque,
and complex. In circumstances where barriers to entry prevent competition from developing,
policymakers and regulators must be prepared to act.
Australia's payments system should work in the interests of those who rely upon it. That
means consumers should have confidence that payment services are secure, affordable and
competitive. Small businesses should not be disadvantaged by complexity or a lack of
bargaining power. And Australian businesses should have a genuine opportunity to compete
and innovate within the system.
Australia's payments system is fundamental to the operation of the national economy. As
technology continues to reshape how money moves, the challenge will be ensuring that
competition, transparency and consumer benefit keep pace with innovation – and that we
can point to broader benefits for the many within the payments landscape.
This report, A Level Paying Field, seeks to contribute to that objective. The committee has
sought to put a spotlight on issues that demand bold, energetic responses and reflects the
committee's commitment to ensuring that Australia's payments system remains fair, efficient
and fit for the future.
Hon Ed Husic MP

Terms of reference
The House of Representatives Standing Committee on Economics will inquire into matters
relating to current digital payment schemes and emerging technologies, and report on:
1 Card based payment schemes and digital wallets and whether the current
payment schemes landscape is fair and accessible, including:
a. transparency and affordability of scheme fees for payments services
providers;
b. the inequity of payment costs for small businesses;
c. the extent to which consolidated market power between payment schemes
has impacted choice and accessibility to alternative payment systems.
2 New and emerging payment technologies in the payments space, including but
not limited to:
a. digital currency and blockchain technology;
b. account to account-based payments;
c. opportunities for Australia to increase competition and consumer choice, and
decrease fees in the digital payment processing industry;
d. any other matters.

1. Introduction
1.1 Following reference from the Treasurer, the Hon Dr Jim Chalmers MP, in December
2025, the House of Representatives Standing Committee on Economics (the
committee) commenced an inquiry into matters relating to current digital payment
schemes and emerging digital technologies.
1.2 This inquiry examines current card-based payment schemes and digital wallets from
the perspective of accessibility and fairness for consumers and small merchants. It
also considers how technological innovation in the payments sector can be adopted
to improve competition, expand consumer choice and reduce costs within the
Australian payments system.
1.3 This report aims to address possible areas for additional regulatory focus in the
payments sector, while acknowledging that regulators have defined roles within the
Australian payments system and that this is regulatory space already under a
significant program of modernisation. Although the recommendations in this report
are directed to the Australian Government, its insights and commentary may be of
value to a wider range of stakeholders.
Context for the inquiry
1.4 Australia’s payments system is fundamental to the national economy. The payments
system enables the transfer of value, and is therefore critical to everyday commerce
between people, business and government. It is in the national interest for the
payments system to be as secure and efficient as possible.
1.5 The payments system has become more complex in recent decades as new
technologies and payment methods have been developed, and as more businesses
offer new payment service options to their customers. The system is now more aptly
described as an ecosystem rather than a linear or near-linear process between payer
and payee. The pace of change in the sector has also increased in more recent
years, particularly with the growth of online shopping and increased general demand
for near-instant payments. The use of contactless card payments has grown and
there is less reliance on cash and cheques. As technological innovations advance,
further forms of payments will be developed and there will continue to be new
participants to the system. As the payments system becomes increasingly digitised,
there is a critical need to maintain the reliability of existing and emerging payments
infrastructure.
1.6 The ongoing change and increased complexity of payments is a challenge for
payments regulators in Australia, as it is for those in other countries. In turn, the
regulatory framework can be complex to navigate for new participants in the
1
payments system. The Australian Government, several regulators and key industry
bodies together make up the regulatory framework. At the time of this report, there
are several consequential review and reform processes occurring in the sector,
including in relation to fees for retail payments and licensing of payment service
providers, which will shape the payments system going into the future.1
1.7 In 2023, the Australian Government set four core principles to guide the direction of
the payments system:
• Trustworthiness: A trustworthy system that is safe, reliable and resilient to fraud
and other threats.
• Accessibility: An accessible system that is readily available, competitive and
offers choice to customers.
• Innovation: An innovative system that is agile, adaptable to challenges and
forward-looking.
• Efficiency: An efficient payments system that is fast, seamless and low-cost.2
1.8 Given the context of fast-changing payments technologies, the increasingly complex
interactions between participants and the breadth of current work to modernise the
regulatory framework, the committee has seen value in scrutinising the payments
sector with a lens focused on small businesses and consumers.
1.9 In a very practical sense, the Australian payments system must be fairly accessible
to everyone that interacts with the Australian economy. A payments system that
fosters innovation should exhibit healthy competition and lead to new payment
methods that can be more convenient and efficient for system participants, including
businesses and consumers.
1.10 At the same time, incorporation of new technologies into the payments system,
together with system maintenance and reliability, requires investment. Accessibility to
technologies used in the system needs to be on a fair basis, as does the distribution
of costs and revenue throughout the system. Pass-through of payment-related costs
to businesses – especially small merchants – and consumers needs to be
reasonable. Ultimately, the cost of receiving payment for goods and services through
the Australian payments system must not be a prohibitive factor in the cost of running
a small business.

2. The payments ecosystem
2.1 A payments system is the network of arrangements which allow consumers,
businesses and other organisations to transfer funds between one another. These
arrangements include methods, rules and procedures, standards, and technological
infrastructures required to support that money value transfer. Key payment
instruments in these systems include both cash and non-cash methods, such as
cards, cheques and electronic funds transfers.
2.2 This inquiry was primarily focused on non-cash payments in the retail payments
system, which facilitates the high-volume, low-value everyday transactions between
consumers and businesses.1 In particular, the focus was on payments made by card
and account-to-account transfer, as well the emerging technologies such as digital
wallets which enable these payment methods and new digital forms of money, which
form this growing ecosystem for non-cash payments.
2.3 This chapter outlines some of the key stakeholders and major parts of the retail
payment system in Australia, with a focus on card and other non-cash payments, to
provide context to the discussion of evidence in later chapters of this report.

2.4 The Australian Government is responsible for designing and implementing legislation
for the regulatory framework of the payments system and setting the strategic
direction for the system by ‘influencing the focus of agencies and regulators’.2

then-Treasurer the Hon Josh Frydenberg MP in August 2021 and made 15
recommendations to support the modernisation of the payments system.3

Payments System, setting out a program of regulatory reform to address those
recommendations. The 2023 Strategic Plan described that:

Reserve Bank of Australia
2.9 The RBA is the primary regulator of payments systems and has oversight of the
wholesale, retail and commercial payments systems. Its payments systems policy is
determined by the Payment Systems Board (PSB). The mandate of the PSB is to set
the RBA’s payments policy to ‘promote competition, efficiency and stability’ in the
payments system.6
2.10 The regulatory framework for payment systems is governed largely by the Payments
Systems (Regulation) Act 1998 (PSRA). Under the PSRA, the RBA has powers to
designate payment systems and impose rules such as access regimes and
standards. Regulation is developed by the RBA in close consultation with industry
and relevant parties, and any intervention in the system is generally preceded by
consultation with industry on non-regulatory options.7
2.11 In September 2025, the Australian Government passed the Treasury Laws
Amendment (Payment Systems Modernisation) Act 2025 which amended the PSRA
to enable the RBA to regulate a wider range of payment systems and their
participants; these changes took effect in December 2025.8 In mid-2026, the RBA will
begin consultation on the application of the amended PSRA.9

Review of Merchant Card Payment Costs and Surcharging
2.13 In October 2024, the RBA launched its Review of Merchant Card Payment Costs and
Surcharging (RBA Review) to identify regulatory actions to promote competition and
efficiency for the card-based payment system as part of its periodic review of retail
payments regulation.
2.14 Following an initial period of consultation, the RBA released a consultation paper in
July 2025, setting out preliminary proposals from the PSB that competition and
efficiency in the payments system would be enhanced by removing surcharging on
designated card payment networks, lowering card payment costs and increasing
transparency. Stakeholders were invited to submit their views on the policy proposals
by August 2025, and in October 2025 the RBA published a summary of submissions.
During this period, the RBA also conducted cost studies to gather data to support the
review.11
2.15 In March 2026, the RBA published the conclusions of its review, setting out the
PSB’s final decisions on surcharging, fees and transparency for card payments.12
These conclusions are covered in further detail in Chapter 3 of this report.
Australian Securities and Investments Commission
2.16 ASIC is Australia’s corporate, markets, financial services and consumer credit
regulator. Under the Australian Securities and Investments Commission Act 2001,
ASIC’s statutory objectives include ‘maintaining, facilitating and improving the
performance of the financial system and entities in it’ and ‘promoting confident and
informed investor and consumer participation in the financial system’.13
2.17 As part of its remit, ASIC administers the voluntary ePayments Code, which provides

Card payments
2.30 In 2025, card payments accounted for three quarters of total consumer payments in
Australia, remaining the most frequently used consumer payment method.28 Payment
cards are usually a credit card or a debit card.29
2.31 A credit card is a payment card that allows cardholders to borrow money via the card
issuer to undertake transactions, with the obligation on the cardholder to repay the
borrowing amount at a pre-determined date. These cards can attract interest and
other fees but may also provide other benefits such as loyalty points or travel
insurance. A debit card is a payment card where the cardholder’s own funds are
used for transactions.
2.32 Credit and debit card payments in Australia are processed by arrangements known
as three- or four-party schemes, which differ in their structure, the participants
involved, the way payment services are provided and the types of fees charged.
2.33 Matters relating to card payment schemes, their fee structures and their role in the
payments system are detailed in Chapter 3.
Four-party schemes
2.34 Most card scheme transactions in Australia are processed using four-party schemes.
Four-party schemes are regulated by the RBA under the PSRA include the credit and
debit networks operated by Visa and Mastercard, and the domestic eftpos debit
network operated by AP+.30
2.35 In four party schemes, credit or debit cards are issued by financial institutions to
cardholders, and transactions are processed over a card scheme network. The

scheme charges scheme fees to issuers and acquirers for services including
transaction processing, authorisation, network access and brand use.
2.36 A card transaction under a four-party scheme involves the following parties:
• Cardholder: The individual who has been provided a payment card by a financial
institution.
• Issuer: The financial institution that issues the card to the cardholder.
• Merchant: The entity that is accepting a card as payment for goods or services.

2.45 The other type of card scheme available in Australia is a three-party scheme. A

Surcharging
2.48 Surcharges are fees paid by customers, in addition to the price of a good or service,
which allow merchants to pass on the cost of a customer’s chosen payment method.
While the proportion of card payments incurring surcharges appears to have
increased in recent years, this still represents only a minority of consumer card
purchases, with 2022 survey data indicating that 7 per cent of card payments
attracted surcharges.46
2.49 The low share of transactions attracting surcharges partly reflects that large,
frequently visited merchants such as supermarkets, large online retailers, petrol
stations and large fast-food chains typically do not apply surcharges. Evidence
suggests there is an increasing prevalence in surcharging particularly within the
hospitality industry.47
2.50 At the time that the committee received evidence for this inquiry, merchants had the
right to apply a surcharge on card payments, limited to the amount it cost the
merchant to accept that type of card for that transaction. The RBA’s surcharging
framework sought to incentivise customers to use lower cost payment methods,
which would in turn lower payment acceptance costs for merchants.
2.51 The RBA Review concluded that the surcharging framework no longer achieved this
objective and the PSB decided it would be in the public interest to remove
surcharging on cards on the designated card networks (eftpos, Visa and
Mastercard).48

Other non-cash payments
2.57 While card payments represent most non-cash payments in the Australian retail
payments system, new and emerging technologies continue to change the landscape
for digital payments. Some of these changes include the emergence of new non-
bank payment service providers, Buy Now Pay Later (BNPL) services, innovations in
account-to-account payments, the rapid uptake of digital wallets and the increasing
use of tokenised assets, such as cryptocurrencies and stablecoins.
Online payments and new payment service providers
2.58 The increase in online payments in Australia has come with an increase in the
number of non-bank payment gateways, which enable merchants to accept online
payments, and payment facilitators operated by financial technology (fintech)
companies. Many of these new payment facilitators offer simpler onboarding for
smaller merchants across online and in-person payments, sometimes bundling these
services with software for order management or inventory tracking, as well as other
services not usually provided by traditional issuer-acquirers, such as software-based
acceptance on mobile devices.57 Some of these new payment service providers
include Square (Block, Inc.), Stripe, Smartpay (Shift4), Shopify, Tyro and Zeller, the
latter two being Australian-owned.
2.59 The introduction of new fintech PSPs has contributed to a drop in the four major
banks’ market share in Australia. FinTech Australia reported that, since 2018, the
major banks’ market share has declined from 77 per cent to 64 per cent in the card
present environment and from 95% to 51% in the card not present environment, and
that this has corresponded with a reduction in average merchant service fees due to
competition in the space.58

Buy Now Pay Later
2.64 BNPL has emerged in recent years as an alternative to card payments for both in-
person and online environments. BNPL allows customers to purchase goods and
services and make a series of interest-free repayments to a BNPL service provider,
such as Afterpay. BNPL transactions now represent around 2.5 per cent of card
purchases in Australia and have doubled in value since 2020.62
2.65 BNPL services are typically expensive for merchants to accept – well above the cost
of card payments – and most BNPL transactions do not allow merchants to pass on
the cost of these services to consumers through surcharging, limiting merchants’
ability to direct consumers to cheaper payment methods.63
2.66 BNPL is now under the regulatory remit of the RBA, following the amendments to the
PSRA in September 2025.64 The PSB has indicated that it will consider concerns that
proposed retail payments reforms to interchange and surcharging for card payments
may provide an ‘undue competitive advantage’ to BNPL service providers – in
particular, ‘whether any increase in credit card fees or interest rates to offset lower
interchange caps could prompt some consumers to switch to BNPL’ – when it
undertakes its PSRA review later this year.65
2.67 The committee received only limited evidence about BNPL, so this form of payment
is not considered in the substantive chapters of this report.

Australia is broad in scope and includes a range of payments infrastructures and
consumer-facing services that facilitate these payments.66 The most significant of
these are the Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS) and the NPP.

governments, businesses and consumers to make one-off and recurring payments
by facilitating direct debit and direct credit (Direct Entry) between accounts at
different financial institutions. BECS has been in operation for more than 30 years. It
processes payments on a periodic basis and only on business days. In 2024, around

most common internationally for countries to have both a batch-based payments
system (like BECS) and a fast payment system (like the NPP) in place. The RBA
outlined that migrating payments from BECS to the NPP ‘would require a
fundamental change in how these critical payments are processed’.74 The risk
assessment identified several risks to the orderly transition from BECS to alternative
systems, primarily the NPP, and noted that these risks were ‘not sufficiently
understood and adequately managed by relevant industry participants’. The RBA
made a set of recommendations and put in place an annual program of oversight of
the intended decommissioning of BECS.75
2.75 In December 2025, AusPayNet announced the removal of its 2030 target date and
committed to keeping BECS in operation until a clear roadmap for its
decommissioning is in place.76
2.76 The payments industry, RBA and Treasury are currently collaborating through the
A2A Payments Roundtable to work on a ‘shared vision’ for the future design of the
A2A system in Australia, with a focus on how to achieve safe, reliable, low-cost and
easy-to-use payments between bank accounts and what the roles of innovation and
payment capabilities are in delivering this.77 Members of BECS and the NPP,
operators of alternative payment systems to BECS and relevant end-user groups are
also involved in these discussions.78
2.77 Further matters relating to A2A payments are set out in Chapter 5.

3. Card-based payment schemes
3.1 A major focus of the inquiry was how card-based payment schemes operate in
practice in Australia, including accessibility and cost impacts for small businesses,
and transparency of scheme fees through to small business and consumers. This
means much of the focus was on retail payments including using card networks,
rather than the wholesale payments sector.
3.2 Card based payments are a core part of everyday commerce in Australia. Small
businesses therefore have limited practical ability to avoid card-based payments. The
-
committee heard that the expanded use of these payment methods has created
challenges for small businesses, particularly in relation to fees, pricing transparency
and inequities between small and large businesses. A range of views were
expressed by industry participants on scheme and interchange fees, small business
programs, market power share of the major card schemes and the major banks’
involvement in card schemes.
3.3 This chapter first sets out evidence heard of the positions in the payments ecosystem
held by the major card networks and the major banks. Then follows an exploration of
fees, including scheme, interchange and merchant service fees and least cost
routing. A key focus related to fees is challenges faced by small business, therefore
programs for small business and barriers to switching providers are also discussed.
The chapter then considers two matters that the committee heard were important to
small business: surcharging and fee transparency.
3.4 The committee received evidence for this inquiry during a period that overlapped with
the final months of the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) Payments System Board’s
(PSB) Review of merchant card payment costs and surcharging (RBA Review).
Consequently, much of the evidence received during the inquiry related to the RBA
Review, and a range of views were expressed to the committee about matters on
which the RBA has since published its conclusions.

the PSB were to:
• remove surcharging from debit and credit cards from 1 October 2026, by lifting the
prohibition on ‘no-surcharge’ rules for debit, prepaid and credit cards of the
Mastercard, Visa and eftpos networks;
• lower interchange fee caps on domestic-issued debit and consumer credit card
transactions, while retaining the interchange cap on domestic-issued commercial

4. Digital wallets
4.1 Digital wallets are a core component of the changing payments landscape in
Australia, providing consumers and merchants an alternative to traditional payment
methods. As outlined in Chapter 2, digital wallets are a technological innovation that
allow users to store funds, transfer funds or make electronic payments through a
software application (app). Mobile wallets are a specific form of digital wallet which
enable contactless payments from tokenised cards stored on users’ mobile devices.
4.2 In Australia, the most prominent digital pass-through wallets are provisioned by
Apple, Google and Samsung. These wallets use near-field communication (NFC)
technology in mobile devices to make tap-and-go payments in person and can also
be used to make card-not-present payments online.
4.3 Throughout this inquiry, the committee heard about the growing use of digital wallets
in Australia. Much of the evidence received had a focus on the use of mobile and
pass-through wallets and how these interact with existing payment system
infrastructure. While many consumers have readily taken up this new technology,
there remain concerns that the regulatory and structural frameworks governing this
segment of Australia’s payments system have not evolved sufficiently to reflect the
modern operating environment.
4.4 This chapter examines how digital wallets interact with the traditional payment
system, gaps in Australia’s policy and regulatory framework for digital wallets and
concerns about Apple’s fee structure and market dominance in the sector.
Rapid uptake of digital wallets
4.5 There has been a wide-spread uptake of digital and mobile wallets by Australian
consumers in recent years. The Australian Banking Association (ABA) outlined that
this rapid change has been driven by both the convenience the modern technology
offers consumers, and the productivity benefits it provides businesses.1
4.6 From March 2020 to November 2025, transactions using digital wallets issued by
Apple, Google and Samsung rose from 10 per cent to 43 per cent.2 According to data
from the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA), by November of 2025, digital wallets

5. New and emerging payment
technologies
5.1 The committee received evidence from an array of stakeholders about new and
emerging payment technologies. Recent innovations in the sector have included
building upon existing payment rails, such as through account-to-account (A2A)
payments and the New Payments Platform (NPP), and emerging forms of payments
such as digital currency.
5.2 Financial technology (fintech) companies are prominent in this part of the payments
sector and bring with them ‘disruptive technologies and services not offered by big
banks’. Fintech companies have the potential to enhance consumer outcomes by
increasing competition with established stakeholders in the payments ecosystem.1
5.3 The emergence of new technology brings with it a need to consider disruptions to
and integration into the current system, limits to the new technologies, access to
existing payments infrastructure for new players, and regulation to keep pace with a
modernising payments system.
5.4 This chapter will consider innovations in A2A payments and the shift to the NPP, new
and emerging payment technologies such as quick response (QR) codes and
changes to point-of-sale (POS) systems as they integrate new technology into their
operations. The chapter then examines the role of digital assets, such as
cryptocurrency and stablecoins, in the payments sector. Finally, it examines
opportunities for new approaches to remittance and regulatory support for fintechs.
Innovation in account-to-account payments
5.5 A2A payments have been a cornerstone of Australia’s payment system for decades.
Collectively, A2A payment networks in Australia process approximately 20 billion
transactions per year which have a value of over $20 trillion. Payments made through
A2A include everyday retail purchases, business payments, salary and welfare
disbursements, peer-to-peer transfers and cross-border remittances. A2A payments
are a priority of the Australian Government’s Strategic Plan for Australia’s Payment
System.2

Modernising existing A2A infrastructures
5.14 As outlined in Chapter 2, Australia’s A2A payments have historically been operated
through the Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS), while the introduction of the
NPP in 2018 has provided options for near real-time A2A payments using PayID,
PayTo and Osko services. Work is underway to decommission BECS and transition
to more modern payments clearing through the NPP or similar technologies.
Accessing the NPP
5.15 There are multiple pathways for organisations to access the NPP:
• NPP direct participants offer and process real time payments for their customers
using the NPP, access PayTo and offer PayID services, and provide secondary
access services to third parties such as identified institutions.
• Identified institutions can offer customers NPP enabled payments and products

5.47 When a customer makes a purchase either in person or online, they pay via a POS.

6. Dynamics of a changing
payments sector
6.1 A common theme throughout this inquiry has been the significant degree of
technological innovation driving change in how businesses and consumers engage
with the payments sector. Australia’s payments ecosystem is undergoing a period of
modernisation as key stakeholders consider the future of money, the role of artificial
intelligence to make payments and the introduction of new methods of payment.
6.2 However, technological innovation has brought with it concerns about the
implications for competition in the Australian payments sector, particularly where
global companies are perceived to have market control. There are also worries about
the risks of regulation not keeping up with or supporting the pace of change.
6.3 This chapter aims to bring together the ‘big picture’ views put forward by
stakeholders about the current and future states of Australia’s payments sector and
considers how government, regulators and industry can work together to encourage
a level playing field for providers and a fair go for merchants and consumers.
Pace of technological change
6.4 The committee received evidence that the payments landscape is undergoing
continuous technological transformation. Witnesses and submitters emphasised that
advances in digital infrastructure and emerging technologies will reshape how
payments are made and how they need to be regulated. These developments are
creating new opportunities to improve efficiency, competition and consumer choice in
the payment sector, although they are also presenting new challenges, as emerging
technology changes the nature of payment systems and the way participants interact
with them.
6.5 Several organisations saw modernisation and technological advancement as critical
to supporting the ongoing efficiency and resilience of the payments system, if
accompanied by the appropriate safeguards. The National Australia Bank (NAB)
submitted that the shift towards digitisation and contemporary platforms is ‘essential
for the future needs of the economy’ while recognising it brought new challenges that
would also need attention.1 Visa noted that technological transformation was
providing increasing choice and accessibility for all Australians, but only if sustained
innovation in security and consumer and business protection also remained core.2

6.39 The committee heard a range of divergent views about competition in the Australian

6.40 In addition, participants in Australia’s payments system are commercially motivated.

interest to make the substantial investment required to provide a new service if they

Treasury’s 2021 Review of the Australian Payments System
6.80 Treasury’s review of the Australian payments system in 2021 (Treasury Review)
examined whether Australia’s payments regulatory architecture remained fit for
purpose in light of rapid technological, commercial and behavioural change. The
Treasury Review found that the traditional payments regulation architecture
continued to perform well in achieving its core safety objectives; consumers and
businesses generally have confidence that payments will be processed reliably, and
Australia has experienced relatively few systemic disruptions compared with other
jurisdictions.87
6.81 However, the Treasury Review also concluded that the existing framework was
increasingly strained by the entry of non bank providers, the disaggregation of
payment services, and new business models that do not fit neatly within existing
-
legislative definitions.88 It also found that the framework was unnecessarily complex
to navigate. PSPs may be required to engage with multiple regulators, obtain
overlapping authorisations, and comply with uncoordinated regulatory requirements
for activities that pose similar risks.89 This complexity could disproportionately affect
new entrants and smaller providers and act as a barrier to competition and
innovation.
6.82 In response, the Treasury Review proposed reshaping leadership and governance
arrangements, with a role for the Treasurer and Treasury in setting strategic direction
for the payments ecosystem to provide whole of system policy leadership, resolve
cross cutting issues and align regulatory activity with broader economic and digital
- -
economy objectives. A key recommendation was the development of a national
-
payments strategy, supported by an enhanced payments policy capability within
Treasury and an industry convenor to facilitate structured engagement across
regulators, industry and user groups.90
6.83 Additionally, the Treasury Review identified the existing licensing framework for
payment services as confusing, fragmented and heavily reliant on exemptions and
regulatory discretion. In particular, the non cash payment facility regime was
considered ill suited to contemporary payment services that separate facilitation from
-
the storage of value.91 To address this, the Treasury Review recommended the
-
establishment of a single, tiered payments licensing framework based on regulated
payment functions rather than institutional form.92 Under this approach, licensing
requirements would be scaled to the size and risk profile of providers, with separate
authorisations for payment facilitation services and stored value facilities.

Strategic Plan for Australia’s Payment System
6.84 Treasury released A Strategic Plan for Australia’s Payment System (Strategic Plan)
in June 2023. The Plan noted that while Australia’s framework had supported stability
and innovation to date, regulatory reform was required to address emerging risks,
barriers to competition and regulatory fragmentation.93
6.85 A central focus of the Strategic Plan was reform of the payments regulatory
framework to better reflect modern payment functions and risks. The Plan
emphasised the need for stronger coordination and alignment across regulators to
address the complexity of the multi-regulator model. It acknowledged industry
concerns about inconsistent regulatory approaches, lack of transparency and the
burden of engaging with multiple agencies.94
6.86 The Strategic Plan concluded that regulatory reform was essential to support a
modern, resilient and competitive payments ecosystem. It positioned updated
legislation, a new licensing regime, mandatory consumer protections and clearer
regulatory coordination as foundational to future reform, while also supporting
broader objectives such as reduced small business payment costs, safer digital
payments and improved system resilience.95
6.87 The Strategic Plan also set out an intended 18-month review cycle, based on
feedback from stakeholders than an annual review would not be desirable.96 Under
this review cycle, the next plan was due in 2025, but no updated plan has been
released at the time of this report.

6.97 An effective and contemporary regulatory framework is fundamental to maintaining

increasing complexity of the payments ecosystem require a regulatory approach that
is both coordinated and adaptable to the pace of change.
6.98 The committee supports the direction of reforms to modernise the regulatory
frameworks for the payments system, including expanding the scope of the Reserve
Bank of Australia’s responsibilities under the Payments System (Regulation) Act

providers. These reforms have the potential to promote a more level playing field for
- -

payment services.

uncertainty for some of this regulatory reform risks undermining these objectives.

sequencing and coherence, particularly for new and smaller entrants navigating

clearly articulated reform pathway will be critical to supporting investment, innovation
and competition.
