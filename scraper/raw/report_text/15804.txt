Chapter 1
Overview
Purpose of the bill
1.1 The Tertiary Education Legislation Amendment (There For Education, Not
Profit) Bill 2025 (the bill) seeks to amend the Australian National University Act
1991 (ANU Act) and the Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency Act 2011
(TEQSA Act) to set a statutory limit of $430 000 for vice-chancellor remuneration
and make arrangements to ensure compliance with the statutory limit.1
1.2 In her second reading speech, Senator Jacqui Lambie stated that the bill would
set a 'reasonable and proportionate remuneration benchmark' that would reflect
the 'responsibilities of university chief executives within the broader context of
the Australian public sector'.2
Context of the bill
1.3 The bill was initially introduced in the Senate by Senator Lambie as a private
senator's bill on 5 February 2025.3 The bill was then referred, on 12 February
2025, to the Education and Employment Legislation Committee (the committee)
for inquiry and report.4 On 28 March 2025, the Governor-General issued a
proclamation proroguing the 47th Parliament which limited the committee's
ability to progress its inquiry into the bill. As such, the committee tabled a short
interim report acknowledging this and that it considered it appropriate for the
Senate in the 48th Parliament to resolve whether to re-refer the inquiry to the
committee.
1.4 On 23 July 2025, the bill was restored to the Notice Paper, and the Senate
re-referred the bill to the committee for inquiry and report by 28 November
2025.5

Key provisions of the bill
1.21 The following section provides a summary of the key aspects of the bill
including the setting of remuneration limits for vice-chancellor salaries and the
enhanced powers that TEQSA will have in terms of obtaining information from
universities.
Setting vice-chancellor remuneration for the Australian National University
1.22 Part 1 of the bill relates to the setting of remuneration for vice-chancellors at the
ANU by amending the ANU Act. The ANU is the only Australian university
that is established by and has its functions, powers, and governance
arrangements set out by an Act of the Commonwealth.22

1.23 The bill seeks to add a new subsection to section 34 of the ANU Act that sets out
that the renumeration of the Vice-Chancellor, for each year of the
Vice-Chancellor's term of appointment, must be less than $430 000 or the
amount prescribed by the Minister for Education (Minister).23 The bill provides
for the Minister to prescribe a remuneration amount for the Vice-Chancellor by
legislative instrument, for one year.24
Setting vice-chancellor remuneration for other universities
1.24 Part 2 of the bill seeks to amend the TEQSA Act to instate a vice-chancellor
remuneration requirement for Australian universities, excluding ANU, that are
Table A providers and have an office of vice-chancellor.25 This amendment seeks
to require the remuneration of vice-chancellors to be less than $430,000, or the
amount prescribed by the Minister, by legislative instrument for the first year of
the vice-chancellor's appointment.26
Compliance with vice-chancellor remuneration limit
1.25 The bill also seeks to insert a provision that allows TEQSA to request
information from Australian universities relating to the university's compliance
with the remuneration limit. The bill states that universities must comply with
this request.27

Chapter 2
Views on the bill
2.1 The salaries of vice-chancellors at Australian universities have come under
scrutiny in various reviews and inquiries, with many of the view that these
salaries are excessive. The average pay for an Australian vice-chancellor in 2023
was $1.049 million.1
2.2 The Tertiary Education Legislation Amendment (There for Education, Not
Profit) Bill 2025 (the bill) looks to restrict the salaries of vice-chancellors.
General views on the bill
2.3 Submitters held mixed views on the bill with universities and those representing
universities generally not supporting the bill. This included Universities
Australia, which argued that this bill is 'yet another example of [the] sector being
weaponised for political gain'.2
2.4 Other submitters voiced support for capping vice-chancellor salaries, including
the Australia Institute which expressed that the bill is 'an important step
towards fixing governance at Australia's universities by making these public
institutions publicly accountable for their remuneration decisions'.3
Salaries to attract talent
2.5 According to some participants, high vice-chancellor salaries are needed to
attract high-calibre and world leading talent to Australian universities. The
Universities Chancellors Council (UCC) stated that a legislated cap on
vice-chancellor salaries would 'undermine universities' capacity to attract and
retain the talent and expertise needed for these critical roles'.4
2.6 The Australia Institute highlighted that high vice-chancellor salaries 'are
sometimes defended as necessary to attract managers talented enough to
maintain or improve the international rankings of Australian universities'.5
However, the Australia Institute disputed this justification, highlighting that

2.7 Dr Marija Taflaga and Dr Francis Markham suggested that high vice-chancellor
salaries do not guarantee the right candidates are selected for the role, and in
actual fact, could result in the opposite:
Oversized remuneration packages attract candidates who view universities
as career stepping stones rather than as communities devoted to knowledge.
Instead of selecting leaders committed to teaching and research, councils
risk recruiting corporate managers whose main motivation is financial gain
or reputation enhancement. The aim for such executives is to move on to the
next management role, rather than to invest in the long-term success of the
institution. Universities are not for-profit businesses—they do not excel at
chasing market share.7
2.8 Dr Shumi Akhtar expressed similar views, refuting suggestions that higher
salaries are essential to broaden industry appeal:
The claim that such inflated packages are necessary to attract 'industry
leaders' is deeply flawed. Too often, these so-called leaders lack the skills or
experience to effectively manage a university. The result is catastrophic
mismanagement that takes academics and students decades to repair.8
Salaries of other senior university executives
2.9 While the bill's focus is on capping the salaries of vice-chancellors, a number of
participants also highlighted the salaries of other senior executives at Australian
universities. The National Tertiary Education Union (NTEU) submitted that:
We also believe that the scope of the Bill, which focuses solely on VC pay, is
too narrow, as it ignores the hundreds of university senior executives who
are also earning pay levels in excess of state premiers and senior government
figures.9
2.10 For example, the NTEU stated that in 2024 there were over 300 university
executives being paid more than the premier or chief minister of their state,10
which according to Dr Akhtar, is despite the roles of prime minister and premier
being 'far more complex' than that of a vice-chancellor.11

Implementation of the salary cap
2.17 The Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA) advised that it
'is responsible for assuring that registered higher education providers … meet
their obligations' under the relevant legislation (the Tertiary Education Quality

(Threshold Standards) 2021), noting that:
The Threshold Standards do not include any requirements relating to
remuneration levels for individual staff members, and TEQSA does not
prescribe individual salaries. The Threshold Standards require providers to
be financially viable and sustainable to ensure the continued delivery of
quality education.18
2.18 Queensland University of Technology (QUT) agreed that TEQSA is not the
'appropriate agency' to implement and administer the salary cap, arguing that
'[t]he enforcement of the Bill's proposed arbitrary remuneration cap is not a
matter of education quality and standards'.19

Lack of indexation
2.25 Some submitters, who are both for and against the intent of the bill, expressed

Australian Greens' dissenting report
1.1 The Australian Greens strongly support capping vice-chancellor (VC) salaries
and have been advocating for this for many years. VCs of public Australian
universities are amongst some of the highest paid in the world, with the top
salaries higher than those of equivalent positions at the University of Oxford,
the University of Cambridge, and the University of Toronto. The average salary
for an Australian university Vice-Chancellor in 2023 was $1.049 million, with
Monash University having the highest salary at the time of $1.565 million.1 There
is no justification for these obscenely high salaries for VCs.2
1.2 Vice-chancellors across this country have overseen systemic wage theft,
rampant staff casualisation, staff cuts, and the gutting of courses. All the while,
the largesse of the executive class and their corporate consultants is on the rise.
The neoliberal, corporate university has become the norm where staff and
students are treated as numbers, pockets to empty and passions to exploit.
1.3 The committee's inquiry into the quality of governance at Australian higher education
providers made abundantly clear that the social licence for vice-chancellor
salaries is long gone, and trust between staff and students, and leadership has
eroded. Last year, both the University of Technology Sydney and the Australian
National University passed 'no confidence' motions in their vice-chancellors.3
These failures of governance and accountability cannot be separated from the
issue of high salaries.
1.4 It is commendable that this bill attempts to rein in exorbitant VC salaries;
however, the salary caps need to be extended to executives. A National Tertiary
Education Union analysis found that in 2024, there were over 300 university
executives earning more than the premiers of their states.4 Changes also need to
address the corporate culture of universities that has led to a bloated executive
class, otherwise salary caps will be circumvented through remuneration
packages by offering lavish allowances, fringe benefits, and other non-cash
perks.5 This would only further corrode transparency and restrict public
