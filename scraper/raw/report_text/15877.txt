List of recommendations
Recommendation 1
2.134 The committee recommends that the Australian Government:
 maintain its clear and principled objection to the continuing military rule
of Myanmar and its rejection of the 2025–26 elections and attempts to use
the 'sham' elections to build a cloak of respectability for the military
regime; and
 actively object to any attempts to legitimise the military regime,
normalise military rule and the acceptance of Myanmar into diplomatic,
parliamentary and regional or multilateral forums.
Recommendation 2
2.135 The committee recommends that the Australian Government:
 more regularly state its ongoing concern about the conflict in Myanmar
and continue to draw attention to the sustained repression, violence, and
human rights abuses perpetrated by the military regime;
 recognise that continued conflict in Myanmar is now entering a new and
more complex phase as it evolves from not just a humanitarian challenge
to a strategic one, recognising the challenge now intersects with regional
security, migration pressures and transnational crime networks; and
 maintain its policy of avoiding direct engagement with the military
regime.
Recommendation 3
2.138 The committee recommends that the Australian Government work towards
aligning its Myanmar-related sanctions with key allies such as the
United States of America.
Recommendation 4
2.139 The committee recommends that the Australian Government impose
sanctions against Myanmar's Central Bank and other banks controlled by the
military regime in Myanmar.
Recommendation 5
2.142 The committee recommends that the Australian Government seek to disrupt
and sanction the supply of jet fuel to the Myanmar junta and government.
xi
Recommendation 6
2.145 The committee recommends the Australian Government supplement its
engagement with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations by:
 strengthening engagement through other regional mechanisms such as
The Bali Process on People Smuggling, Trafficking in Persons and
Related Transnational Crime; and
 exploring new additional Myanmar-specific regional initiatives with
countries such as India.
Recommendation 7
2.152 The committee recommends the Australian Government:
 prioritise the finalisation and publication of initiatives contained in the
announcement of the $370 million over three years to address the
humanitarian crisis in Myanmar and support Rohingya refugees and host
communities in Bangladesh, including establishing a Civil Society Pooled
Fund;
 bring forward of the proposed mid-cycle review of the
Australia-Myanmar Development Partnership Plan 2024-2029 to reflect the
rapidly increasing humanitarian needs arising in Myanmar and border
communities in Thailand, India and Bangladesh;
 prioritise the development of the publication of clear operational
principles to guide non-avoidable engagement between development
partners and entities associated with the military regime;
 demonstrate how the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade due
diligence framework is working to facilitate greater access, flexibility and
use of locally led organisations in the delivery of Australia's
humanitarian program in Myanmar.
Recommendation 8
2.153 The committee reiterates the recommendations made in the committee's
report into the Efficacy of the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade due
diligence framework. In particular, recommendation 1 of that report which
urges greater flexibility in Australia’s delivery of aid, recognising that locally
led organisations can often better deliver aid in conflict situations.
Recommendation 9
2.155 The committee recommends the Australian Government increase
resettlement opportunities and alternative visas for education, research and
employment opportunities for Myanmar asylum seekers and refugees and
expand the Myanmar Australia Awards program.

Chapter 1
Introduction and background
Introduction
1.1 Myanmar (formerly Burma) has experienced prolonged periods of political
instability. While there have been brief episodes of political reform since
independence from British rule, the country has largely been governed by the
military following the overthrow of its civilian government in a 1962 coup.
1.2 The current conflict is deeply rooted, reflecting decades of military dominance
alongside longstanding struggles by ethnic groups seeking greater protection,
freedom from oppression, autonomy and independence.
1.3 The phased election process, which commenced in December 2025, was
presented by the military regime as a means of returning to constitutional order
and civilian administration with military embedded into a quarter of
Parliamentary seats. However, the conduct and underlying framework of the
elections have been widely questioned.
1.4 Reports indicate that major political parties were deregistered or otherwise
excluded, voting was limited to certain areas, and political activity remained
tightly controlled.1 As a result, there is significant uncertainty as to whether the
election can be regarded as free, fair, or inclusive.
1.5 The inquiry’s terms of reference examine the current state of democracy and
human rights in Myanmar, with a particular focus on the phased elections that
concluded in January 2026. The report also considers Australia’s role in
supporting democratic outcomes in Myanmar and the provision of
humanitarian assistance to support the people of Myanmar.
Referral of the inquiry
1.6 On 25 November 2025, the Senate referred an inquiry into democracy and
human rights in Myanmar to the Senate Foreign Affairs, Defence and Trade
References Committee (the committee) for report by 28 February 2026.2
On 5 February 2026 the Senate agreed to extend the reporting date to
31 March 2026.3

Background
The history of democracy in Myanmar
1.17 The parliamentary republic established after Myanmar’s independence in 1948
lasted until 1962, when the civilian government was overthrown by a military
coup and replaced with a socialist regime under the rule of General Ne Win.6
1.18 The first general election since 1962 was held in 1990 and gave
the National League for Democracy (NLD), led by Ms Aung San Suu Kyi, the
daughter of the nationalist leader who fought for Myanmar’s independence, a
landslide victory. Ms Suu Kyi was placed under house arrest before the vote and
the election results were rejected by the junta.7 Ms Suu Kyi spent most of the
following twenty years under house arrest in Yangon until the government
released her in 2010.8
1.19 Myanmar held a general election in November 2015, the first in decades to be
broadly regarded as credible. The NLD won by a huge margin. Although
Ms Suu Kyi was constitutionally barred from becoming president9, she was
appointed as State Counsellor and Foreign Minister.10
1.20 While the NLD government enjoyed strong public support, it was criticised for
slow progress on reforms and on advancing peace efforts amid Myanmar’s
long-running ethnic conflicts. The NLD also faced significant international
condemnation for failing to stop mass atrocities against the Rohingya which
drove an estimated 700 000 people to flee the country between 2016 and 2018.11
1.21 Another sweeping NLD electoral victory in 2020 saw the military re-seize
control in another coup. On 1 February 2021, under the command of
Senior General Min Aung Hlaing, the military seized control of Myanmar,

International views on democracy in Myanmar
1.27 Myanmar’s complex ethnic composition, prolonged internal conflict, and
extended periods of military rule have attracted significant international
attention. This section of the report examines the international community’s
views on the history of democracy in Myanmar.
Australia
1.28 The Australian Government expressed concerns regarding the ongoing political
and humanitarian crisis in Myanmar. Australia maintains its condemnation of
the widespread human rights abuses and violations by the military regime.18
1.29 DFAT outlined the four key objectives of Australia’s development and
humanitarian assistance in Myanmar in its submission to this inquiry:
 Objective 1: Support efforts to mitigate conflict and its effects, and protect
space for longer-term progress on human rights, rule of law, democratic
space, and gender equality and social inclusion.
 Objective 2: Build resilience in vulnerable communities by supporting
health, education, livelihood and climate change adaptation needs.
 Objective 3: Build the leadership and skills of the people of Myanmar and
strengthen our people-to-people links.
 Objective 4: Effectively respond to the humanitarian needs of those affected
by conflict, disasters and displacement.19
1.30 Australia has imposed sanctions on 21 individuals and seven entities associated
with Myanmar's military regime as part of its response. Australia's sanctions
include an arms embargo, targeted financial sanctions (including on commercial
entities with direct links to the military and on two state-owned banks) and
travel bans.20
1.31 In response to the 2025–26 general election, Australia asserted:
We are seriously concerned that elections held without these conditions will
lead to greater instability and prevent a peaceful resolution to the ongoing
crisis, with grave consequences for the people of Myanmar. The current
situation in Myanmar falls short of what is needed for free, fair and inclusive
elections.21

European Union
1.33 Following the 2021 coup, the European Union (EU) condemned the military
takeover and called on the junta to end human rights violations. The EU
continues to withhold recognition of the military regime in Myanmar and is a
major donor of humanitarian aid to the population.23
1.34 The EU Parliament adopted a resolution on 27 November 2025 regarding the
general election in Myanmar, calling for a ceasefire and rejecting the election as
illegitimate. The EU Parliament:
Rejects as illegitimate any elections organised unilaterally by the junta, in
particular the general election scheduled for December 2025 and
January 2026, which is in clear violation of democratic standards; stresses
that no credible electoral process can take place under conditions of
repression, mass displacement, continued arbitrary detentions, and the
absence of inclusive political dialogue; denounces any results of these
illegitimate elections that represent the junta’s attempt to gain international
legitimacy…24
1.35 Additionally, the EU Parliament stated that any pathway towards true
democracy requires the military junta to 'end the violence and relinquish power,
allow a civilian transitional authority, restore democratic institutions and
reinstate legitimate civilian leaders'.25

Chapter 2
A fragile democracy
2.1 Myanmar is in a geographically and geopolitically complex, resource rich area
which armed groups have competed for over many years. As outlined in
Chapter 1, the country’s long history of political instability is marked by only
brief periods of democratic civilian government with military personnel
constitutionally embedded into Parliament. For most of Myanmar’s modern,
post-independence history, power has remained firmly in the hands of the
military that demanded assimilation of the minority ethnic groups to
the dominant ethnic and cultural group (Bamar).
2.2 The current conflict is deeply entrenched, arising from decades of military
domination and the persistent denial of civilian political rights, alongside
long-standing struggles by ethnic groups seeking protection, freedom and
independence from the harsh regime seeking assimilation at all costs.
2.3 Efforts to assist the people of Myanmar are further complicated by the political
and economic support the military regime receives from China. This support
enables the regime to withstand pressure from the international community.
2.4 Myanmar’s military regime cast the 2025–26 election as a step toward stabilising
the country’s political environment and restoring democracy. Many
stakeholders and independent analysts, however, have serious doubts about the
conduct of the election and concerns about whether the process can be
considered democratic at all.
2.5 This chapter examines evidence relating to the 2025–26 general election in
Myanmar, the escalation of violence against civilians, the worsening
humanitarian situation and Australia’s response. It outlines key concerns raised
by witnesses and submitters regarding the election’s legitimacy, the impact of
ongoing conflict and the delivery of humanitarian assistance.
2.6 The chapter also considers Australia’s role in supporting democracy in
Myanmar and concludes with the committee’s views and recommendations.

The legitimacy of the 2025–26 general election
2.7 Five years after the 2021 coup, Myanmar's junta has concluded its first general
election in three phases.1 The election was held amid continuing internal
conflict, widespread displacement and a deepening humanitarian crisis. These
circumstances raised significant concerns that the election was held less as a
genuine attempt to return to democracy and more as a mechanism to legitimise
the military's rule.
2.8 Following the atypical month-long election process, it was reported that
the Union Solidarity and Development Party (USDP), the military-backed
party2, had claimed victory. The USDP's victory was widely anticipated.
Evidence received by the committee emphasised the lack of a viable opposition
alternative as a result of the National League of Democracy (NLD) being
dissolved and senior leaders of the NLD remaining imprisoned since
the 2021 coup.3
2.9 It was further reported that that a President will be selected when Parliament
convenes in March 2026.4
2.10 The legitimacy of the election was questioned from the outset by the
international community, with several governments identifying that an election
held under the current turbulent circumstances in Myanmar falls short of what
is required for it to be considered a free and fair election.5

Committee view
Legitimacy of the election
2.131 The committee is of the view that the Australian Government needs to more
firmly declare its position on the legitimacy of the regime currently presiding
over Myanmar. The committee concludes that the constitutional and legal
framework governing the 2025–26 general election severely undermined its
democratic legitimacy. Conducted under the military drafted 2008 Constitution,
the election entrenched military power through guaranteed parliamentary
representation, control of key ministries, and barriers to constitutional reform.
These structural constraints, combined with restrictive election laws that
suppressed dissent and excluded major opposition parties and leaders, ensured
a process that limited genuine competition or political choice.
2.132 The committee is concerned by the low and uneven voter participation.
Evidence shows that polling did not occur in large parts of the country due to
ongoing conflict, leaving many communities, displaced persons and refugees
unable to vote. Only around one third of eligible voters participated, a
significant decline from the 2020 election. The committee was also concerned to
hear that some citizens were coerced into voting by threats of punishment,
detention and forced conscription.
2.133 Overall, the committee notes the strong consensus among submitters that the
election was neither free, fair, nor inclusive, and was widely regarded as a
'sham'. Submitters consistently argued that the election was not intended to
facilitate a transition to civilian democratic government, but rather to entrench
military rule. In the committee's view, conditions marked by restricted freedom
of movement, expression, and association cannot result in a genuine democratic
process.
Recommendation 1
2.134 The committee recommends that the Australian Government:
 maintain its clear and principled objection to the continuing military rule
of Myanmar and its rejection of the 2025–26 elections and attempts to use
the 'sham' elections to build a cloak of respectability for the military
regime; and
 actively object to any attempts to legitimise the military regime,
normalise military rule and the acceptance of Myanmar into diplomatic,
parliamentary and regional or multilateral forums.
40
Recommendation 2
2.135 The committee recommends that the Australian Government:
 more regularly state its ongoing concern about the conflict in Myanmar
and continue to draw attention to the sustained repression, violence, and
human rights abuses perpetrated by the military regime;
 recognise that continued conflict in Myanmar is now entering a new and
more complex phase as it evolves from not just a humanitarian challenge
to a strategic one, recognising the challenge now intersects with regional
security, migration pressures and transnational crime networks; and
 maintain its policy of avoiding direct engagement with the military
regime.
Sanctions
2.136 While the committee appreciates the importance of diplomacy and statements
made condemning human rights abuses, the committee notes with concern that
the sanctions Australia has imposed have failed to keep pace with our
international partners. By failing to ensure that sanctions policy is determined
through close cooperation and alignment with international partners, in
the committee's view, Australia risks rendering its response both morally
insufficient and strategically ineffective.
2.137 The committee is of the view that lagging behind international partners in the
application of sanctions against the regime in Myanmar creates gaps which
undermine the collective pressure sanctions place on the military regime. It is
the committee’s strong view that aligning sanctions policy with global partners
is not a matter of political symbolism—it is the difference between targeted
economic isolation and a porous system which can be exploited and
circumvented. By harmonising sanctions with allies Australia can directly
contribute to, and materially strengthen, the leverage available to the
international community in response to large-scale human rights abuses.
Recommendation 3
2.138 The committee recommends that the Australian Government work towards
aligning its Myanmar-related sanctions with key allies such as the
United States of America.
41
Recommendation 4
2.139 The committee recommends that the Australian Government impose
sanctions against Myanmar's Central Bank and other banks controlled by the
military regime in Myanmar.
Escalating airstrikes on civilian populations
2.140 The committee is gravely concerned by the escalation of airstrikes against
civilian populations, including attacks on residential areas, schools, hospitals,
religious sites, and camps for internally displaced persons, despite their
protected status under international humanitarian law. The growing use of
armed drones and light aircraft has further intensified risks to civilians,
particularly following the announcement of the election.
2.141 The committee is deeply saddened by the scale of civilian casualties, including
women and children. The committee considers the strong and consistent
evidence from witnesses that the military's reliance on air power is central to its
campaign against civilians, and that urgent measures, particularly disruption
and sanctions on jet fuel supply, are required to prevent further loss of life.
Recommendation 5
2.142 The committee recommends that the Australian Government seek to disrupt
and sanction the supply of jet fuel to the Myanmar junta and government.
Human trafficking and transnational crime
2.143 The committee is concerned that conflict-driven instability has enabled the
growth of Myanmar’s illicit economy. Evidence indicated that Myanmar now
ranks globally as having both extremely high criminality and very low
resilience, with trafficking, drug production, cyber-scam operations, and
money-laundering networks expanding across the region. The committee notes
in particular the heightened risks of human trafficking in Rakhine State.
2.144 The committee considers that strengthening cooperation through established
regional mechanisms, especially the Bali Process on People Smuggling,
Trafficking in Persons and Related Transnational Crime, would provide a
practical and effective avenue for coordinated action. The committee also sees
value in exploring additional Myanmar-focused initiatives with key regional
partners, including India.
42
Recommendation 6
2.145 The committee recommends the Australian Government supplement its
engagement with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations by:
 strengthening engagement through other regional mechanisms such as
The Bali Process on People Smuggling, Trafficking in Persons and
Related Transnational Crime; and
 exploring new additional Myanmar-specific regional initiatives with
countries such as India.
Humanitarian assistance
2.146 The committee recognises the escalating humanitarian crisis in Myanmar, with
evidence indicating that nearly one third of the population will require
humanitarian assistance in 2026. Ongoing conflict, mass displacement, and
restrictions imposed by the junta have severely constrained humanitarian aid
access, particularly in areas outside military control. Evidence received by
the committee showed strong support for delivering assistance through cross
border channels, local NGOs, and ethnic and civil society organisations to reach
the most vulnerable populations.
2.147 The committee is concerned by the widening humanitarian funding gap, which
has been exacerbated by the withdrawal of aid from the United States and other
international donors. Evidence indicated that this withdrawal has intensified
pressure on long standing Rohingya refugee camps and for refugee
communities along the Thai-Myanmar border. The committee is particularly
concerned that limited resources may result in premature refugee repatriation.
2.148 While Australia cannot address this shortfall alone, the committee notes
consistent evidence that Australia has an important regional leadership role and
should work with like-minded partners to consider increasing humanitarian
and development assistance to Myanmar.
2.149 The committee considers it essential that Australia’s humanitarian response to
the crisis in Myanmar keeps pace with the scale and urgency required on the
ground. While recent funding commitments represent a significant and
welcome step, the committee is concerned that key initiatives have yet to be
finalised or publicly articulated. The committee also considers it important to
establish a Civil Society Pooled Fund to strengthen Australia's support to NGOs
and community-based organisations providing operational assistance.
43
2.150 The committee recognises the rapidly evolving humanitarian conditions across
Myanmar and in border communities displaced in Thailand, India, and
Bangladesh and believes a more urgent review of Australia's support would be
beneficial. The committee also notes that clear operational principles are needed
to guide development partners when engagement with entities linked to the
military regime is unavoidable, ensuring that assistance is delivered responsibly
and in line with Australia’s values and legal obligations.
2.151 The committee considers that greater flexibility in the delivery of
Australian humanitarian assistance is required in Myanmar's highly volatile
environment. Evidence highlighted that a more flexible system is essential to
ensuring that Australian aid can be provided safely and effectively.
The committee supports a more flexible framework and recognises that locally
led organisations can often provide better support to civilians in conflict
situations.
Recommendation 7
2.152 The committee recommends the Australian Government:
 prioritise the finalisation and publication of initiatives contained in the
announcement of the $370 million over three years to address the
humanitarian crisis in Myanmar and support Rohingya refugees and host
communities in Bangladesh, including establishing a
Civil Society Pooled Fund;
 bring forward of the proposed mid-cycle review of the
Australia-Myanmar Development Partnership Plan 2024-2029 to reflect the
rapidly increasing humanitarian needs arising in Myanmar and border
communities in Thailand, India and Bangladesh;
 prioritise the development of the publication of clear operational
principles to guide non-avoidable engagement between development
partners and entities associated with the military regime;
 demonstrate how the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade due
diligence framework is working to facilitate greater access, flexibility and
use of locally led organisations in the delivery of Australia's
humanitarian program in Myanmar.
44
Recommendation 8
2.153 The committee reiterates the recommendations made in the committee's
report into the Efficacy of the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade due
diligence framework. In particular, recommendation 1 of that report which
urges greater flexibility in Australia’s delivery of aid, recognising that locally
led organisations can often better deliver aid in conflict situations.
Educational opportunities
2.154 Evidence to the committee highlighted that Australia could strengthen
educational opportunities for Myanmar nationals working to advance
democracy and human rights, particularly through the
Myanmar Australia Awards program and related regional initiatives. In
addition to the Myanmar Australia Awards program, the committee considers
it important for the Australian Government to also support resettlement
pathways and alternative visas that enable Myanmar asylum seekers and
refugees to pursue education, research, and employment opportunities.
Recommendation 9
2.155 The committee recommends the Australian Government increase
resettlement opportunities and alternative visas for education, research and
employment opportunities for Myanmar asylum seekers and refugees and
expand the Myanmar Australia Awards program.
Review
2.156 Given the rapidly evolving situation and the breadth of Australia’s engagement
with Myanmar the committee considers it important that the Parliament is kept
regularly informed of progress to ensure Australia’s actions remain aligned with
its strategic objectives, policy settings and work with ASEAN.
Recommendation 10
2.157 The committee recommends that the Australian Government provide a
quarterly statement to Parliament on its response to the ongoing conflict in
Myanmar and include:
 progress on the implementation of the Australia-Myanmar Development
Partnership Plan 2024-2029;
 progress of initiatives undertaken with the Association of Southeast
Asian Nations;
 the monitoring and compliance of Australia's sanctions regime; and
 engagement with the Myanmar diaspora and
National Unity Government.

Engagement with the Myanmar diaspora
2.158 The committee expresses its appreciation to the Myanmar diaspora
organisations and individuals who provided submissions and appeared at its
public hearings. Their insights were vital in informing the committee’s
understanding of the evolving situation in Myanmar. The committee
acknowledges the courage and commitment shown by diaspora representatives
in sharing their firsthand experiences and considers ongoing engagement with
these groups essential for informing Australia’s future policy responses.
Recommendation 11
2.159 The committee recommends the Australian Government formalise its
engagement with Myanmar diaspora groups to access the extensive first-hand
knowledge, experience and networks these groups can bring to sustaining
Australia's response to the ongoing conflict in Myanmar.
