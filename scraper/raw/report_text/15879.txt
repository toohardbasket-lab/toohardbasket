List of recommendations
Recommendation 1
6.36 The committee recommends that a revised draft National Environmental
Standard for Matters of National Environmental Significance be released for
further public consultation.
Recommendation 2
6.37 The committee recommends that a revised draft National Environmental
Standard for Environmental Offsets and the proposed components of any
offsets calculator be released for further public consultation.
Recommendation 3
6.38 The committee recommends that the statement of reasons provided for a
decision under the amended Environment Protection and Biodiversity
Conservation Act 1999 should provide sufficient detail to permit any
interested parties to understand the legal, scientific and factual basis of the
decision and be drafted in plain English language.
Recommendation 4
6.39 The committee recommends that the Minister for the Environment and Water
issue principles or guidance as to the circumstances, relevant factors, and / or
thresholds that will inform and guide the application of the national interest
exemption and the national interest approval pathway under the
Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999.
Recommendation 5
6.40 The committee recommends that the National Environmental Standards
should facilitate clear, consistent, timely, and effective decision-making
under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999
and to provide clear standards and benchmarks to allow the National
Environmental Protection Agency to enforce compliance.
Recommendation 6
6.41 The committee recommends that a consistent approach should be developed
for Key Threatening Processes to provide clear guidance on identifying,
listing, and classifying key threats, the development of threat abatement
plans, and monitoring and reporting on threat and abatement progress, as
well as processes for review.
xiii
Recommendation 7
6.42 The committee recommends that the Department of Climate Change, Energy,
the Environment and Water prepare a document that identifies and sets out
the priority and relationship of the various statements, guidance documents,
policy papers, and other elements that guide and inform decision-making
under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999.
Recommendation 8
6.43 The committee recommends that any policy papers prepared to supplement
National Environmental Standards should include the intended or
anticipated application to case studies or examples.
Recommendation 9
6.44 The committee recommends that the Restoration Contributions Holder
should outline the principles to be applied and process to be followed in
assessing and calculating the quantum required to offset relevant impacts
(including an offset calculator) as soon as possible.
Recommendation 10
6.45 The committee recommends that the State of the Environment Report should
report on the enduring conservation outcomes and impact achieved through
offset and restoration initiatives undertaken by proponents and the
Restoration Contributions Holder, including outlining where conservation
outcomes have not been achieved and the status of any offset initiatives
waiting to be secured.
Recommendation 11
6.46 The committee recommends that the Department of Climate Change, Energy,
the Environment and Water should engage in further public consultation
about the appropriate timing, staging, calculation, and revision of the
quantum of offset amounts to be paid by proponents to the Restoration
Contribution Holder.
Recommendation 12
6.47 The committee recommends that the revised National Environmental
Standard for Environmental Offsets should incorporate feedback about:
- (a) the way in which the proposed maintenance periods (from 25 to 100
years) will be implemented and monitored and the appropriateness of
these periods;
- (b) how the maintenance periods will be aligned and administered in
relation to state Crown leases of varying lengths; and
xiv
- (c) how the management rules will accommodate and apply to self-
sustaining offset outcomes.
Recommendation 13
6.48 The committee recommends that the draft National Environmental Standard
for Environmental Offsets should provide clear and detailed guidance on the
availability of non-standard offsets, clarify whether offsets can be deemed
capable of commencement prior to the action, and clarify the nature, scope
and application (and any variance) of the ‘like-for-like’ offset rule.
Recommendation 14
6.49 The committee recommends that the suite of National Environmental
Standards be urgently progressed.

Chapter 1
Introduction and background
Referral and conduct of the inquiry
1.1 On 30 October 2025, the Senate referred the following bills to the Environment
and Communications Legislation Committee (the committee) for inquiry and
report by 24 March 2026:1
 Environment Protection Reform Bill 2025 (EPR bill);
 National Environmental Protection Agency Bill 2025 (National EPA bill);
 Environment Information Australia Bill 2025 (EIA bill);
 Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (Restoration Charge
Imposition) Bill 2025 (Restoration Charge bill);
 Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (General Charges
Imposition) Bill 2025 (General Charges bill);
 Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (Customs Charges
Imposition) Bill 2025 (Customs Charges bill);and
 Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (Excise Charges
Imposition) Bill 2025 (Excise Charges bill).
1.2 On 24 March 2026, the Senate granted the committee an extension of time to
report to 31 March 2026.2
1.3 These bills sought to deliver significant reforms to environmental laws through
three key pillars:
 stronger environmental protection and restoration;
 more efficient and robust project assessments and approvals; and
 greater accountability and transparency in decision-making.3
1.4 The committee called for submissions on the inquiry website, and invited
relevant organisations and individuals to make a submission by 5 December
2025. Late submissions focussed on the draft National Environmental Standards
were accepted by the committee.
1.5 In total, 689 submissions were received and published on the inquiry website.
The committee has also received approximately 4 600 campaign form letters, of

Overview of the bills
Purpose
1.14 The seven bills in the package implement the second stage of the Albanese
Government’s response to the Samuel Review. The Hon Tony Burke MP,
Minister representing the Minister for the Environment and Water, stated in his
second reading speech that the bill package 'remains faithful to [the Albanese
Government's] commitment to follow the spirit of the Samuel Review'.6
1.15 The bill package aims to streamline and improve Australia's national
environmental laws by delivering both improved environmental outcomes as
well as greater clarity and certainty for businesses. The Australian Government
has indicated that these reforms are long overdue and would make critical
changes to:
… adequately respond to the overall decline of the natural environment, to
support important national priorities like renewable energy, housing and
critical minerals and to gain the trust of the Australian community.7

National EPA bill
1.27 The National EPA bill proposes to establish the National EPA as an independent
statutory agency with regulatory functions—including assessment, monitoring,
auditing, compliance and enforcement functions—under the EPBC Act. The
National EPA would be led by a CEO who would be obligated to establish and
maintain a publicly available register of decisions and deliver annual reports to
the Minister. The National EPA would be subject to independent reviews every
five years.18
EIA bill
1.28 The EIA bill proposes to establish the Head of Environment Information
Australia as a statutory position within the department, with responsibility for
providing the Minister, the CEO of the National EPA, and the public with access
to environmental data and information. The Head of Environment Information
Australia would also be required to prepare and publish the State of the
Environment report; establish and maintain environmental economic accounts;
and maintain a public register of any national environmental information
assets.19

1.34 Prior to the passage of the EPR bills, the Australian Government’s primary
environmental legislation was the EPBC Act, which set out the legal framework
for environmental protection at the Commonwealth level. The EPBC Act passed
the Australian Parliament in 1999 and commenced in July 2000.

biodiversity and heritage, and promote ecologically sustainable development

requirements for reviews of decisions and requirements for annual reports and
five-yearly State of the Environment reports. Independent statutory reviews of
the Act’s operation and the extent to which its objects have been achieved are
also required, and are discussed below.

Second independent review of the Environment Protection and Biodiversity
Conservation Act 1999 (the Samuel Review)
1.56 Under the EPBC Act, an independent review is required to take place at least
every ten years.44 The second of these reviews was led by Professor Graeme
Samuel AC, which commenced in October 2019. The Samuel Review's final
report was provided to the then-Minister for the Environment in October 2020
and publicly released in January 2021.45
1.57 The Samuel Review found that the EPBC Act and its operation required
fundamental reform:
Australia's natural environment and iconic places are in an overall state of
decline and are under increasing threat. They are not sufficiently resilient to
withstand current, emerging or future threats, including climate change.
The EPBC Act is [outdated] and requires fundamental reform. It does not
enable the Commonwealth to effectively fulfil its environmental
management responsibilities to protect nationally important matters. The
Act, and the way it is implemented, results in piecemeal decisions, which
rarely work in concert with the environmental management responsibilities
of the States and Territories. The Act is a barrier to holistic environmental
management which, given the nature of Australia’s federation, is essential
for success.46
1.58 Professor Samuel put forward reforms to deliver nationally important outcomes
for the environment through:
 legally enforceable Standards, which would set the boundaries for decision-
making to deliver the protections needed;
 restoration of the environment and facilitation of the scale of investment
required to deliver better environmental outcomes;
 taking an adaptive approach through planning, measuring the effectiveness
of implementation and making adjustments where needed; and
 harnessing the knowledge of Indigenous Australians to inform
environmental management.47

2.15 The development and implementation of legally enforceable Standards were

The introduction of Standards
Making, varying and revoking Standards
2.21 The EPR bill inserted new provisions in the EPBC Act empowering the Minister

quickly take account of emerging threats to the environment, as well as changes

inviting public comment within a specified period of at least 20 business days.22

No-regression principle
2.27 Before varying or revoking a Standard, the Minister needs to be satisfied that
the variation or revocation does not reduce: (a) protection of the environment;
(b) the likelihood that environmental data or information is appropriate
(including as to the quality of the information); (c) the likelihood of appropriate
consultation or engagement; or (d) the likelihood that outcomes or objectives
specified in the Standard will not be achieved. The proposed change must also
meet any prescribed requirements.31
2.28 In this way, the Explanatory Memorandum explained that the EPR bill would
enshrine a no-regression principle in the EPBC Act to:
… ensure that [environmental] protections cannot be reduced or removed
by the variation or revocation of a national environmental standard; all
variations and revocations must ensure that such protections are either
maintained or improved.32

The introduction of the 'net gain' test
2.96 The EPR bill amended the EPBC Act to require that residual significant impacts
on protected matters be compensated to achieve a net gain.115
2.97 A 'residual significant impact' was defined as a significant impact on a protected
matter that will not be avoided, mitigated or repaired in the course of taking the
action or by complying with conditions attached to an approval.116 The
requirement to pass the net gain test applies to the residual significant impact of
an action, or class of actions.
2.98 The EPR bill did not define net gain, but provided the Minister with the power
to prescribe requirements relating to net gain or offsets for each protected matter
in regulations.117
2.99 The net gain test could be satisfied by the proponent either compensating for
damage by delivering an environmental offset or restoration action by paying a
restoration contribution charge or both,118 provided that compliance results in a
measurable net gain for the relevant protected matter.119
2.100 According to the Explanatory Memorandum to the EPR bill, the intent of this
provision was to ensure that:
Actions that will have, or are likely to have, residual significant impacts on
matters of national environmental significance will generally only be able to
approved if the taking of the action will leave the relevant protected matter
better off than it was before the action.120
2.101 The EPR bill sought to allow regulations to be made to prescribe 'the level of net
gain required' for a particular protected matter. Where this does not occur, the
required net gain would be a net gain the Minister is satisfied is appropriate.121

Chapter 3
More efficient and robust project assessments - the
second pillar
Overview
3.1 This chapter outlines the key functions contained in the Environment Protection
Reform Bill 2025 (EPR bill), and details the key provisions in the EPR bill, and
views on these from key bodies, in relation to the second pillar of reforms,
relating to efficient and robust project assessments, including streamlined
assessment pathways, landscape scale assessments, accreditation and bilateral
agreements, rulings, national interest considerations and related amendments.
3.2 The chapter focuses on key aspects and views on the reforms and does not
address every change contained in the EPR bill.
Second pillar reforms—more efficient and robust project assessments
3.3 The Australian Government has explained that the second pillar of proposed
environmental reform is intended to deliver 'a system which can better respond
to big national priorities like the renewable energy transition, a future made in
Australia and the housing that we need'.1
3.4 This section sets out:
 proposed amendments to the EPBC Act's assessment pathways;
 landscape scale approaches, including bioregional planning, development
and conservation zones, and strategic assessments;
 proposed amendments to accreditation and bilateral agreements;
 proposed national interest considerations;
 Ministerial and National EPA Chief Executive Officer (CEO) rulings; and
 proposed amendments to reconsideration of decisions provisions.
3.5 Key views on these are discussed at the end of the section.

3.42 A proponent would be able to undertake 'priority actions' in development
zones without requiring further approvals under the EPBC Act, if they have
registered the priority action and comply with any conditions contained in the
bioregional plan.
3.43 A development zone's boundaries would be determined with regard to certain
specified matters, such as a declared World Heritage property, National
Heritage places, ecological characteristics of a Ramsar Wetland or
Commonwealth marine park; and the location and habitat of any listed
threatened species and ecological communities, or migratory species.45
3.44 A bioregional plan must specify the classes of actions (a priority class of action)
that can be taken in the development zone, with each action in such a class a
priority action.
3.45 The department stated that, in certain circumstances, this process would avoid
the need for EPBC Act approval on a project-by-project basis. Further:
Any residual significant impacts from such developments will be
compensated to a net gain through a program of strategic offsets required
to be delivered under the plan. This planned approach to offsets achieves
better environmental outcomes than would be achieved by uncoordinated
offsets delivered under single project approvals.46
Conservation zones
3.46 Conservation zones are important protected areas for nature. Restricted actions
would not be able to be undertaken in a conservation zone, unless there is an
exemption.
3.47 A conservation zone's boundary must be specified with regard to:
 a declared World Heritage property;
 a National Heritage place;
 a declared Ramsar wetland;
 a Commonwealth marine area;
 the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park; and
 the location and habitat of any listed threatened species, any listed
threatened ecological communities, or any listed migratory species.47
3.48 The department stated that conservation zones are intended to ‘guide
development in a given sector away from areas of higher environmental
sensitivity towards areas of lower environmental sensitivity’, and stated that:

Accreditation and bilateral agreements
3.63 Most actions requiring approval under the previous EPBC Act require separate
approvals under relevant state or territory legislation. In order to minimise
duplication, the EPBC Act allowed the Commonwealth to enter into bilateral
agreements with states and territories to allow the Commonwealth to accredit
relevant state and territory processes, and to delegate the assessment of actions
which would otherwise require assessment under the EPBC Act.64
3.64 The department stated that the proposed amendments to accreditation and
bilateral agreements flowed on from recommendations in the Samuel Review,
which recommended that the framework for accreditation be improved, to
ensure that it is in line with the Standards and 'strengthened mechanisms for
ongoing assurance and oversight'.65
3.65 The department outlined that the recommendations of the Samuel Review
would be delivered by:
 Ensuring that environmental protections under bilateral agreements,
including National Environmental Standards, are consistent with the rest
of the reforms.
 Strengthening ongoing assurance mechanisms to ensure that
environmental protection requirements are being met.
 Improving flexibility by allowing accredited processes to incorporate
documents as in force from time-to-time and allowing minor
amendments to accredited processes without having to formally remake
the agreement. This means existing agreements are less like to 'break'
with changes in state laws.66
3.66 Prior to the EPR bill’s amendments, there were two types of bilateral
agreements:
 assessment bilateral agreements, which accredit a state or territory process
to assess the environmental impacts of a proposed action, and create a
single assessment process. After assessment, the proposed action requires
two separate approval decisions from the Commonwealth and relevant state
or territory framework; 67 and
 approval bilateral agreements, which accredit the assessment and approval
process of a state or territory. Proposed actions covered by an approval
bilateral agreement do not require further approval by the Commonwealth

Reconsideration of decisions
3.118 Prior to the EPR bill reforms, the EPBC Act provided for certain limited
circumstances in which the Minister may reconsider a decision about whether a
proposed action is a controlled action and which provisions of Part 3 are
controlling provisions (the first decision). Once the Minister has decided
whether to approve the taking of the action, or the action is taken, the Minister
is not able to revoke the first decision.137
3.119 The Minister may, however, revoke the first decision and substitute a new
decision for it, on certain grounds, including if the Minister is satisfied that
revoking and substituting the decision is warranted on the grounds of:
 the availability of substantial new information about the impacts that the
action has, will have, or is likely to have, on a protected matter (the SNI
ground);
 a substantial change in circumstances that was not foreseen at the time of
the first decision and relates to the impacts that the action has, will have, or
is likely to have, on a protected matter (the SCIC ground);
 if the first decision was that the action was not a controlled action because
the Minister believed the action would be taken in a particular manner
(NCA-PM decision), and the Minister is now satisfied that the action is not
being, or will not be, taken in that particular manner;
 if the first decision was that the action was not a controlled action because of
a provision of a bilateral agreement and a bilaterally accredited
management arrangement or a bilaterally accredited authorisation process
under that agreement, and the provision no longer operates in relation to
the action, or the management arrangement or authorisation process is no
longer in force;
 if the first decision was that the action was not a controlled action because of
a ministerial declaration and an accredited management arrangement or an
accredited authorisation process under that declaration, and the declaration
or management arrangement or authorisation process no longer operates in
relation to the action; or
 if the first decision was that the action was not a controlled action because of
a ministerial declaration and a bioregional plan to which the declaration
relates, and the declaration no longer operates in relation to the action, or
the bioregional plan is no longer in force.138

Chapter 4
Greater accountability and transparency in
decision-making - the third pillar
Overview
4.1 This chapter outlines the key functions contained in the Environment Protection
Reform Bill 2025 (EPR bill), and details the key provisions in the EPR bill, and
views on these from key bodies, in relation to the third pillar of reforms, relating
to accountability and transparency, including First Nations engagement,
information sharing, cost recovery and related amendments.
Third pillar reforms—greater accountability and transparency in decision-
making
4.2 The third pillar of proposed environmental reforms, relating to transparency
and accountability in environmental decision making, intended to ensure 'that
all Australians can have confidence in these laws', including delivering the
National EPA.1 This chapter includes:
 proposed provisions relating to the disclosure of greenhouse gas emissions;
 proposed amendments to the Indigenous Advisory Committee; and
 proposed amendments to statutory committees.

Compliance and enforcement
5.57 The EPR bill also proposed amendments to the EPBC Act relating to the
compliance, enforcement and penalty regime. It would do this by:
 introducing a new compliance tool, an Environment Protection Order, for
use in urgent circumstances to manage a contravention, or likely
contravention of the EPBC Act that creates an imminent risk of serious harm
to the environment, or where damage has already occurred;
 expanding what approvals and orders 'directed environmental audits' can
be applied to, including a new 'compliance audit' that could be conducted
without a requirement to provide prior notice;
 introducing a new register of independent auditors to conduct directed
environmental and compliance audits; and
 introducing a civil penalty formula for serious offences under the EPBC Act
and increasing maximum financial penalties for criminal offences.
5.58 Some of these key provisions are set out below.
The introduction of Environment Protection Orders
5.59 As set out earlier, the EPR bill introduced new powers for the CEO of the
National EPA to issue Environment Protection Orders that could be used to
address, prevent, treat and mitigate imminent significant environmental risks
and harm.63
5.60 Environment Protection Orders are intended for use in urgent circumstances
where there is an imminent threat of serious damage to a protected matter, or
where the damage has already occurred. These orders are based on comparable
orders used in the states and territories.64
5.61 Prior to issuing an Environment Protection Order, the Minister must reasonably

5.62 An Environment Protection Order would be revoked when the Minister
reasonably believes that it is no longer necessary for the purposes for which it
was issued, or would otherwise lapse if a timeframe is specified in the EPO.65

Environment Protection Order. A combination of fault-based and strict liability

Overview
6.1 This chapter outlines the committee’s view on the Environment Protection
Reform Bill 2025 (EPR bill) and six related bills, and draft National
Environmental Standards.
6.2 The committee acknowledges the considerable interest in the EPR bills, and
thanks all who provided evidence to the committee during its inquiry. The
committee appreciates the time and effort invested by submitters and witnesses
to participate in this inquiry through public hearings, submissions and
supplementary submissions. The urgent need for reform of Australia’s
environmental laws was a consistent theme of the evidence received.
6.3 The bills proposed significant reform to Australia’s environmental laws in order
to protect Australia’s unique natural environment for generations to come. The
introduction of the package of bills came after ‘five frustrating years’1 without
significant environmental reform progress. Professor Graeme Samuel AC,
whose independent review in 2020 laid the foundation for this legislative
reform, stated that:
… we have a real chance of halting the decline in the state of the
environment and biodiversity and tackling the restoration of species whose
existence has been allowed to decline to the state of near extinction over
recent decades.2

Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC Act) (the
Samuel Review) identified that the EPBC Act itself is a ‘barrier to holistic
environmental management’. Professor Samuel’s landmark review found that
the EPBC Act was outdated, and in need of fundamental reform to reduce
‘piecemeal decisions’ and protect nationally important matters.3
6.5 Professor Samuel’s suggested reforms to deliver nationally important outcomes
for the environment included:
 legally enforceable National Environmental Standards, which would set the
boundaries for decision-making to deliver the protections needed;
 restoration of the environment and facilitation of the scale of investment
required to deliver better outcomes;

National interest approval pathway
6.21 The committee agrees with the recommendation of the Samuel Review that
there is a need for the Minister to be able to exercise discretion in rare
circumstances to approve projects that do not meet the new environmental tests
(unacceptable impacts, Standards, and net gain tests), where it is demonstrably
in the public interest. The committee recognises that the proposed national
interest approval pathway responds to this recommendation.
6.22 However, the committee heard concerns from inquiry participants about the
risk that this new pathway could be misused to approve a range of high-impact
projects that are not genuinely in the national interest. The committee considers
that it would be appropriate to set guardrails around the use of the national
interest approval pathway and the national interest exemption to ensure that
they are only enlivened in rare circumstances, where there is a genuine need.

6.25 The committee supports the intent of the proposed provisions relating to

Accountability and transparency
6.26 The committee heard that there is strong support for the establishment of a
National EPA, which was highlighted by the Australian Climate and
Biodiversity Foundation as one of the strengths of the bills.18
6.27 The committee acknowledges that some submitters and witnesses expressed
concern that the National EPA model has varied from that proposed by
Professor Samuel AC. However, the committee is of the view that the model
proposed in the bills strikes an appropriate balance between the role of the
Environment Minister and the independence of the National EPA Chief
Executive Officer.
6.28 The proposed transparency and accountability requirements placed on the new
National EPA should provide confidence to the Australian community,
environmental groups, as well as industry, on its operation.
6.29 The strengthened compliance and enforcement framework proposed by the bills
package was also supported by submitters and witnesses, including increases in
penalty amounts. The proposed National EPA would have more effective
compliance powers than are currently available, and would be able to receive
and act on public reports of actions and proposals that may be in breach of the
legislation.
6.30 The committee notes that views on the scope of compliance and enforcement
powers were mixed. Some submitters cautioned that the proposed powers had
the potential to be unconstrained and would benefit from clear guardrails, while
others suggested that these should be complemented by stronger rules and
reforms to exemptions. Overall, the committee is of the view that the model
proposed by the bills package achieves an appropriate balance between

National Environmental Standards
6.32 The committee notes that two draft Standards have been released by the
department, and will require amendments to align them with the passed EPR

during its consultation process for both draft Standards. The committee also
notes that these draft Standards are the first of a suite that will be developed in
time. It is critical that the Government takes lessons from current consultative
processes and applies these to future ones.
6.33 The committee acknowledges the constructive contributions made by
individuals and organisations who have participated in the inquiry through
submissions and attendance at public hearings, and also through the
department’s and the Minister’s consultation process.
6.34 The committee considers that the Standards require ongoing consultation in
order to get the details right, and provide workable, efficient parameters to
guide development. The committee notes evidence that called for definitions to
be clear, and to have consistent language used in the Standards and associated
documents. The committee also recognises calls for greater clarity and certainty
about how the policy papers and Standards work together. The committee also
heard about the importance of consistent and predictable decision-making.
6.35 The committee heard that, due to the significance of the content of the

Australian Greens' Additional Comments
1.1 During negotiations to pass this suite of bills, while Labor wanted to continue
playing to industry with carve outs and loopholes, the Greens secured critical
wins to ensure that these reforms were better than the status quo and actually
worked to protect nature. These amendments ensure environment laws will
better protect native forests, close loopholes for land clearing and stop coal and
gas being fast-tracked.
1.2 However, there is still much more work to do to protect nature, and the Greens
urge the Government to stand up to industry and progress strong national
environmental standards that prioritise nature protection and restoration.
1.3 Strong national environmental standards are crucial to halting the rapid decline
of Australia’s wildlife and ecosystems. The reality is that when it comes to the
detail of these standards, as with the bills, there remain differing views from
environmental experts and industry. Given that the whole point of
environmental protection reform is to protect nature from destructive
industries, it is unlikely these differences will be resolved.
1.4 Instead, it is time for the Government to be clear about which side they are on—
they cannot and will not make everyone happy. Attempting to do so will only
lead to standards that neither protect nature nor provide any clarity to industry,
as was reflected in evidence related to the draft standards. Nature cannot wait
any longer.
1.5 Environmental and legal experts were clear that as they stand, these standards
lack the specificity and clarity to actually increase environmental protection on
the ground. Rather than focusing on process, the standards must include clear,
legally enforceable outcomes so that we can measure the impact of the standards
on protection and restoration of nature. This was a clear recommendation from
the Samuel Review, which also provided important guidance on the inclusion
and consideration of cumulative impacts as part of the Matters of National
Environmental Significance under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity
Conservation Act 1999 (MNES) standard. To ensure clarity for all interested
parties, the use of discretionary language and undefined terms that muddy the
waters of decision-making must be removed.
1.6 The purpose of the national environmental standards is that they are applied to
all relevant environmental decisions under the Act, however the Greens have
concerns that the Government is still seeking to water down requirements for
certain industries.

devastating impacts of fossil fuel extraction on water resources. State and
territory governments have shown time and time again that they will sacrifice

Lack of data and performance benchmarks
1.30 Looking ahead, the success of the new EPBC legislation will depend on a clear
evidence base and supporting data, including on environmental economic
accounts.
 This will be down to the agencies such as Environment Information
Australia and, in particular, the Data and Information Standard which is
still under development and does not exist at this time.
 Industry stakeholders from all sides have called for clear metrics when
assessing things like environmental offsets.
 To allow for timely development – especially housing development – there
is a need for measurable metrics, including statutory decision timeframes
without ‘stop clock’ provisions – which hold up housing projects,
transparent reporting and clear data on approval performance.
 Protecting the environment must be supported by strong and verifiable data
and evidence base, rather than ideology.
1.31 The proposed legislation does not focus on clear key performance indicators
(KPIs) that would allow scrutiny over whether the reformed EPBC Act is
working, including around approval timeframes. In line with feedback from
industry, clear, measurable and enforceable KPIs are urgently needed on areas
such as:
 the average approval time for housing or infrastructure projects under the
existing EPBC Act;
 the expected average approval time under the reformed framework;
 the maximum and minimum timeframes for decision making;
 measurable targets for improving approval efficiency.
1.32 The absence of such metrics makes it difficult to determine whether the reforms
will deliver faster and more efficient approvals.
 Any reform of national environmental law must include clear, publicly
reported metrics that allow Parliament to track performance and hold the
regulatory system accountable.

Undemocratic reliance on legislative instruments
1.39 The legislation establishes a framework that allows the Minister to make, vary
and revoke national environmental standards, as legislative instruments.
 These instruments will determine how the legislation operates in practice,
including how environmental standards are interpreted and how offsets or
restoration contributions are calculated.
 While legislative instruments are subject to disallowance motions, they do
not receive the same level of scrutiny as primary legislation.
1.40 This raises important democratic concerns. Major policy decisions affecting
environmental protection, housing development and national infrastructure
should be debated and legislated by Parliament rather than determined through
Ministerial decree.
 Industry representatives advised that draft standards should be road tested
using realistic project case studies before they are finalised.
 Without such testing, there is a risk that the standards could produce
unintended consequences when applied to development proposals.

1.48 It is clear that the federal government’s quarter of a century incursion into
environmental protection has not been able to get the balance right between
preservation and investment. Labor’s latest effort to amend EPBC is more
uncertainty, more bureaucracy and more paralysis. The extent of the delegation
of power is not only fuelling uncertainty, it is undemocratic.

Finding 2
1.49 The environmental approval system is conspiring against housing construction.

1.50 Australia is closed for business. There are long delays in infrastructure, energy
and resources development projects across Australia. This has cruelled
Australia’s capacity to maintain economic and strategic autonomy in times of
crisis such as during the current Middle East oil shock. Environmental
approvals have taken gas and oil projects up to 8 years to get off the ground.

and for conservation groups - in terms of what needs to be protected and what
areas can be safely developed. Key concepts in the proposed framework,
including the “unacceptable impact” test and new environmental standards,
remain unclear and have not been adequately tested for real world workability,
including in terms of driving investment.
Finding 5
1.52 The Environment Protection Reform Bill 2025 contains no clear performance
benchmarks or key performance indicators to measure whether approval times
will improve. Further there is a lack of clear data and evidence to help with
approval decisions.

standards reduces parliamentary scrutiny and shifts key policy decisions away
