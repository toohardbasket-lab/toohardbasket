1. Introduction
1.1 This review is undertaken pursuant to paragraph 29(1)(cf) of the Intelligence Services
Act 2001 (IS Act) which provides that it is a function of the Parliamentary Joint
Committee on Intelligence and Security (Committee) to commence a review, by the
second anniversary of the commencement of the Migration Amendment (Clarifying
International Obligations for Removal) Act 2021 (CIOR Act), of the operation,
effectiveness and implications of the amendments made by Schedule 1 of that Act.
1.2 The second anniversary of the commencement of the CIOR Act was 25 May 2023.
Conduct of the review
1.3 The Committee initially commenced its review on 11 May 2023 and requested
submissions from interested stakeholders by 23 June 2023. The Committee received
10 submissions from government and civil society stakeholders. The Committee did
not hold any public hearings.
1.4 The initial review lapsed when the House of Representatives was dissolved and
parliament prorogued for national elections on 28 March 2025.
1.5 Following the formation of the new Parliament and appointment of a new Committee,
the present Committee re-initiated the review on 3 November 2025. Pursuant to
clause 8 of Schedule 1 to the Intelligence Services Act 2001, the Committee took into
consideration the evidence received during the previous Parliament. The Committee
also received two supplementary submissions from the Department of Home Affairs,
responding to requests for information made by the Committee.
1.6 Appendix A sets out a list of all submissions received.
1.7 Copies of the submissions and other material relevant to the inquiry can be accessed
at the Committee’s website.1

Legislative history
1.9 On 25 March 2021 the Migration Amendment (Clarifying International Obligations for
Removal) Bill 2021 (CIOR Bill) was introduced into the House of Representatives by
then Minister for Immigration, the Hon Alex Hawke MP. In his second reading
speech, the Minister stated that the CIOR Bill would clarify Australia’s international
obligations relating to non-refoulement in the removal of unlawful non-citizens:
The Migration Amendment (Clarifying International Obligations for Removal) Bill
2021 amends the Migration Act to clarify that, in line with Australia's international
obligations relating to non-refoulement, the removal power in the Migration Act
does not require or authorise removal of a person where they have been
assessed as engaging those obligations.
Furthermore, the bill improves our ability to assess if a person engages
protection obligations, regardless of whether or not they fail to satisfy other
criteria for the grant of a protection visa.2
1.10 At the time of its introduction, the CIOR Bill was not referred to this or any other
committee for inquiry. However, its provisions were considered by the Senate
Standing Committee for the Scrutiny of Bills3 and the Parliamentary Joint Committee
on Human Rights.4
1.11 On 12 May 2021 the then Government moved amendments to the CIOR Bill to
append a new Schedule 2. Schedule 2 inserted a new clause in the IS Act to require
the Committee to review the operation, effectiveness and implications of the
provisions of Schedule 1 after two years of their operation. The review was to
consider whether the provisions:
are effective in ensuring that the removal of an unlawful non-citizen who has
been found to engage Australia’s protection obligations through the protection
visa process is not required or authorised, unless relevant exceptions apply.5
1.12 The Committee’s review was also intended to apply to the merits review provisions
inserted by items 3A to 3F in Schedule 1 of the Bill.6

Migration Amendment (Clarifying International
Obligations for Removal) Act 2021
1.13 Section 198 of the Migration Act 1958 (Migration Act) provides for the removal from
Australia of ‘unlawful non-citizens’, including mandating their removal ‘as soon as
reasonably practicable’ in certain circumstances including where the person does not
hold or is denied a relevant visa.
1.14 Section 197C of the Migration Act, as in force prior to the CIOR Act amendments,
provided that:
(1) For the purposes of section 198, it is irrelevant whether Australia has
non refoulement obligations in respect of an unlawful non citizen.
(2) An officer’s duty to remove as soon as reasonably practicable an unlawful

‑

1.15 The CIOR Act was intro‑duced following a number of court decisions which found that
section 197C of the Migration Act operated to require the removal of an unlawful non-
citizen who had been assessed as engaging non-refoulement obligations, including
to the country where they faced a real risk of suffering significant harm. The
Government confirmed that this was inconsistent with both the original intent of the
Migration Act and Australia’s international obligations.8
1.16 The purpose of the CIOR Act was to clarify that the duty to remove under the
Migration Act should not be enlivened where to do so would breach non-refoulement
obligations, including Australia’s obligations under the:
• 1951 Convention relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol
(Refugee Convention)
• International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)
• Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or
Punishment (CAT).9
1.17 A further purpose of the CIOR Act was to give non-citizens access to merits review in
the Migration and Refugee Division of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (now the
Administrative Review Tribunal: ART), in circumstances where the Minister decides
that an unlawful non-citizen no longer engages protection obligations.10

Section 36A: Consideration of protection obligations
1.19 The CIOR Act inserted section 36A into the Migration Act which requires that the
Minister (or their delegate), when considering an application for a protection visa,
consider as a first step whether Australia owes any protection obligations to the
person. Subsection 36A(1) provides that the Minister must consider and make a
record of whether they are satisfied of any of the following:
• The person satisfies the refugee criterion under paragraph 36(2)(a) with respect to
a country and satisfies the security criterion under subsection 36(1C) that the
applicant is not a person whom the Minister considers, on reasonable grounds, is
a danger to Australia’s national security or, having been convicted by a final
judgement or a particularly serious crime, a danger to the Australian community.
• The person satisfies the complementary protection criterion under paragraph
36(2)(aa) with respect to a country: that is, the Minister has substantial grounds
for believing that, as a necessary and foreseeable consequence of the non-citizen
being removed from Australia to a receiving country, there is a real risk that the
non-citizen will suffer significant harm.
• The person satisfies the refugee criterion under paragraph 36(2)(a) but does not
satisfy the security criterion under subsection 36(1C), but would also satisfy the
complementary protection criterion under paragraph 36(2)(aa) if they did not
meet the refugee criterion.

1.23 Subsections 197C(4) to (7) set out the circumstances in which a protection finding is
made:
• Where a record is made under section 36A that the Minister is satisfied a
protection visa applicant is a person Australia has protection obligations over with
respect to a particular country.
• Where the Minister is satisfied, however expressed and even impliedly, that the
person satisfies the refugee criterion or the complementary protection criterion
with respect to a particular country, including if they did not satisfy other criterion
or are otherwise ineligible for grant of the visa.
• Where a protection finding is made for a person in respect of a particular country
on one of the above grounds, and the minister is satisfied that although the
person may have a right to enter and reside in a third country, they have a well-
founded fear of persecution or real risk of significant harm in that country, or have
a well-founded fear that the third country would return them to another country in
which this would arise.

Section 197D: Decision that a protection finding would no longer be made
1.24 The CIOR Act inserted a new section 197D into the Migration Act. Section 197D,
specifically subsection 197D(2), permits the Minister (or their delegate) to make a
decision that they are satisfied that an unlawful non-citizen is no longer a person in
respect of whom a protection finding would be made. This ultimately means the
unlawful non-citizen can be removed under section 198 of the Migration Act to the
country by reference to which a protection finding had previously been made.14
1.25 Subsection 197D(4) requires that if the Minister makes such a decision, the person
must be notified in writing of the decision, the reasons for it, and the person’s review
rights in relation to the decision.
Part 7: Reviewable decisions
1.26 Part 7 of the Migration Act provides for review by the ART of specified decisions
made under the Act. The CIOR Act amended the Migration Act to make decisions
under subsection 197D(2) reviewable.
1.27 The amendments included:
• Inserting decisions under subsection 197D(2) into the definition of ‘Part 7
reviewable decisions’ in subsection 411(1)15
• Inserting a requirement into section 419 that the ART must notify the applicant of
its decision on a review of a subsection 197D(2) decision, within the prescribed
period.16

2.6 The CIOR Act was enacted following two court judgements: DMH16 v Minister for
Immigration and Border Protection (2017) 253 FCR 576 (DMH16) and AJL20 v
Commonwealth of Australia [2020] FCA 1305 (AJL20). The findings of these two
court decisions altered the effect of section 197C of the Migration Act on persons
who had been found to engage protection obligations.
• In DMH16, the Federal Court found that section 197C as then in force obliged the
Department to remove an unlawful non-citizen where it was reasonably
practicable to do so, even where the person had been found to engage Australia’s
non-refoulement obligations.3
• In AJL20, the Federal Court ordered the release from immigration detention of the
same individual, who it found had no ongoing matters before the Department of
Home Affairs, Minister or the Courts and had not been removed from Australia as
soon as reasonably practicable (in circumstances where removal may have been
inconsistent with non-refoulement obligations).4

which held that a failure to comply with the duty under section 198 of the
Migration Act did not make continued immigration detention unlawful, but it did
compel officers to comply with the duty to remove the person ‘as soon as
reasonably practicable’.5

Ministerial intervention and reviewable decisions
2.13 As outlined in Chapter 1, the CIOR Act inserted section 197D into the Migration Act
which permits the Minister (or their delegate) to make a decision that a person
subject to a protection finding is no longer a person in respect of whom a protection
finding would be made. The CIOR Act also inserted decisions under subsection
197D(2) into Part 7 of the Migration Act, allowing for review of such decisions by the
Administrative Review Tribunal (ART).
2.14 A number of submitters expressed concerns with the broad power given to the
Minister by section 197D.9 For example, the Law Council of Australia said:
There are no criteria prescribing circumstances in which this power may be
exercised. This is inconsistent with the principle of the rule of law that the law
must be both readily known and available, and certain and clear. While the Law
Council notes that it was anticipated that a decision made under this power would
be ‘rare’ - that ‘rarity’ is not reflected in the legislation. The exercise of this power
will be a matter entirely at the discretion of the Minister.
The provisions do not currently provide for key procedural fairness guarantees
with respect to the decision under new subsection 197D(2). That is, it appears
that the affected person will only be informed about the decision after it is made.
This is inconsistent with the Law Council’s policy with respect to the application of
the rule of law to asylum seekers, that protection determination processes must
include procedural fairness guarantees, such as the right to be notified, and to
present and challenge evidence where adverse decisions are made. The Law
Council’s Rule of Law Policy Statement also provides that Executive decision
making should comply with the principles of natural justice.10
2.15 The Law Council recommended that subsection 197D(2) be repealed. If not
repealed, the Law Council recommended that the provision be amended to make
clear the rare circumstances in which the Minister may revisit the decision, and to
require that procedural fairness be provided to the affected person.11
2.16 These views were shared by the Australian Human Rights Commission (AHRC),
which similarly commented that the language in section 197D is too broad and
recommended that it be amended to identify specific grounds on which a person
might have their protection finding reviewed:
Section 197D is framed in such broad language that no particular objective
criteria need to be demonstrated. It is sufficient if the Minister is satisfied that if
the extensive protection visa application process were to commence again, the
person would not be found to be owed protection obligations this time around. In

Interplay of CIOR Act with other sections of the Migration Act
2.26 Several submitters commented on the interoperability of sections of the CIOR Act
with other sections of the Migration Act. A key interplay focused on by submitters was
that between the extant subsections 197C(1) and (2) and the new subsections
inserted by the CIOR Act.
2.27 As mentioned in Chapter 1, subsection 197C(1) states that for the purposes of
section 198, which outlines the circumstances in which an officer must remove an
unlawful non-citizen from Australia, it is irrelevant whether Australia has non-
refoulement obligations in respect of an unlawful non-citizen. Subsection 197C(2)
provides that an officer’s duty to remove as soon as reasonably practicable an
unlawful non-citizen under section 198 arises irrespective of whether there has been
an assessment, according to law, of Australia’s non-refoulement obligations in
respect of the unlawful non-citizen.
2.28 Both the AHRC and the Law Council of Australia noted tensions between
subsections 197C(1) and 197C(3) of the Migration Act. AHRC submitted that:
On its face, s 197C(1) is inconsistent with the Government’s policy, which is now
reflected in s 197C(3). That is, if Australia has non-refoulement obligations in
respect of an unlawful non-citizen, this is relevant for the purposes of the duty of
removal in s 198. The relevance is that the person should not be removed to a
place where they have a well-founded fear of persecution or other serious harm.
The inconsistency created by subsections (1) and (3) leaves open the possibility
of officers misunderstanding their duties and powers.23
2.29 The Law Council recommended redrafting the section so that it is clear when a
person will engage Australia’s non-refoulement obligations.24 The AHRC
recommended that subsection 197C(1) be repealed.25
2.30 The AHRC also submitted that section 197C(2) is unnecessary now that section 36A
requires an assessment of any non-refoulement obligations before considering any
other eligibility criteria for a visa.26

international obligations relating to non-refoulement to the country from which they

3. Committee comment
3.1 Pursuant to paragraph 29(1)(cf) of the Intelligence Services Act 2001 (IS Act), the
Committee was required to review the operation, effectiveness and implications of
the Migration Amendment (Clarifying International Obligations for Removal) Act 2021
(CIOR Act).
3.2 The purpose of the CIOR Act was to ensure the Migration Act 1958 (Migration Act) is
consistent with Australia’s non-refoulement obligations. The Committee observes that
the CIOR Act is not national security legislation, and that it is questionable whether
this was the best-placed committee to conduct the review. The Committee’s
comments and recommendation in that regard are discussed further at the end of this
chapter.
3.3 The Committee has nevertheless undertaken the present review in good faith and the
following sections comment on issues raised by those who submitted to the inquiry.
Non-refoulement obligations
3.4 The CIOR Act amended the Migration Act to clarify that the power to remove an
unlawful non-citizen does not require or authorise an officer to involuntarily remove a
person to a country where a protection finding has been made in relation to the
person with respect to that country.
3.5 As noted in Chapter 2, the Department of Home Affairs (Department) confirmed in its
submissions that no unlawful non-citizens with a protection finding have been
removed contrary to Australia’s non-refoulement obligations since the CIOR Act
commenced.
3.6 On this basis, the Committee agrees that the CIOR Act has achieved its purpose.
3.7 The Committee also notes that, in support of this objective, the CIOR Act introduced
provisions which ensure that protection obligations are always assessed as part of
the protection visa process, including before the Minister considers whether the
person meets other criteria for the grant of a protection visa.
3.8 The Committee acknowledges that some submitters expressed concerns that the
CIOR Act was too narrow in scope and a risk remains that Australia may continue to
breach non-refoulement obligations for some people, such as those precluded from
accessing the protection visa process. Nevertheless, noting that the scope of this
review was to look at the effectiveness of the CIOR Act, the Committee assesses
that it is effective on its terms in ensuring that Australia meets its non-refoulement
obligations for the intended cohort.
19
Arbitrary and indefinite detention
3.9 As discussed in Chapter 2, concerns were raised by many submitters about the
potential for indefinite detention of a person in respect of whom a protection finding is
made under the Act and who is therefore unable to be returned to their country of
origin.
3.10 In this respect it is pertinent that, during the time the Committee was undertaking this
review, significant legal developments took place. Notably, the High Court of
Australia handed down its judgment in the case of NZYQ v. Minister for Immigration,
Citizenship and Multicultural Affairs & Anor [2023] (NZYQ), which determined that
indefinite immigration detention was unlawful where there was ‘no real prospect’ of
the person’s removal from Australia in the reasonably foreseeable future.
3.11 Following the NZYQ decision, Parliament passed the Migration and Other Legislation
Amendment (Bridging Visas, Serious Offenders and Other Measures) Act 2023,
which amended the Migration Act to provide for additional conditions to be placed on
bridging visas granted to non-citizens released from immigration detention.1 The
Explanatory Memorandum for that legislation confirmed many in the cohort impacted
by the NZYQ decision are individuals in respect of whom Australia has non-
refoulement obligations:
The NZYQ-affected cohort is made up of people who have been refused grant of
a visa, or had their visa cancelled, and who are on a removal pathway but who
have no real prospect of removal becoming practicable in the reasonably
foreseeable future. In many cases, the person has a protection finding, within the
meaning of section 197C of the Migration Act, which prevents their removal to
their country of citizenship or habitual residence and there is currently no other
country to which their removal can be effected. A ‘protection finding’ reflects the
circumstances in which Australia has non-refoulement obligations with reference
to a person. In other cases, removal is not practicable in the foreseeable future
for other reasons, including where the person is stateless and their country of
former habitual residence is not willing to accept their return.2
3.12 In light of these legal developments during the course of its review, it is no longer
necessary or relevant for the Committee to respond to the evidence submitted in
relation to the CIOR Act and indefinite detention.

Ministerial intervention and reviewable decisions
3.13 The Committee notes the concerns raised by some submitters about the insertion of
section 197D into the Migration Act by the CIOR Act, providing the Minister with an
‘own motion’ authority to effectively reverse a protection finding previously made for a
person. The Committee notes the advice of the Department that it would expect the
197D power to be used only in rare circumstances—and that it had been used on
eight occasions as at 20 February 2026.
3.14 In this regard the Committee draws attention to the evidence received from the
Department regarding safeguards on this power, including the insertion of section
197D decisions into Part 7 of the Migration Act, allowing these decisions to be
reviewed by the Administrative Review Tribunal. The Department confirmed that all
eight individuals in relation to whom 197D decisions have been made have been
given procedural fairness, and six of those individuals have chosen to seek merits
review at the Administrative Review Tribunal.
3.15 As such, the Committee makes no recommendations in relation to section 197D.
Need for a broader review
3.16 Some of the evidence provided to the Committee in this review focused on broader
concerns with the Migration Act and Australia’s immigration detention regime.
3.17 As noted above, the Committee was tasked with reviewing the operation,
effectiveness and implications of the amendments made by the CIOR Act. As such
the Committee considers that these broader issues fall out of scope of the current
review.
3.18 The Committee makes no comment on whether further review of the Migration Act is
warranted, beyond the views elaborated below regarding the appropriate forum for
any future parliamentary committee consideration of such matters.
Migration-related amendments and future parliamentary review
3.19 As noted in Chapter 1, the placement of a provision into the Intelligence Services Act
2001 requiring the Committee to review the CIOR Act did not follow a review of the
CIOR Bill by this Committee. The amendments made by the CIOR Act do not directly
engage the intelligence agencies whose oversight is this Committee’s main function,
nor do they deal with national security powers and functions which form the basis of
the Committee’s other functions under the IS Act.
3.20 In its Annual Report of Committee Activities 2020-2021, the Committee observed that
the addition of the CIOR Act review to the Committee’s functions within the IS Act
was not formally advised to the Committee. The Committee recommended that the
appropriate Minister notify the Committee of any amendment to the IS Act affecting
the role or functions of the Committee, when it does not derive from a Bill reviewed
21
by the Committee or a government response to a Committee report.3 A government
response to the Committee’s Annual Report of Committee Activities 2020-2021 was
presented in June 2024, which noted this recommendation.
3.21 The Committee considers that this legislation would have been more appropriately
referred for review to a parliamentary committee with direct responsibility and
experience relating to the Migration Act and international human rights obligations,
such as the Joint Standing Committee on Migration, the Parliamentary Joint
Committee on Human Rights or the Senate Standing Committee on Legal and
Constitutional Affairs. A review by this Committee may have been intended to allow
for classified information to be provided which could not have been provided to one
of those committees. However, in practice, the Department chose not to provide any
classified information in its response to the Committee’s enquiries.
3.22 The Committee therefore recommends that in future, review of these provisions or
any related matters should be referred to a parliamentary committee with a relevant
mandate in relation to immigration issues.
Recommendation 1
3.23 The Committee recommends that any future review of the operation,
effectiveness and implications of the amendments made by the Migration
Amendment (Clarifying International Obligations for Removal) Act 2021, or
related issues of migration, protection and human rights, be referred to an
appropriate parliamentary committee such as the Joint Standing Committee on
Migration, the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Human Rights, or the Senate
Standing Committees on Legal and Constitutional Affairs.
