Chapter 1
Background
1.1 Public trust in government’s handling of sensitive information is essential for
safety and the effective functioning of government. However, this must be
balanced with transparency, accountability, and access to information.1
1.2 Commonwealth legislation contains numerous secrecy provisions that prohibit
the disclosure of sensitive information held by government, where
unauthorised disclosures or other dealing may cause harm to essential public
interests, such as national security and the safety of the public.2
1.3 While these secrecy laws mainly apply to current and former Commonwealth
officers and service providers, they can also apply to members of the public,
including journalists.3
1.4 Since 2005, there has been a downwards trend in referrals of potential breaches
of secrecy provisions to the Commonwealth Director of Public Prosecutions.
Prior to that, since 1985 there have been prosecutions in relation to 18 secrecy
provisions in 11 laws, particularly under taxation, social security and law
enforcement legislation.4
1.5 To ensure that Australia’s secrecy offence framework remains just and
proportionate, as well as relevant to the current Australian context, a number of
reviews of secrecy offences have been conducted in recent years. The two most
recent are the reviews of the secrecy offence framework undertaken by the
Attorney-General’s Department (AGD) in 2023 and the Independent National
Security Legislation Monitor (INSLM or the Monitor) in 2024. Both are discussed
in depth later in this chapter.5

Reviews of Australia’s secrecy framework
1.13 As noted above, the bill will implement recommendations of the reviews of
secrecy offence frameworks undertaken by the AGD in 2023 and the INSLM in
2024, both discussed below.10
Review by the Attorney-General’s Department
1.14 The AGD released a report of its review of secrecy provisions in November 2023,
Review of Secrecy Provisions, Final Report, which was undertaken to address
concerns about the number, consistency, appropriateness and complexity of
Commonwealth secrecy offences.11
1.15 The review specifically looked at secrecy offences that are no longer required
after the enactment of the National Security Legislation Amendment (Espionage and
Foreign Interference) Act 2018, the compliance of the secrecy offences framework
with relevant principles, the balance between appropriate secrecy sanctions and
public interest and, any amendments required to protect public interest
journalism.12
1.16 In its final report, the review concluded that while ‘open and accountable
government is fundamental to our democracy’, secrecy offences ‘play a
legitimate and important role in protecting the confidentiality of certain
Commonwealth information’. The review made an overarching
recommendation that the Australian Government should:
… apply a more consistent approach to the framing of secrecy offences going
forward, and that consideration be given to reducing the overall number of
secrecy offences, whilst ensuring that the broad range of information held
by the Commonwealth is appropriately protected from unauthorised
disclosure.13
1.17 Specific recommendations included to:
 Adopt a set of 12 principles to be used to frame Commonwealth secrecy
offences moving forward, that would:
− Limit secrecy offences to clear public interest needs.
− Prefer general offences over multiple specific ones.
− Use a harms-based approach.
− Cover current and former officials, clearly define conduct and third
parties.

Review by the Independent National Security Legislation Monitor
1.18 The INSLM is a statutory office holder who independently reviews Australia’s
national security and counterterrorism laws and can make recommendations for
law reform. The INSLM undertook an independent review of the secrecy
offences in Part 5.6 of the Criminal Code Act 1995 (Cth) (Criminal Code) between
December 2023 and May 2024. The INSLM released a report of the review’s
findings and recommendations, Secrecy Offences, in May 2024.15
1.19 The recommendations of the review were crafted to ‘ensure that information
which is or is likely to be harmful to critical national interests remains protected
but in a way that is clearer and more consistent with the rule of law and concepts
of necessity and proportionality; and with Australia’s international
obligations’.16
1.20 A total of 15 recommendations were made. The Australian Government
responded in November 2024, agreeing fully, in part or in principle to 12
recommendations, noted two and did not agree to one recommendation to
narrowing ‘deemed harm’ offences for intelligence and national security related
information, to bring them into line with offences for other categories of
information.17
1.21 Agreed and noted recommendations included:
 Narrowing the types of information covered by deemed harm offences, to
ensure that it does not rely on a broad security classification to be deemed
harmful, only covers intelligence-related information of intelligence and

Key provisions of the bill
1.23 As discussed earlier in this chapter, the Attorney-General noted that ‘Australia's
secrecy framework has evolved into a complex patchwork of provisions—while
some are essential, others are outdated and broader than necessary’. The bill
‘makes comprehensive reforms to [Australia’s] secrecy framework in response
to these findings’.19
1.24 The four main changes proposed to be enacted are:
 The bill reduces by more than one third the total number of Commonwealth
secrecy provisions that attract a criminal sanction, by repealing or removing
criminal liability from more than 300 secrecy provisions across various
Commonwealth legislative instruments.
 At the same time, the bill also strengthens secrecy provisions by introducing
a new targeted secrecy offence in the Criminal Code to target improper
communication and use of information to obtain a benefit or cause a
detriment.

Schedule 1
1.26 Schedule 1 repeals the existing section 122.4 of the Criminal Code. The schedule
would:
 remove criminal liability for certain Commonwealth officials’
communications that breach non-disclosure duties;
 preserve criminal liability for 16 non-disclosure duties in Commonwealth
Acts; and
 introduce a new secrecy offence capturing improper use or communication
of information by certain Commonwealth officials and persons with a
relationship with the Commonwealth to obtain, or seek to obtain, a benefit
or to cause detriment to a Commonwealth entity or other person.22
Schedule 2
1.27 Schedule 2 makes amendments to existing Commonwealth secrecy offences and
non-disclosure duties in other legislation. The schedule would repeal a number
of secrecy offences and non-disclosure duties and remove criminal liability from
secrecy offences. In effect, this converts these offences to non-disclosure duties
without an attached criminal liability. These secrecy provisions were identified
following the 2023 AGD’s review of the secrecy offence framework, as no longer
requiring criminal liability ‘either as the offences are no longer required or
alternative administrative or civil sanctions were more proportionate to protect
the information’.23

ICCPR: Right to liberty and freedom from arbitrary detention and right to be tried
without delay
1.34 The ICCPR provides that:
 Everyone has the right to liberty and security of person and that no one
shall be subjected to arbitrary arrest or detention – Article 9(1).
 Anyone who is arrested shall be informed, at the time of arrest, of the
reasons for their arrest and shall be promptly informed of any charges
against them – Article 9(2).
 Anyone arrested or detained on a criminal charge shall be brought
promptly before a judge or other officer authorised by law to exercise
judicial power and shall be entitled to trial in a reasonable time or release –
Article 9(3).
 Everyone shall be entitled to minimum guarantees in the determination of
any criminal charge against them, including the right to be tried without
undue delay – Article 14(3).30
1.35 The bill engages the right to freedom from arbitrary detention and the right to
be tried without delay as it requires the consent of the Attorney-General to
prosecute a journalist, or relevant media staff for secrecy offences. A person can
be arrested, charged and remanded in custody or on bail, but no further steps
can be taken without consent. This individualised assessment prior to
prosecution may prevent any unwarranted deprivation of liberty.31
1.36 The bill, in subsection 123.6(3), makes it clear that if there is a significant delay
between a person’s arrest, charge, remand or release, and the decision of the
Attorney-General regarding a prosecution, a person may be discharged and
released. This would ensure that the requirement for consent does not limit the
right of a detained person to a trial in a reasonable time or release.32
1.37 The limitation to rights is reasonable, necessary and proportionate to ensure
only appropriate prosecutions proceed against journalists and media staff.33

Right to privacy
1.38 The ICCPR provides that no-one shall be subjected to arbitrary or unlawful
interference with his or her privacy, nor to unlawful attacks on their honour and
reputation – Article 17.
1.39 While the bill engages the right to privacy, it does not interfere with a person’s
right to have their personal information treated confidentially and in accordance
with privacy provisions. The bill retains privacy obligations but replaces
criminal penalties for privacy breaches with existing civil and administrative
frameworks.34
Right to freedom of expression
1.40 The ICCPR provides that all individuals have the right to freedom of expression,
including the right to seek, receive and impart information of all kinds – Article
19(2). Restrictions on this right to freedom of expression is permitted to protect
the rights and reputations of others, or for the protection of national security,
public order, public health or morals – Article 19(3).35
1.41 Secrecy provisions limit freedom of expression, by imposing criminal liability
on certain persons who impart certain information. By removing criminal
liability from more than 300 secrecy provisions, the amendments promote the
right to freedom of expression by ensuring that secrecy provisions only attract
criminality where this is necessary and proportionate to protect the most
sensitive information.36
1.42 Criminal liability will remain for disclosing certain types of information, such
as sensitive healthcare, personal and commercial information, to respect the
rights or reputation of individuals to whom the information relates, and to
protect public trust in government to appropriately handle and disclose this
information.37
1.43 The bill creates a new secrecy offence of the ‘improper use or communication of
information, by certain Commonwealth officials and persons with a relationship
with the Commonwealth, to obtain, or seek to obtain, a benefit or to cause, or
seek to cause, detriment to a Commonwealth entity or other person’. This new
provision is more narrowly targeted than the existing offences, as it only applies
where a Commonwealth official improperly uses information to gain a benefit
or cause detriment, in breach of expected standards of conduct. The existing

Additional rights impacts
1.44 The bill extends secrecy offences for Commonwealth officials to persons that
have provided services to the Commonwealth, as recommended by the review
of the INSLM into secrecy offences. These changes hold these persons to the
same higher standard for information-handling as Commonwealth officers.
These offences apply to protect sensitive Commonwealth information,
including in relation to national security and law enforcement. The offences
ensure the equal treatment of persons who manage information which, if
handled improperly, could harm key public interests in the protection of
national security and public order.39
Consideration by the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Human Rights
1.45 The Parliamentary Joint Committee on Human Rights (PJCHR) made comments
on the bill in its report of 7 May 2026. The PJCHR noted that the amendments to
repeal offences or remove criminal liability for offences that protect the privacy
of personal information, these measures engage and may limit the right to
privacy. The committee found that there was limited information in the
explanatory memorandum to assess the impact of these measures in relation to
the right to privacy for each specific offence amended by the bill.40

Chapter 2
Comments on the bill
2.1 As noted in Chapter 1, the Secrecy Provisions Amendment (Repealing Offences)
Bill 2026 (the bill), will implement recommendations of two recent reviews of
Australia’s secrecy offence frameworks. The first was undertaken by the
Attorney-General’s Department (AGD) in 2023, Review of Secrecy Provisions, and
other was the review by the Independent National Security Legislation Monitor
(INSLM or the Monitor) in 2024, Secrecy Offences.
2.2 The key amendments proposed by the bill include:
 The repeal and removal of criminal liability from over 300 secrecy
provisions via amendments to the Criminal Code Act 1995 (Criminal Code).
 The creation of a new general secrecy offence.
 Measures related to press freedom such as:
− the requirement for ministerial consent to the prosecution of journalists,
and
− raising the threshold for criminal culpability of journalists compared to
Commonwealth officials.
 Amendments in response to the INSLM Secrecy Review, including to:
− Establish a single material threshold of harm for secrecy offences in the
Criminal Code.
− Clarify definitions for ‘dealing with’ information offences and removing
the ‘dealing with’ offence for members of the public.
− Clarify circumstances for aggravated offences.
− Amend the Criminal Code to ensure the secrecy offences did not have
elements that were reliant on security classification under a policy
framework.
− Repeal the proper place of custody offences.
− Require the Defence Intelligence Organisation (DIO) mandate to be made
public.1
2.3 While generally supportive of the amendments to be made by the bill,
submitters made substantive comment on some of the proposed changes.
Primarily in that the changes did not go far enough, or some of the
recommendations of the various reviews of the secrecy offence framework were
not included in this tranche of reforms.

Overarching response to the bill
2.5 The majority of comments on the bill were supportive, acknowledging the
significant improvements made to Australia’s secrecy offence framework by the
proposed changes. While some submitters argued that the changes did not go
far enough in enacting reform, they generally did not disagree with the direction
of the proposed changes.
2.6 Most submitters acknowledged the very delicate balancing act of enacting
legislation that can both protect secrecy necessary to national security and the
functioning of government, while ensuring appropriate public scrutiny and
oversight.
2.7 Dr John Coyne, Director of National Security Programs with the Australian
Strategic Policy Institute (ASPI), noted this tension between the need for secrecy
and transparency in a health democracy focused on both social cohesion and
national resilience:
Government decision making should be transparent, and officials and
politicians should be able to be held to account for abuses of power and even
mistakes of judgment. The threats facing Australians and investments in
capabilities to prevent or mitigate those threats should be explained.
Further, the risk of mere political or bureaucratic embarrassment should not
justify a failure to disclose. However, secrecy in the fields of intelligence,
security and law enforcement operations is inherently necessary for public
safety, national sovereignty and international affairs … There should always
be a distinction between that which would be publicly interesting but
required to be concealed and that which is in the public interest and able to
be revealed.2
2.8 The INSLM submitted that overall, ‘the Bill represents a substantial and
welcome reform of the Commonwealth secrecy framework’. The INSLM noted
the bill repeals a large number of ‘unnecessary security offences’ and makes a
number of other changes to retained offences that will ‘materially improve
clarity, proportionality and consistency with the rule of law, and are strongly
supported’. However, the INSLM also noted that the bill ‘only partially
implements several key recommendations of the Monitor’ and requires further
justification and refinement in these areas to ensure full alignment with the
principles of necessity and proportionality’.3
2.9 The Alliance for Journalists’ Freedom (AJF) submitted that it ‘is delighted to see
the major amendments in the Bill that will better protect the right for journalists
to report freely and safely’. However, it also noted concerns ‘about several

Comments on provisions in the bill
Reducing the number of security offences
2.22 The bill repeals or removes criminal liability from over 300 secrecy provisions,
which would represent a reduction of more than a third of all Commonwealth
secrecy provisions that attract criminal liability. This would be achieved by:
 Part 1 of Schedule 1 of the bill which repeals secrecy offences in the
Criminal Code.
 Part 1 of Schedule 2 which would repeal specific secrecy offences.
 Part 2 of Schedule 2 which allows for the conversion of specific secrecy
offences into non-disclosure duties that do not attract criminal liability.19
2.23 Schedule 1 of the bill would repeal the existing section 122.4 of the Criminal
Code which ‘provides that it is an offence for a Commonwealth officer to
communicate information that they made or obtained as part of their
employment, despite being under a duty not to disclose that information and
where that duty arises under Commonwealth legislation’. This offence currently
criminalises all non-disclosure duties for Commonwealth officers or a person
engaged to work for a Commonwealth entity. There are currently over 300 non-
disclosure duties across various pieces of legislation that enliven this offence.20

2.62 Multiple organisations raised concerns that the new offence would have a

2.63 ARTK argued that the new offence may impose a chilling effect on public

in scope as compared to the original version. The committee concluded that this

of expression inherent in offences that criminalise the communication of
information’.63 As such, any limitations of rights to freedom of political
communication are likely to be reduced by the bill, not increased.

What this Bill does
1.63 This Bill amends Part 5.6 of the Criminal Code, which contains the
Commonwealth's general secrecy offences.
1.64 The Committee's report notes that the Bill implements the Independent National
Security Legislation Monitor's recommendation that the Attorney-General’s
consent be required for prosecutions under Part 5.6 of the Criminal Code. The
Bill also extends that concept more broadly in relation to prosecutions of
journalists.
1.65 Schedule 3 would place existing journalist protections on a statutory footing and
significantly expand them. Under proposed section 123.6, the Attorney-
General's consent would be required before a journalist, or an administrative
staff member of a news organisation, could be prosecuted for a secrecy offence
under any Commonwealth law.
1.66 For present purposes, Commonwealth secrecy law can be understood as
operating at two levels. The distinction matters because the Bill treats each
differently.
1.67 The first level consists of specific secrecy offences contained in individual Acts
dealing with areas such as taxation, social security, intelligence and financial
regulation.
1.68 Each offence is a complete criminal provision with its own elements and
penalty. For example, taxation legislation contains offences relating to the
disclosure of protected tax information. According to the Government, there are
569 such secrecy offences and the Bill leaves most of them untouched.
51
1.69 The second level consists of approximately 300 Commonwealth non-disclosure
duties scattered across more than 100 Commonwealth laws.
1.70 These duties are not, of themselves, criminal offences. Rather, they are legal
obligations imposed on those who hold particular information.
1.71 What currently gives criminal effect to many of those duties is the general
secrecy provision at section 122.4 of the Criminal Code.
1.72 That provision operates as a general offence, making it an offence punishable by
up to two years' imprisonment for Commonwealth officers, and certain other
persons who obtained information through work performed for, or services
provided to, a Commonwealth entity, to disclose information in breach of a
Commonwealth non-disclosure duty.
1.73 The origins of section 122.4 are relevant.
1.74 In 2018, the Coalition Government repealed section 70 of the Crimes Act 1914,
which had criminalised unauthorised disclosures for many decades. Without a
replacement provision, hundreds of existing non-disclosure duties would have
ceased to attract criminal consequences.
1.75 On the recommendation of the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Intelligence
and Security, section 122.4 was enacted as a transitional measure to preserve
criminal liability while the broader secrecy framework was reviewed and
rationalised. It was also made subject to a sunset clause.
1.76 That review process culminated in the Attorney-General's Department Review of
Secrecy Provisions (2023) and the Independent National Security Legislation
Monitor's Review of Secrecy Offences (2024), both of which inform the reforms
contained in this Bill.
1.77 The Bill makes three principal changes.
1.78 First, it removes criminal liability from more than 300 secrecy provisions across
Commonwealth legislation.
1.79 At the first level, the Bill repeals or downgrades a comparatively small number
of existing secrecy offences. One example is section 92 of the Sex Discrimination
Act 1984, which creates an offence relating to the disclosure of complaint
information. The Australian Human Rights Commission supported repeal of
that provision in its evidence to the Committee.
1.80 Where a secrecy offence is downgraded, the underlying obligation may remain.
A person may still be prohibited from disclosing information, but breach of that
obligation will no longer itself constitute a criminal offence.
1.81 Most of the change occurs at the second level. The Bill repeals the existing
section 122.4, with the consequence that most non-disclosure duties currently
relying upon that provision will cease to carry automatic criminal consequences.
52
1.82 Some of the provisions affected appear to be outdated. Examples include secrecy
obligations applying to officials of the former Australian Broadcasting Tribunal,
a body abolished more than three decades ago.
1.83 Importantly, repeal of criminal liability does not necessarily remove the
underlying obligation. In many cases, disciplinary, contractual or
administrative consequences may continue to apply. Public servants may
remain subject to the APS Code of Conduct and contractors may remain subject
to contractual obligations. However, breach will no longer automatically
constitute a criminal offence.
1.84 Secondly, the Bill repeals and replaces section 122.4 with a new general secrecy
offence.
1.85 Thirdly, the Bill introduces a statutory Attorney-General consent requirement
before journalists may be prosecuted for secrecy offences, significantly
expanding the current arrangements.
1.86 At present, section 123.5 of the Criminal Code requires the Attorney-General's
written consent before a person may be committed for trial for certain Part 5.6
offences. That protection applies to all defendants, not only journalists.
However, it operates at a relatively late stage of proceedings and does not apply
where matters are dealt with summarily.
1.87 The current law does not contain a dedicated statutory protection for journalists.
Instead, protection is provided through a ministerial direction issued in 2019,
requiring the Attorney-General’s consent before journalists may be prosecuted
for specified secrecy offences.
1.88 The Bill would place that protection into legislation and extend it substantially.
Under proposed section 123.6, the Attorney-General’s consent would be
required before a journalist, or an administrative staff member of a news
organisation, could be prosecuted for a secrecy offence under any
Commonwealth law, whether the matter proceeds summarily or on indictment,
subject to any exceptions prescribed by regulation.
1.89 In practical terms, the safeguard would extend beyond the secrecy offences in
Part 5.6 to the hundreds of secrecy offences that remain throughout
Commonwealth legislation, including in taxation, intelligence and social
security laws.
1.90 However, the proposed provision retains one feature of the existing regime. A
journalist may still be investigated, arrested and charged before Attorney-
General consent is obtained. This issue is discussed later in this report.
1.91 The practical effect is that a prosecution against a journalist for a
Commonwealth secrecy offence cannot proceed unless the Attorney-General
first gives consent.
53
Old offence vs new offence
1.92 As set out so far, a non-disclosure duty is a rule, not a crime.
1.93 What turns a breach of the rule into a crime is the existing section 122.4 of the
Criminal Code, which provides, in the relevant part:
122.4 Unauthorised disclosure of information by current and former
Commonwealth officers etc.
(1) A person commits an offence if:
(a) the person communicates information; and
(b) the person made or obtained the information by reason of his or her
being, or having been, a Commonwealth officer or otherwise engaged to
perform work for a Commonwealth entity; and
(c) the person is under a duty not to disclose the information; and
(d) the duty arises under a law of the Commonwealth.
Penalty: Imprisonment for 2 years.
(2) Absolute liability applies in relation to paragraph (1)(d).
1.94 Its operation is mechanical.
1.95 If one of those duties exists somewhere in a Commonwealth law and a person
who obtained the information through their Commonwealth work discloses it
in breach of the duty, they commit an offence carrying up to two years’
imprisonment.
1.96 Currently, the prosecution does not have to prove that the disclosure harmed
anyone or anything and it does not have to prove why the person acted.
1.97 The breach of the rule is itself the crime.
1.98 A public servant who breaches a duty today therefore faces two tracks at once:
discipline under the APS Code of Conduct, up to and including dismissal, and
criminal prosecution under section 122.4.
1.99 The principal reviews relied upon by the Government identified concerns with
a framework that criminalised disclosures regardless of their consequences.
1.100 A leak that endangers lives and a leak of something entirely harmless are crimes
in exactly the same way.
1.101 This Bill tries to rectify this seeming imbalance by repealing that offence.
1.102 The duties remain, with discipline and contractual remedies still applicable, but
the breach itself ceases to be a crime.
1.103 In many cases, a breach of a non-disclosure duty would be dealt with through
employment, disciplinary, contractual or administrative mechanisms rather
than through prosecution.
1.104 The Code of Conduct requires employees to comply with all applicable Australian
laws, so breaching a duty is itself a breach of the code.
54
1.105 What follows is a workplace process rather than a court process.
1.106 The agency investigates and may impose sanctions that range from a reprimand
or a fine deducted from salary, through reduction in classification or salary, to
dismissal.
1.107 Contractors face the equivalent consequences under their contracts.
1.108 One limit should be noted, and the Department conceded it in evidence.4
1.109 The Code binds only current employees.
1.110 Once a public servant has left the service, there may be little or no remedy unless
an enforceable contract applies.
1.111 That matters where the relevant duty is no longer backed by a criminal offence.
1.112 In its place this Bill enacts a new section 122.4, which in substance applies where:
 a person uses or communicates information, and
 the person obtained it because they were a Commonwealth officer, were
engaged to perform work, or provided services (paid or unpaid) to a
Commonwealth entity, other than the ABC or SBS; and
 the person intends to obtain a benefit, or to cause a detriment; and
 it would be reasonable to conclude that the use or communication is
improper.
1.113 The new offence does not ask whether a duty was breached. It asks three
different questions:
 whether the person used or communicated information obtained through
their connection to the Commonwealth;
 whether they intended to obtain a benefit or cause a detriment; and
 whether a reasonable person would conclude the use or communication
was improper.
1.114 If the answer to each is yes, the person commits an offence, again carrying a
maximum of two years’ imprisonment.
1.115 It follows then that after this Bill passes, a person cannot be prosecuted for
breaching a duty alone.
1.116 Yet the same disclosure can still reach a criminal court as set out above.

Harm the Bill cannot see at present
1.211 The foundation of ASPI’s case is a distinction the Bill overlooks, namely that
democratic accountability judges secrecy after the event while national security
has to act before it.
1.212 Intelligence officers, defence officials, police investigators and diplomats protect
information before an outsider can see why it matters, and by the time a court
or a journalist or the Government can point to measurable harm, the source may
already be compromised, the method already exposed and the partner’s
confidence already lost.
1.213 Many of the most serious harms can never be proven in public at all, because
foreign intelligence services do not announce what they have learnt and
adversaries do not reveal how they used a leak, so the absence of visible harm
is not the same as the absence of harm.
1.214 That insight is the key to everything else ASPI says, because a Bill that treats
harm as real only once it can be demonstrated after the fact builds the central
offence on a test that the most damaging disclosures will always defeat.
1.215 ASPI does not argue against reducing unnecessary secrecy, and it accepts that
Australia should improve transparency and strengthen protection for legitimate
journalism and whistleblowing, but it warns that a secrecy regime cannot rest
on hindsight alone without becoming a system that recognises harm only after
the damage is done.

The gap at the serious end
1.227 ASPI's principal recommendation addresses what it considers to be a significant
gap in the proposed offence.
1.228 The new section 122.4 focuses on whether a person intended to obtain a benefit
or cause a detriment. ASPI argued that this places emphasis on motive rather
than on the seriousness of the harm that may result from a disclosure.
1.229 ASPI submitted that a person who releases classified information for
ideological, political or personal reasons may cause the same damage to national
security, intelligence capabilities or international relationships as a person
acting for financial gain. It pointed to the possibility of a disclosure motivated
by conviction rather than gain, where significant harm may occur despite the
absence of any provable intention to obtain a benefit or cause a detriment.
1.230 ASPI also noted that the Attorney-General's Department had previously
proposed an offence directed at disclosures prejudicial to the effective working
of government, and argued that the decision not to proceed with that proposal
may leave certain harmful disclosures outside the scope of the new offence.
1.231 Similar concerns were raised by the Australian Federal Police, which noted the
practical difficulties in proving motive and warned that serious disclosures may
fall outside the offence where no benefit or detriment can be established.
1.232 To address this issue, ASPI proposed the addition of a recklessness-based
serious harm limb. Such an offence would apply where a person was aware of
a substantial risk that a disclosure would seriously harm national security,
defence, intelligence operations, international relations or public safety, and
proceeded regardless.
1.233 ASPI described this as the single most important amendment it would make to
the Bill. Coalition Senators note that both ASPI and the Australian Federal Police
identified the same underlying concern: that a secrecy offence focused
principally on motive may fail to capture some of the most damaging
disclosures. A recklessness-based harm test would instead focus the offence on
the reasonably foreseeable consequences of the disclosure.

1.234 ASPI's third concern relates to the use of the term ‘improper’ in the new offence
without any statutory definition or guidance as to its meaning.
1.235 ASPI submitted that criminal offences should provide a sufficient degree of
certainty so that those subject to the law can understand the circumstances in
which criminal liability may arise. It argued that reliance on an undefined

concept risks creating uncertainty for officials, contractors and volunteers
handling sensitive information, while also leaving important questions of
interpretation to be resolved through litigation.
1.236 ASPI proposed a similar solution to that advanced by the INSLM. It
recommended that the legislation include a non-exhaustive list of statutory
factors to assist courts in determining whether conduct is improper.
1.237 Suggested factors included the classification of the information, any caveat or
handling instruction, the person's security responsibilities and training, whether
lawful reporting pathways were available or used, and the reasonably
foreseeable consequences of the disclosure.
1.238 Coalition Senators note that ASPI and the Independent National Security
Legislation Monitor approached the issue from different perspectives, yet both
identified uncertainty surrounding the concept of ‘improper’ conduct and both
proposed the inclusion of statutory guidance. Similar concerns were raised by a

Rights Law Centre, the Centre for Public Integrity and ARTK.
1.239 The fact that organisations with markedly different perspectives on secrecy laws
reached substantially the same conclusion suggests that the issue is not confined
to any particular stakeholder group. Rather, the evidence before the Committee

courts, officials and the wider community in understanding the circumstances
in which criminal liability may arise under the proposed offence.
1.240 Coalition Senators consider that this issue warrants further consideration before

Fragments that become intelligence
1.241 ASPI also highlighted the environment in which the Bill must operate. A
disclosure no longer disappears after a single news cycle but persists as data that
can be scraped, indexed, translated and combined.
1.242 A leaked staffing list, procurement record, travel schedule or technical detail
may appear harmless in isolation. Yet hostile intelligence services and other
sophisticated actors can combine such fragments with other information to
build a picture of capability, readiness and vulnerability.
1.243 National security agencies describe this as the ‘mosaic problem’. ASPI argues
that an offence focused only on the immediate and provable harm of a single
disclosure does not reflect how intelligence is gathered today.
1.244 Its solution is a statutory cumulative harm factor, allowing courts to consider
the reasonably foreseeable intelligence value of information when combined
with other material, not merely its significance in isolation.
69
1.245 ASPI also proposes structured agency harm statements, requiring agencies to
explain in a particularised and reviewable way how information could be
aggregated into something damaging. This is not a call for broader secrecy, but
for a framework that recognises aggregation harm while requiring the
Government to substantiate its claims.
Journalism and limits of Ministerial grace
1.246 On press freedom, ASPI reaches conclusions that align with this report from a
different starting point.
1.247 ASPI accepts that the Attorney-General’s consent has value as a safeguard
against inappropriate prosecutions. However, it argues that consent is an
executive mechanism applied late and behind closed doors, and that press
freedom should rest primarily on clear statutory protections rather than the
discretion of a minister of the day.
1.248 Its recommendation is to retain consent as a backstop, while adding statutory
criteria, fixed timeframes and a requirement to give reasons, and strengthening
the substantive journalism defence so that protection is embedded in law rather
than ministerial discretion.
1.249 Where ASPI differs from media organisations is in the definition of a journalist.
It warns that an overly broad test could allow activists, foreign proxies or hostile
actors to cloak disclosures as journalism after the fact. Its preferred approach is
a functional definition that protects genuine journalistic activity while requiring
a person claiming the protection to establish they were acting as a journalist.
1.250 Importantly, both sides agree the current test is unsatisfactory. Media
organisations fear genuine freelancers and independent journalists may fall
outside it, while ASPI fears non-journalists may fall within it. When witnesses
approaching the issue from opposite directions identify the same flaw,
Parliament should take notice. Such questions are better resolved through
consultation on an exposure draft than through hurried legislation.

received security training. ASPI's concern is that such people should not face a
criminal offence built around the undefined concept of ‘improper’ conduct
without first receiving clear guidance about their obligations.
1.253 That concern mirrors the Coalition's own view that the offence reaches
volunteers and ordinary Australians who assist the government as though they
were trained insiders.
70
1.254 ASPI also identifies a gap at the other end of a career. Administrative sanctions
cease to operate once a person leaves the public service, meaning the removal of
criminal liability from many provisions may weaken deterrence for former
officials who still hold sensitive information.
1.255 That observation reinforces the Department's evidence that the APS Code of
Conduct binds only current employees, leaving limited recourse against former
officials who disclose information that is no longer protected by a criminal
offence.
What ASPI’s evidence points to
1.256 Taken together, ASPI’s evidence is that a reform it supports has produced a Bill
whose central offence asks the wrong question, whose key term is undefined,
whose treatment of classification removes an important safeguard, whose
design has not kept pace with modern disclosure risks and whose protection for
journalists rests largely on ministerial discretion.
1.257 That conclusion carries particular weight because it comes from an organisation
approaching the Bill from a national security perspective, in the very field the
Government invokes to justify the reform.
1.258 Its force is reinforced by the company it keeps. ASPI reaches many of the same
conclusions as the media organisations, the Law Council, the Centre for Public
Integrity, the Human Rights Law Centre, the INSLM and the AFP. These
witnesses begin from very different premises and agree on little else, yet
converge on many of the same concerns.
1.259 When witnesses this far apart identify the same defects, those concerns cannot
readily be dismissed as matters of opinion. They point instead to issues in the
drafting that warrant further work.
1.260 ASPI's preferred course is amendment rather than rejection of the Bill, and
Coalition Senators record that fairly. However, the breadth of the changes it
proposes - touching the offence itself, the treatment of classification, the
definition of journalism and the reach of the law into volunteers and contractors
- illustrates why these issues are better resolved through an exposure draft than
through amendments developed against a deadline which will be extended to
December.
1.261 Coalition Senators therefore consider that the appropriate course is to preserve
the existing law through the sunset extension, release the reform as an exposure
draft and return a revised Bill to Parliament once these issues have been
addressed.

ASPI are:

 Restoring legal weight to security classification where information meets a
clear statutory threshold linked to national security, defence, intelligence,
law enforcement, international relations or public safety.
 Adding a recklessness-based serious harm limb to section 122.4, which ASPI
identifies as its highest priority reform.
 Defining ‘improper’ through a non-exhaustive list of statutory factors,
including classification, caveats, clearance, training, reporting pathways and

 Introducing a cumulative harm factor to account for aggregation, mosaic
effects and machine-assisted analysis.
 Requiring structured agency harm statements to justify claims of harm in a

 Constraining the Attorney-General's consent power through statutory
criteria, fixed timeframes and reasons requirements.
 Strengthening the substantive journalism protection and adopting a

 Providing guidance, training and other safeguards before extending

Conclusion
1.263 The majority recommends that this Bill be passed. On the evidence this
Committee itself received, that recommendation cannot be sustained.
1.264 Across the course of this inquiry, the Committee took evidence from national
security experts, media organisations, legal bodies, civil society groups and law
enforcement agencies. Those witnesses disagreed on much. But on the central
question they converged: the objective of reform is sound, but the Bill in its
present form is not ready to become law.
1.265 The Committee's own report records the evidence. It records that key
recommendations of the Independent National Security Legislation Monitor
remain unimplemented. It records concerns about the design of the new section
122.4 secrecy offence, the absence of a harm-based framework, the uncertainty
surrounding the concept of ‘improper’ conduct, the breadth of the offence's
application to volunteers and contractors, the operation of the journalist
protections, the adequacy of consultation and the treatment of a range of
existing secrecy provisions.
1.266 These are not peripheral concerns. They go to the architecture of the reform
itself. And having recorded them in detail, the majority recommends that
Parliament proceed as though they had been resolved. They have not.
1.267 The purpose of committee scrutiny is not merely to identify the deficiencies in a
Bill. It is to determine whether those deficiencies have been fixed before the Bill
72
becomes law. On the majority's own findings, they have not been, and no
explanation is offered for proceeding regardless.
1.268 The majority's recommendation also rests on a premise Coalition Senators reject:
that the choice before Parliament is to pass this Bill or leave the country exposed.
1.269 That choice does not exist. The Parliament will pass the necessary legislation
and extend the existing framework until 29 December 2026. The Attorney-
General's Department confirmed that the extension was granted for the express
purpose of giving Parliament time to consider these reforms properly.
1.270 That confirmation is fatal to the case for urgency. The Government cannot
maintain, on the one hand, that the extension was necessary to allow proper
consideration of these reforms, and contend, on the other, that the reforms must
be enacted now without it. No secrecy offence will lapse if this Bill does not pass
immediately. No operational gap will arise. No national security protection will
disappear. The existing framework continues in full while Parliament
determines whether the proposed replacement is fit for purpose.
1.271 The real choice is therefore a different one.
1.272 It is between enacting a Bill that witnesses from across the spectrum say requires
further work, and using the time the Parliament has already given itself to settle
the reform properly before it becomes law. The evidence favours the second
course decisively.
1.273 The weight of that evidence lies not only in its content but in its source.
1.274 The Independent National Security Legislation Monitor - whose review this Bill
is said to implement - told the Committee the Bill departs from the harm-based
approach that underpinned his recommendations and implements them only in
part.
1.275 The Australian Federal Police - the agency that would have to enforce the new
offence - raised serious concerns about its enforceability, including that its
penalty sits below the threshold required to access the surveillance powers
needed to prove the very intent the offence demands.
1.276 Australia's Right to Know - representing the nation's major media organisations
- submitted that the Bill does not adequately protect journalists and journalism.
1.277 The Law Council of Australia identified rule of law concerns regarding
uncertainty and legislative design.
1.278 The Human Rights Law Centre and the Centre for Public Integrity questioned
whether the offence is properly calibrated. And the Australian Strategic Policy
Institute, examining the Bill through a national security lens in the very field the
Government invokes to justify the reform, warned that aspects of it create
avoidable national security vulnerabilities.
73
1.279 The significance lies in the spread. The Australian Strategic Policy Institute and
Australia's Right to Know do not ordinarily reach the same conclusions. Nor do
the Human Rights Law Centre and the national security agencies. Yet on issue
after issue, witnesses who agree on almost nothing else identified the same
defects in the same legislation. When the case for strong secrecy and the case for
press freedom arrive at the same conclusion, the Government has not struck a
careful balance between them. It has produced a Bill that satisfies neither. That
is not a signal Parliament can responsibly ignore.
1.280 The question is not whether reform should occur. It is whether this reform is
ready. On the evidence before the Committee, it is not.
1.281 The offence at the centre of the Bill remains uncertain in important respects. It
departs from the model recommended by the reviews the Government relies
upon. It is of doubtful necessity, given that the conduct which prompted it was
already criminal under existing law and that there has been a single prosecution
under this Part of the Criminal Code since 2018, resolved summarily with a good
behaviour bond. Serious questions remain about its scope, its operation and its
enforceability. And important elements of the broader reform remain
incomplete, with the Attorney-General's Department still working on matters
that witnesses regarded as central to the coherence of the new framework.
1.282 The responsible course is clear. Parliament should preserve the existing
framework through the sunset extension, release the proposed reforms as an
exposure draft, consult with those who must live under these laws, and return
a revised Bill well before the extension expires. There is time to do this. The
Government has identified no operational imperative requiring immediate
passage, and it has not explained why legislation its own witnesses
acknowledge is incomplete should be enacted before the outstanding issues are
resolved.
1.283 Coalition Senators support the objective of reform. We do not support
legislating first and refining later. Criminal offences should be settled before
they are enacted, not after. The evidence before this inquiry points
overwhelmingly to further work, not immediate passage.
1.284 For those reasons, Coalition Senators recommend that the Bill not be passed in
its current form.

Dissenting report from Australian Greens
Secrecy reform that creates as many problems as it solves
1.1 The Australian Greens have serious concerns about the Labor government’s
plan to legislate a sweeping new secrecy offence to cover, not just public
servants, but potentially any member of the public that engages with the
government. This is an ill-conceived and dangerous response to a very real
issue, namely the proliferation of secrecy offences with severe criminal penalties
across hundreds of pieces of federal legislation.
1.2 We do not get a win for transparency by abolishing three hundred hardly-used
secrecy offences and replacing them with a single ready to go catch-all offence
that applies so broadly and with so few protections.
1.3 The Greens do not share the position put in this committee report that this bill
is ready to pass. In fact the overwhelming evidence received by the committee
was that, while it is good secrecy reform was on the table, that this bill has the
potential to make matters worse, and increase fear and secrecy, not limit it.
1.4 The stated justification from the Government is that this bill implements
recommendations from the INSLM’s report into secrecy offences, but a cursory
read of the INSLM’s own submission shows how this Bill ignores critical
additional changes and protections and how without them, this reform should
not pass.
1.5 While we support the stated ambition of modernising Australia's secrecy
framework, the majority report mistakes the appearance of reform for its
substance, and waves through a bill that is in several respects broader, vaguer
and more dangerous to whistleblowers and public interest journalism than the
laws it replaces.
1.6 The committee's recommendation to pass fails to grapple honestly with the
significant omissions and defects identified by submitters, and in doing so does
not approach this reform with the rigour needed to get it right.
1.7 Repealing dead letters is not reform. The centrepiece of the Government
position is that the bill converts more than 300 secrecy offences into non-
disclosure duties. This sounds impressive until you ask how many of those 300
offences have ever been prosecuted, and the answer is almost none of them.
Sweeping away a pile of offences that nobody uses is a sensible housekeeping
measure but it should not be allowed to distract from what the bill builds in
their place.
1.8 To be clear though, this is an element of the bill that the Greens and nearly all
stakeholders support, and if it was separated from the new dangerous catch-all
secrecy offence then it has merit.
77
78
1.9 One big new offence in place of many small ones. In exchange for clearing out
the unused offences, the bill introduces a single, powerful and broadly drafted
general secrecy offence at section 122.4.
1.10 The new offence turns on whether a use of information is "improper" and
intended to obtain a benefit or cause a detriment, terms which the Criminal
Code expressly declines to limit, with benefit including "any advantage" and
detriment "any disadvantage". There is no materiality or seriousness threshold
on the face of the provision, and "improper" is left undefined. For such a serious
offence this is overly harsh.
1.11 ‘Benefit’ could be a broad public benefit that also attaches to the whistleblower,
and this may be enough to ground a criminal offence. ‘Detriment’ could include
embarrassing the government of the day or forcing the government to spend
money to address a problem. These are not meaningful thresholds or even
rational inclusions as part of a new criminal secrecy offence.
1.12 The Attorney-General's Department's assurance that the courts and prosecutors
will sort it out is cold comfort to the civil society organisations, lawyers and
academics who will have to guess, in advance, whether their engagement with
government exposes them to criminal liability. It is even colder comfort for
potential whistleblowers who can see David McBride in jail and who witnessed
the criminal persecution of Richard Boyle, all sanctioned by prosecutors and
agreed to by judges.
1.13 We strongly believe this new offence is intended to, and will have, a chilling
effect on whistleblowers.
1.14 The missing harm threshold. Most seriously, the new offence abandons the
harm-based approach recommended by both the Independent National Security
Legislation Monitor and the Australian Law Reform Commission. The INSLM
was clear that this offence departs in material respects from the harm-based
model, and the Human Rights Law Centre was clear that it criminalises conduct
producing minor, peripheral or inconsequential effects without requiring any
material harm at all.
1.15 A criminal secrecy offence of this gravity should be anchored to demonstrable
harm to an essential public interest, not to a values-laden and uncertain
standard of impropriety or the reputation of the Government. The Greens
consider that an express harm threshold must be written into the provision, and
that the failure to do so is the single largest defect in the bill.
1.16 The press freedom protections are thinner than advertised. The requirement
for the Attorney-General to consent to the prosecution of a journalist is
presented as a landmark safeguard. In reality it makes press freedom dependent
on the discretion of a politician whose own government's interests are routinely
impacted by the very reporting in question.
79
1.17 As the Alliance for Journalists' Freedom and Mr Sanderson noted, this creates
obvious actual and perceived conflicts of interest, it applies only to prosecution
and not to arrest, charge or remand, and it is no substitute for protections
enshrined in legislation.
1.18 Worse, Australia's Right to Know demonstrated that journalists remain exposed
through the principles of accessorial liability, joint commission, incitement and
conspiracy, which can make a journalist liable under "insider" offences that on
their face exclude them. The decision not to extend the public interest journalism
defence beyond section 122.5(6), and to leave the evidential burden sitting on
the accused, compounds rather than resolves the chilling effect on legitimate
reporting. This clause should not become law as it currently stands.
1.19 Whistleblowers are left out in the cold. The bill proceeds as though robust
public interest disclosure protections already exist, when the evidence is that
they do not, and that people who expose serious wrongdoing, including
potential international crimes, may fall outside the Public Interest Disclosure
Act entirely. Tightening secrecy offences while the whistleblower framework
remains under review and incomplete is the wrong order of operations.

Introduction
1.1 Australia has been described as the most secretive democracy in the developed
world.1 Successive expansions of Commonwealth secrecy law have left a
sprawling regime of more than 300 non-disclosure offences, much of it
disproportionate, poorly calibrated, and corrosive of the transparency on which
accountable government depends.2 The Secrecy Provisions Amendment

the class of persons exposed to criminal liability is certain and confined to
those with whom significant trust has genuinely been invested;
 provide materiality thresholds for ‘benefit’ and ‘detriment’, and make
clear on the face of the legislation that embarrassment alone does not
constitute a sufficient detriment; and
 provide, in the primary legislation, guidance on the intended meaning of
‘improper’ and the circumstances relevant to that assessment.
Recommendation 3
1.23 That a general public interest defence be inserted in section 122.5 of the
Criminal Code, available where information was communicated or otherwise
dealt with in the public interest and no other defence or exemption applies.
Recommendation 4
1.24 That the Bill be amended so that any changes to section 122.4 operate
prospectively only, and that the saving provisions in item 2(3) be redrafted so
that any non-disclosure offences intended to continue in force appear plainly
in the current compilation of the Criminal Code or the relevant Acts, rather
than being preserved through an otherwise-repealed provision signposted
only in explanatory notes.
Recommendation 5
1.25 That the Australian Government progress comprehensive reform of the
Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 and sections of the Corporations Act 2001
that relate to whistleblowing as a matter of urgency.
