List of recommendations
Recommendation 1
7.19 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
and the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission provide the committee
with an annual update on the progress and actions taken in response to all
committee recommendations made in the 45th, 46th, 47th, 48th and future
parliaments. These updates should be provided to the committee
concurrently with the presentation of each agency's annual reports to the
Parliament.
Recommendation 2
7.37 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
conduct testing of the I-CAN tool across a broad range of disability types and
people with different intersectional characteristics, including First Nations
people and people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, to
confirm the accuracy and reliability of results.
Recommendation 3
7.38 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
table the details of the I-CAN and the broader support needs assessment
framework, including how the tool calculates a participant's budget, as soon
as possible. This information should be published well in advance of the mid-
2026 initial implementation timeframe.
Recommendation 4
7.42 The committee recommends that in the development of the rules for the new
support needs assessment framework, the National Disability Insurance
Agency (NDIA) include the following:
 that participants are provided with an opportunity to review a draft of
their plan, including details on the rationale for decision-making, with
the ability to make suggested amendments before the finalisation of their
budget; and
 assurance that the Administrative Review Tribunal (ART) retains its
ability to make changes to a participant's budget or support allocation,
rather than limit the ART to return a decision to the NDIA for
development of a new I-CAN report.
xi
Recommendation 5
7.63 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
(NDIA) recommence development of its NDIS gender strategy as a matter of
priority. In addition, to ensure transparency, the NDIA should publish on its
website:
 an update on the work completed on the gender strategy to-date;
 a timeframe for its completion; and
 updates on its progress, which should also be included in future annual
reports.
Recommendation 6
7.64 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
include a data strategy in its NDIS gender strategy. The data strategy should
consider methods of data collection, collation and analysis which contribute
to better understanding the impact of gender on NDIS eligibility and access.
Data on gender should be analysed as part of the gender action plan and
included in future quarterly reports.
Recommendation 7
7.68 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
consider developing a specific, safe and confidential process for victim-
survivors of family and domestic violence to remove a perpetrator as their
nominee, in a way which does not notify that nominee they have been
removed.
Recommendation 8
7.72 The committee recommends that the Australian Government include the
National Disability Insurance Scheme in its audit of Australian Government
systems, identifying areas where they may be weaponised by perpetrators of
family and domestic violence, and strengthening these systems to improve
safety for victim-survivors and prevent further abuse.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 The Joint Standing Committee on the National Disability Insurance Scheme (the
committee) was established by resolution of the House of Representatives on
23 July 2025 and of the Senate on 24 July 2025.1
1.2 The committee is composed of five members and five senators, and is tasked
with inquiring into and reporting on:
(a) the implementation, performance and governance of the National
Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS/Scheme);
(b) the administration and expenditure of the NDIS; and
(c) such other matters in relation to the NDIS as may be referred to it by either
House of the Parliament.
1.3 The committee is required to present an annual report to the Parliament as soon
as practicable after 30 June each year on its activities during the year.
1.4 The committee's annual reports include examination of the annual reports of the
National Disability Insurance Agency (NDIA) and the NDIS Quality and
Safeguards Commission (NDIS QSC/Commission), and the committee can also
report on any other matters it considers relevant.
Structure of the report
1.5 This chapter introduces the role and work of the committee, including its
updated establishing resolutions in the 48th Parliament. It also provides notes on
terminology used throughout this report.
1.6 Chapter 2 sets out the current NDIS environment, including the policy and
legislative landscape of the NDIS, highlighting the main changes to the Scheme
since the committee's last annual report of November 2024, and summarising
the main policy and legislative amendments currently in train.
1.7 Chapter 3 considers the 2024–25 Annual Report of the NDIA and the key matters
raised in that report, as well as findings made by the Australian National Audit
Office (ANAO).
1.8 Chapter 4 examines the 2024–25 Annual Report of the NDIS QSC and considers
the role and function of the Commission, including legislative proposals
currently before the Parliament to expand and strengthen its role.
1.9 Chapter 5 outlines the evidence received by the committee about NDIS
participant funding and eligibility, with a specific focus on support lists,

Work of the committee
1.18 As highlighted by its first annual report in the 47th Parliament, the committee
has now provided a decade of oversight of the NDIS, through both its annual
reporting and self-referred inquiries.4 Over this time, the committee has made
over 400 recommendations aimed at improving every aspect of the NDIS, so that
this world-leading initiative can work as intended for people with disability,
their carers and advocates, and the broader disability sector.
1.19 At the time of the first annual report in the last parliament, the committee
highlighted the consistent themes which have emerged in evidence—namely
the need for true co-design at every stage of the Scheme; design and
implementation of an NDIS which focuses on choice and control; and ongoing
sustainability of the NDIS as part of a broader ecosystem of supports for people
with disability.5
1.20 As noted by the committee at the time, 'issues relating to these key themes have
dominated the committee's work over the last ten years and are the areas where
significant work is still needed', with the committee concluding that the 'future
sustainability of the Scheme will rely on the Commonwealth, and state and
territory governments, working together to build an "ecosystem" of supports'.6
1.21 With these conclusions in mind, the committee's strengthened establishing
resolution provides it with the ability to regularly interrogate the administration
and performance of the NDIA and the NDIS QSC, and any other elements of the
Scheme's administration, as the NDIS matures and is fully realised.
1.22 The NDIS was established in 2013 as a public commitment to the rights of people
with disability with its promise to support the independence, wellbeing, social
and economic participation, and inclusion of NDIS participants. Public
confidence that the Scheme continues to uphold this commitment is vital,
particularly given the significant public investment in it.
1.23 Although the NDIS has been around for more than a decade, it is still a relatively
young system in terms of implementation, with much work ahead to ensure the

1.24 To maintain this confidence and its social licence, the NDIS requires strong

adjustments and changes are appropriate, necessary, beneficial and cost-

1.25 The maturation and continued development of the NDIS must be
complemented by ongoing public confidence in the Scheme and the support it
provides to people with disability throughout the community. To ensure its
ongoing success and the maintenance of its social licence, the NDIS requires
strong oversight to make certain it is providing the best possible supports for
people with disability.
1.26 To this end, and as part of the committee's revised resolution, the committee
expects to invite both the NDIA and NDIS QSC to appear before it regularly at
public hearings to enhance public accountability around the Scheme's
administration, governance and expenditure and to ensure the public's
confidence in the NDIS. These hearings will be accompanied by examination of
the annual reports of both the NDIA and the NDIS QSC. It is vital that both
agencies are held to account to best support the disability community, and to
ensure strong oversight and risk-based regulation of NDIS providers.
Role of the committee
1.27 As part of its ongoing annual report inquiries, the committee hears from people
with disability, their carers and advocates, and various organisations across the
disability sector. It is a crucial part of the committee's work to hear from and
learn about the experiences of people with disability, and their engagement with
the Scheme, the NDIA and the NDIS QSC.
1.28 The committee receives a considerable volume of written information from
people sharing their views and experiences with the NDIS. The committee
appreciates the potential impact on people's well-being that sharing their story
can have, and the time it takes to share their story—often repeatedly—and the
committee is grateful to hear about and learn from these experiences and
insights.
1.29 Throughout any inquiry, the committee receives a significant volume of
information from the disability community. It should be noted that information
5
provided to the committee which outlines individual complaints or cases may
inform its understanding of broader systemic issues in the NDIS. However, the
committee is not empowered to provide advice on, engage with or resolve any
individual cases or concerns, nor can it amend any decisions made by the NDIA.
Notes on terminology and references
1.30 References to submissions are to individual submissions provided to the
committee's annual report inquiry and published on the committee's website,
unless otherwise indicated. References to Committee Hansard are to proof
transcripts, unless otherwise indicated.
1.31 The committee acknowledges that there are various terms used to reflect the
diversity of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures and identities. In this
report, the terms 'Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people' and 'First
Nations people' are used, with respect.
1.32 The committee also notes that some submitters and witnesses may refer to NDIS
participants and other people with disability as 'clients' of particular services.
This report may use the term 'client' when quoting from a submission or a
hearing transcript. Otherwise, the report uses the terms 'participant', 'person
with disability' and 'people with disability', with respect.
1.33 The committee recognises that people use many terms when talking about
disability. The committee is aware that there are people in the community who
prefer 'people first language', people who prefer 'identity first language', and
people who use terms interchangeably.
1.34 People first language seeks to put the person before their disability and avoid
the disability becoming the primary, defining characteristic of an individual. For
example, 'person with disability'. Identity first language reflects the belief that
being disabled is a core part of a person's identity which cannot, and should not,
be treated as separate. For example, 'disabled person'.
1.35 The committee recognises there is no consensus as to which language should be
used, and that each member of the community will have their own opinion on
terminology. The committee also understands that each person will have a
preferred way of communicating and self-describing. The committee respects
that language is an individual and highly personal choice.
1.36 In the context of this inquiry, the committee has used people first language in
its reports to ensure consistency with the terms of reference.

there have been several substantial changes made to the operation and
administration of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS).

section 10 support lists, changes to funding periods, pricing changes and NDIS
provider registration. There are also proposals before the Parliament to enhance
the regulatory and enforcement powers of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards

Eligibility reassessments
2.26 The NDIA explains that an eligibility reassessment is a check to make sure a
participant is still eligible to be on the Scheme and receive funding for NDIS
supports. Reassessments are conducted when there is evidence suggesting a
participant may no longer be eligible; for example, a participant may no longer
meet residence, disability or early intervention requirements. Additionally, all
children with developmental delay who are eligible for the NDIS under early
intervention requirements have an eligibility reassessment after they turn six, as
not all children with developmental delay go on to have a permanent
impairment.25
2.27 A plan reassessment may also occur when a participant's current plan's end date
is nearing, to check if a participant's circumstances have changed. A full plan
reassessment is not always needed, and the NDIA may not need to ask a
participant all planning questions. Alternatively, participants can also request
an early plan reassessment.26
2.28 Whilst there have been no changes to the eligibility reassessment process, there
has been a notable increase in the number of eligibility reassessments being
conducted in 2024–25 due to the allocation of additional staff conducting these
assessments.27

recommended:

outcomes for NDIS participants, including a new planning framework and
the introduction of a support needs assessment. Together, these represent a
significant and complex package of work.

NDIS pricing
2.45 Due to the immaturity of the NDIS market when it was first established, price
caps were put in place to manage price risk for participants. Price caps were
implemented to limit the prices providers could charge as a means of protecting
participants from overcharging in an emerging market, as well as to limit the
overall costs of the NDIS. When this system was established, it was expected
that the market would come to set prices over time.47

National Disability Insurance Agency
3.1 The National Disability Insurance Agency (NDIA) is an independent statutory
agency which implements the National Disability Insurance Scheme
(NDIS/Scheme) and operates to 'improve the independence, and the social and
economic participation of eligible people with disability through the
management of a financially sustainable [Scheme]'.1
3.2 This chapter examines the 2024–25 Annual Report of the NDIA, as required by
the committee's establishing resolutions.
Role of the NDIA
3.3 The NDIA has six core functions, including:
 deliver the NDIS to support the independence, and social and economic
participation, of people with disability;
 manage, and advise and report on, the financial sustainability of the NDIS;
 develop and enhance the disability sector, including by facilitating
innovation, research and contemporary best practice in the sector;
 build community awareness of disabilities and the social contributors to
disabilities;
 collect, analyse and exchange data about disabilities and the supports
(including early intervention supports) for people with disability; and
 undertake research relating to disabilities, the supports (including early
intervention supports) for people with disability and the social contributors
to disabilities.2

NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission
4.1 The NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission (NDIS QSC/Commission) is an
independent agency responsible for improving the quality and safety of
National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) supports and services. It also
works to reduce the risk of harm to people with disability when accessing those
services. The NDIS QSC is independent of the National Disability Insurance
Agency (NDIA)1 and its role is to:
 regulate the NDIS market;
 provide national consistency;
 promote safety and quality services;
 resolve problems; and
 identify areas for improvement.2
4.2 Following the machinery of government changes in May 2025, the Commission
moved from the Social Services portfolio to the Health, Disability and Ageing
portfolio, under the remit of the Hon Mark Butler MP, Minister for Health,
Ageing, Disability and the NDIS, and Senator the Hon Jenny McAllister,
Minister for the NDIS.3
4.3 This chapter examines the recent work of the NDIS QSC as well as the 2024–25
Annual Report of the NDIS QSC, as required by the committee's establishing
resolutions.

Scheme Act 2013 (NDIS Act). The core functions of the NDIS QSC are to 'protect
the quality of disability supports and services, and to reduce the risk of harm to
people with disability when accessing those services'.4

Integrity and Safeguarding Bill 2025
4.10 On 26 November 2025, the government introduced the National Disability
Insurance Scheme Amendment (Integrity and Safeguarding) Bill 2025 (the
Safeguarding Bill). The Safeguarding Bill is the second tranche of legislative
amendments to the NDIS Act, following the recommendations of both the Royal
Commission, and the Independent Review.
4.11 The Explanatory Memorandum (EM) explains that the Safeguarding Bill reflects
the recommendations of the Independent Review, which 'emphasised the
importance of the NDIS Commission having the powers and approach to
proactively and effectively regulate the market'. The EM continues:
The Bill will strengthen the NDIS Commission's capacity to detect, prevent
and respond to breaches of obligations under the Act. The Bill will address
current concerns of safety and non-compliance within the Scheme and
resolve existing structural issues that minimise the effectiveness of
regulatory action to combat abuse, neglect and exploitation that continue to
cause significant harm to participants in the NDIS.
The amendments also address priority measures required by the NDIA and
the Commission to protect the NDIS and NDIS participants from non-
compliant activity. This will enable the Commission to undertake swifter
action in investigating and prosecuting those who significantly harm NDIS
participants. It will also enable the NDIA to strengthen operational elements
of the Scheme to increase its integrity.10
4.12 Specifically, and in line with the Minister's Statement of Expectation issued to
the NDIS QSC, the Safeguarding Bill proposes to:
 Strengthen the penalty framework to ensure a fit-for-purpose penalties
and offences framework to deter people from doing the wrong thing and
providers from providing poor quality and unsafe services and supports.
 Ensure unsuitable persons can be excluded from providing services in
the [NDIS] by adding categories of people against whom a banning order
can be imposed.
 Restrict a person from engaging in promotional conduct in connection
with the NDIS where that conduct undermines the objects or the
principles of the Act or the principles in section 4 by adding a new power
allowing the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commissioner
(Commissioner) to issue an anti-promotion order.
 Strengthen the [NDIS QSC's] powers to obtain relevant information from
NDIS providers and other persons within appropriate, shorter than
minimum timeframes.
 Provide participants wishing to withdraw from the Scheme with
additional safeguards and additional communication options, and enable
the [NDIA] to move to an entirely electronic claiming system for NDIS
providers.

Gender and the NDIS
5.69 The issue of gender inequality in the NDIS was discussed by submitters and

Chapter 6
Disability providers and decision review processes
6.1 While the previous chapter focused on changes to the National Disability
Insurance Scheme (NDIS) impacting directly on individual participants, this
chapter looks at the broader context of disability service providers and the
disability workforce, as well as the decision review, appeals and litigation
processes.
6.2 This chapter puts forward the evidence received about the NDIS provider
registration process and the various models put forward about effective market
regulation and oversight, service quality and the risks of regulatory burden, and
the possibilities around disability worker registration and training.
6.3 It will also consider the current environment in which National Disability
Insurance Agency (NDIA) decisions are being reviewed, appealed and litigated,
including a discussion around the increasing volume of reviewed decisions and
the impact on participants and disability legal advocacy organisations.
Provider registration model
6.4 As outlined in Chapter 2, recent changes have been made to the NDIS provider
registration process and the circumstances under which a provider must be
registered, including formation of the NDIS Provider and Workforce
Registration Taskforce (Registration Taskforce) to design a graduated risk-
proportionate regulatory model.
6.5 The committee received mixed views on whether all service providers should
or should not be required to be registered, and what impact registration might
have on market oversight. There were also concerns about the bureaucratic
burden on providers, the provision of quality service, the safety of participants,
and ensuring participant choice and control. Some submitters also proposed the
establishment of a worker registration scheme.
6.6 National Disability Services (NDS) fully supported the NDIS Provider and
Worker Registration Taskforce's recommendations of August 2024, and
indicated that the reforms required a level of urgency and clear signalling to the
provider market of the planned changes to ensure that quality providers do not
exit the market in the meantime.1
6.7 NDS also highlighted the importance of the NDIA engaging with providers to
gain an understanding of how providers operate and what their cost structures
are—which would provide better insights into how the market works—so that

Reviewable decisions and the I-CAN
6.56 At an estimates hearing in late 2025, the NDIA stated that changes to the way
that NDIS support plans will be generated under the New Framework Planning,
coming into effect from mid-2026, the I-CAN, could impact the ART's ability to
review funding decisions. However, this would depend on how the rules are
developed, which are currently under active consideration.48
6.57 The NDIA also later clarified there would be changes to what is considered to
be a reviewable decision under the new planning model, stating that a
participant's total funding amount would no longer be a reviewable decision.
This would mean that a participant would not be able to seek internal or external
review of their total funding amount.49
6.58 However, a participant could seek review on other elements of their support
needs assessment and, if found to require an adjustment, could result in a
recalculation of their total budget amount through a reassessment.50 Under the
current system, the ART can order that additional funds be allocated to a
participant's total funding amount. Under the new model, the ART would only
be able to order that the NDIA conduct a reassessment, which may or may not

7.1 The National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS/Scheme) continues to be a
world-leading initiative, improving the lives of many Australians, including
people with disability, their carers and families, and the broader ecosystem of
disability supports. The NDIS supports greater social and economic
participation across the disability and broader community, while maintaining
the key elements of the Scheme—individual choice and control, co-design and
sustainability.
7.2 Notwithstanding the benefits provided to participants and the disability
community through the NDIS, the committee continues to receive evidence that
the Scheme is falling short in several key areas, and the NDIS is not working as
effectively as it should.
7.3 Evidence to the committee pointed to the volume and pace of change to the
Scheme's administration. This was further complicated by the machinery of
government changes in May 2025, where various ongoing consultations
changed departments (and websites).
Public confidence in the NDIS
7.4 Evidence to the committee for this first annual report inquiry of the 48th
Parliament included matters such as co-design, plan eligibility and
reassessments (including the New Framework Planning and roll-out of the I-
CAN), the performance and conduct of providers (both registered and
unregistered), and the decision review process through both the National
Disability Insurance Agency (NDIA) and the Administrative Review Tribunal
(ART).

106
7.5 Many of the matters about the operation of the NDIS are persistent and have
been repeatedly raised with the committee across Parliaments and inquiries.
These concerns are considered together with the increased focused on the costs
of the Scheme and instances of fraud, as outlined in this report's opening
chapter.
7.6 The committee has also heard repeatedly during this inquiry that the current
pace of change and the constant reviews, revisions and reforms to the NDIS—
whether in consultation with the disability community or not—is causing
considerable angst amongst participants, carers and disability representative
organisations, and leading to confusion and distress about funding and ongoing
Scheme eligibility. The announcement of the Thriving Kids initiative, for
example, caused considerable alarm across the disability sector and for the
parents and carers of children with disability, owing to its sudden nature and
lack of detail.
7.7 The committee reiterates its position that there is a strong need to maintain
public and participant trust in the NDIS, to ensure there is genuine co-deign
with the disability community, and to uphold the Scheme's original objectives.
This committee has made over 400 recommendations to date, aimed at every
aspect the Scheme, including improving its administration, governance and
public accountability. This strong oversight must continue to ensure the
ongoing success and maintenance of the social licence of the NDIS.
Annual reports of the NDIA and NDIS QSC
7.8 As noted in Chapter 1, with the revision of its establishing resolutions and as
part of its independent oversight role, the committee is now explicitly
empowered to examine the work and annual reports of the NDIA and NDIS
Quality and Safeguards Commission (NDIS QSC/Commission).
7.9 Chapters 3 and 4 of this report examine the annual reports of both agencies,
outlining the key achievements for 2024–25 and outcomes against key
performance indicators (KPIs). Both agencies met several targets and performed
well against expectations. However, both also failed to meet a number of KPIs.
While in some cases this can be attributed to amendments to survey
methodologies, it remains concerning that some key outcomes were not
achieved.
7.10 The NDIA's target participant satisfaction rates and rates of satisfaction with
progress towards participant goals were not achieved and, in fact, were lower
in 2024–25 than the 2023–24 results. Particularly alarming was the fact that the
Participant Service Guarantee timeframe fell to only 10 per cent (against a target
of 80 per cent, falling from 32.9 per cent in 2023–24).
7.11 The NDIA attributed poor participant satisfaction outcomes to the
implementation of NDIS reforms, which had led to uncertainty for participants.
This reflects the evidence received by the committee from the disability
107
community about the tumultuous impact of reforms. The committee encourages
the NDIA to continue its work on reducing wait times across its various
activities, in order to improve the participant experience.
7.12 In addition, the committee notes that the Easy Read version of the annual report
was not available at the time the NDIA's 2024–25 Annual Report was tabled. The
committee encourages the NDIA to publish the Easy Read version at the time of
tabling.
7.13 The NDIS QSC did not achieve two of its performance measures, and partially
achieved another. Trust in the Commission fell in 2024–25 from the previous
year, from 64 to 56 per cent, and there was not a sufficient decrease in the
number of participants subject to unauthorised restrictive practices. The
committee supports the steps being taken by the NDIS QSC to address these
results, including the investment in specialist behaviour support resources and
the development of strategies to enhance trust in the Commission.
7.14 Taken together, the results in the annual reports of both agencies show that
while there are some positives, participants are struggling with the pace of
change and with the ability of the administrative and regulatory agencies to
properly administer and enforce the Scheme. The committee will watch with
interest as to the results of these performance indicators in 2025–26.
Oversight of the NDIA and NDIS QSC
7.15 The committee held public hearings with both the NDIA and NDIS QSC in 2025
and expects both entities to continue to appear before it as part of the
committee's work to examine the administration and governance of the NDIS.
Both these agencies play a vital role in supporting the disability community,
through the administration of the Scheme and the oversight and risk-based
regulation of NDIS providers.
7.16 Noting the important role of both agencies, the committee has made many
recommendations over the years for improvements to NDIA and NDIS QSC
governance, processes and oversight of the disability sector. However, noting
both the pace of change and the volume of recommendations made both by this
committee and others—including the 2023 Independent Review and the
Disability Royal Commission—the committee wishes to ensure that its
recommendations are being considered and actioned.
7.17 To this end, in line with the committee's enhanced oversight powers and noting
the many recommendations it has already made to both agencies over many
years, the committee recommends that the NDIA and NDIS QSC provide it with
annual updates on the progress taken against each recommendation made in
recent parliaments. Both agencies should report to the committee on all progress
and actions taken in response to all relevant committee recommendations made
in the 45th, 46th, 47th, 48th and all future parliaments.

Support lists
7.20 The new definition of 'NDIS supports' and the transitional support lists under
section 10 of the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 commenced in
October 2024.
7.21 The committee acknowledges that the Evidence Advisory Committee is
considering supports as part of the development of the new Category A rule.
The committee encourages the Advisory Committee to consider the evidence
received by this committee as part of the development of the permanent rule.
7.22 Once the permanent rule has been drafted, it should be subject to fulsome and
genuine consultation with NDIS participants and the broader disability
community.
7.23 Some witnesses and submitters told the committee that the support lists (known
as the 'in and out lists') were restrictive, lacked flexibility and reduced
participant choice and control. The committee heard there was confusion
amongst both participants and the NDIA as to what supports were eligible for
funding, with participants losing access to supports which had previously been
a critical part of their plan.
Eligibility and plan reassessments
7.24 In this inquiry, several submitters noted their plan funding was reduced upon
reassessment. Some submitters also noted their dissatisfaction with shorter
funding periods.
7.25 The committee welcomes the recent work of the NDIA to improve the eligibility
reassessment experience, and its communication of the outcomes of these
assessments through co-design with the sector and the community.
7.26 The committee has reviewed relevant data and is satisfied that it shows
significantly more reviews resulted in an increase in a participant's budget.
7.27 The data also shows an increase in the number of eligibility reassessments
taking place in the first quarter of 2025–26, with approximately half of these
109
resulting in a revocation decision. The NDIA put this increase down to greater
staffing and, therefore, greater capacity to process reassessments.
7.28 The committee takes this opportunity to remind the NDIA of its legislative
obligation to use its revocation powers only when it is abundantly clear that a
participant no longer meets the eligibility criteria.
Funding periods
7.29 The committee received evidence that the change of funding periods from 12 to
three months was taking place on a regular basis. This was confirmed by the
NDIA which stated it would assist with budgeting. The committee also
considers that funding periods will help to address risks of fraud, participant
harm and financial exploitation.
7.30 Data from the NDIA showed that in four months, over 100 000 participants were
moved to funding period plans—including nearly 50 000 participants who had
unscheduled plan reassessments.
7.31 The committee encourages the NDIA to apply funding periods for a maximum
period of 12 months as the default position, in accordance with the NDIS Act.
Shorter, flexible funding periods should only be applied where the evidence
supports this decision, does not put the participant at risk, and allows for
improved participant budgeting.
New Framework Planning
7.32 The announcement of the New Framework Planning and roll-out of the new I-
CAN tool has generated significant discussion in the disability community.
7.33 The committee is encouraged that the NDIA is working closely with the Centre
for Disability Studies on the development of the I-CAN and the training and
accreditation of assessors, and on a staged roll-out of the assessment tool which
will allow for important, real-time feedback from participants. The I-CAN will
also help to reduce the administrative burden on NDIS participants as part of
the planning process, and the committee welcomes the NDIA's advice that the
support needs assessment report will be a reviewable decision.
7.34 Notwithstanding these benefits and the steady pace of the I-CAN's
implementation, there remains some uncertainty across the disability
community about this new approach.
7.35 The committee urges the NDIA to continue its cautious approach in the rollout
of the I-CAN tool. Further, in order to help alleviate concerns, and to ensure
accurate outcomes for NDIS participants, the committee recommends that the
NDIA extensively test the I-CAN tool against a broad range of disabilities. This
testing should consider people with disability with different intersectional
characteristics, including First Nations people and people from culturally and
linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds, to confirm the accuracy and
reliability of results.

7.36 With this in mind, the committee also recommends the NDIA provide clear
guidelines about how the I-CAN calculates a participant's budget, upon its
commencement. In the event that issues are identified, there needs to be a
proactive approach to rectifying them as part of the I-CAN assessment tool
rollout.
Recommendation 2
7.37 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
conduct testing of the I-CAN tool across a broad range of disability types and
people with different intersectional characteristics, including First Nations
people and people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, to
confirm the accuracy and reliability of results.
Recommendation 3
7.38 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
table the details of the I-CAN and the broader support needs assessment
framework, including how the tool calculates a participant's budget, as soon
as possible. This information should be published well in advance of the mid-
2026 initial implementation timeframe.
7.39 Since 2020, the committee has called for fully costed, detailed draft plans to be
provided to participants and their nominees, in order to reduce distress for
participants and to secure properly funded supports. The use of the I-CAN or
any other assessment process does not change the committee's views or this
recommendation. Participants should be involved in each stage of the planning
process. Access to draft plans will better support participants and reduce the
need for the decision review process.
7.40 Participants should be provided with an opportunity to amend their draft plan
before finalisation of their budget.
7.41 In addition, the ART should retain its ability to make changes to a participant's
budget or support allocation, rather than limiting the ART to reverting the
decision back to the NDIA for a new assessment.
Recommendation 4
7.42 The committee recommends that in the development of the rules for the new
support needs assessment framework, the National Disability Insurance
Agency (NDIA) include the following:
 that participants are provided with an opportunity to review a draft of
their plan, including details on the rationale for decision-making, with
the ability to make suggested amendments before the finalisation of their
budget; and
111
 assurance that the Administrative Review Tribunal (ART) retains its
ability to make changes to a participant's budget or support allocation,
rather than limit the ART to return a decision to the NDIA for
development of a new I-CAN report.
Decision review process
7.43 The committee received evidence that there is an increasing number of
applications for appeals to the ART for NDIA decisions around NDIS eligibility
and funded supports.
7.44 It is noted there is contrasting data regarding the number of review decisions
being upheld by the NDIA's internal review (70 per cent in 2024–25) and the
number of decisions overturned by the ART in the same period—78 per cent of
cases. Only one-fifth of the NDIA's original decisions are being upheld by the
ART.
7.45 The committee urges the NDIA to focus on the early resolution of reviews
internally before they end up at the ART, to the benefit of both participants and
the NDIA.
7.46 The committee recognises the important role that legal advocacy organisations
like National Legal Aid, Villamanta Disability Rights Legal Service and
Multicultural Disability Advocacy Australia provide. The committee would like
to see the government consider boosting the funding allocated to the NDIS
Appeals Program to better meet demand. As highlighted by the advocacy
organisations, this program not only assists people with disability to navigate
what is a complex legal process, but it also supports the fair and efficient
operation of the ART, representing good value for money.
7.47 The committee reminds the NDIA of its model litigant requirements and, again,
encourages the NDIA to continue to resolve matters at the earliest possible point
in the review process.
NDIS provider registration
7.48 NDIS provider registration is mandatory for certain providers, using a risk-
based approach to registration. Regardless of mandatory registration
requirements, all providers must adhere to the Code of Conduct.
7.49 The committee acknowledges the good work of the NDIS QSC in its
implementation of a more risk-based approach to its regulatory and
enforcement work, and to the staggered introduction of mandatory registration
for high-risk providers and environments. The safety of participants should
always be the focus of registration and regulation.
7.50 The committee recognises there are diverse opinions on whether mandatory
registration is required across the board for all providers, and understands the
concerns in the community about the impact that mandatory registration may
112
have on maintaining the important pillars of choice and control within the
Scheme.
7.51 The committee appreciates the frank manner in which the NDIS QSC engaged
with the committee on its perspectives on registration. It is clear that the NDIS
QSC is committed to ensuring the human rights of people with disability are
upheld through the provision of quality and safe products and services, whilst
recognising the challenges it faces in monitoring a market over which is has
limited visibility.
7.52 The committee notes that the NDIS QSC would prefer all providers to be
registered to improve its ability to regulate the market. The NDIS QSC's need
for visibility to effectively carry out its regulatory functions in monitoring the
provider market and enabling it to intervene to prevent harm and promote
quality was also acknowledged by the NDIS Review and the Australian
National Audit Office (ANAO).
7.53 The committee is encouraged by the government's immediate response to the
Provider and Worker Registration Taskforce's recommendations in mandating
registration for high-risk areas of the market, and notes the government's plans
to implement further changes to the regulatory framework—ensuring a
graduated, risk-proportionate approach as recommended by the NDIS Review.
The committee heard this approach was also supported by key peak bodies such
as National Disability Services (NDS), First Peoples Disability Network (FPDN)
and Children and Young People with Disability Australia (CYDA).
7.54 NDS told the committee there was a sense of urgency to implementing the
reforms and a need for clear signalling to the provider market of the planned
changes to ensure that the market of quality providers survives in the interim
period, potentially even providing a temporary loading for registered
providers.
7.55 The committee sees regulation in this space as requiring careful consideration
of competing priorities, including ensuring participant safety, upholding the
quality of service provision, the need for visibility of the market for effective
regulation, the maintenance of choice and control for participants, and ensuring
that the bureaucratic burden of processes around registration and accountability
do not force quality providers out of the market.
7.56 The merits of a worker registration scheme were also brought to the attention of
the committee, noting that the Royal Commission, NDIS Review and Provider
and Worker Registration Taskforce made recommendations on establishing
such a scheme.
7.57 The committee looks forward to further updates from the government as it
considers options in this space to further strengthen the quality and safety of
service provision, without putting unnecessary burdens on the providers and
workers who are providing valuable and timely services to NDIS participants.

Gender and the NDIS
7.58 The available data on women in the NDIS shows there are areas where gender
inequities may persist. For example, from the age of 15 onwards, male access
requests are approved at higher rates than access requests from females/those
recorded as 'other'; women are also leaving the Scheme at higher rates than men.
7.59 The NDIA has attributed gender differences in participation rates to differences
in diagnoses by disability type. As the NDIS matures, it is important to consider
how the Scheme is being accessed and utilised by specific community cohorts.
In this regard, the importance of data cannot be underestimated.
NDIS gender strategy
7.60 The committee understands that the NDIA had commenced work on a gender
strategy at the start of 2025.
7.61 The committee commends the NDIA for starting this work and notes its vital
importance to women accessing the NDIS. The committee therefore
recommends the NDIA recommence work on the gender strategy, as a matter
of priority. The NDIA should publish on its website an update on this work,
including an expected date of completion. Further, ongoing updates regarding
progress of the gender strategy should published online and included in future
annual reports.
7.62 In order to better understand the impact of gender on NDIS accessibility, the
committee further recommends the NDIA include a data strategy in its gender
strategy, with a view to collect, collate and analyse data to provide a clearer
understanding of the impact of gender on NDIS eligibility and access.
Recommendation 5
7.63 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
(NDIA) recommence development of its NDIS gender strategy as a matter of
priority. In addition, to ensure transparency, the NDIA should publish on its
website:
 an update on the work completed on the gender strategy to-date;
 a timeframe for its completion; and
 updates on its progress, which should also be included in future annual
reports.
Recommendation 6
7.64 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
include a data strategy in its NDIS gender strategy. The data strategy should
consider methods of data collection, collation and analysis which contribute
to better understanding the impact of gender on NDIS eligibility and access.
114
Data on gender should be analysed as part of the gender action plan and
included in future quarterly reports.
Family and domestic violence
7.65 The Disability Royal Commission made clear that women with disability
experience family and domestic violence at higher rates than those without
disability and, in particular, women with psychological or intellectual disability,
First Nations women with disability, and young women with disability.
7.66 Evidence given spoke of instances where disability supports are being provided
by the perpetrator of this violence or abuse. This person may also be the
participant's nominee. The committee was told there was no codified, safe and
confidential way for victim-survivors to remove nominees from their plan, in
cases where the nominee was the perpetrator, without them being notified of
their removal. This could place vulnerable women at greater risk.
7.67 The committee therefore recommends that the NDIA develop a safe and
confidential process for victim-survivors to remove a perpetrator as their NDIS
nominee, which does not notify the nominee they have been removed.
Recommendation 7
7.68 The committee recommends that the National Disability Insurance Agency
consider developing a specific, safe and confidential process for victim-
survivors of family and domestic violence to remove a perpetrator as their
nominee, in a way which does not notify that nominee they have been
removed.
7.69 The committee is aware of the Australian Government's ongoing audit of key
Commonwealth systems, identifying areas where these systems are being used
by perpetrators of family and domestic violence as a form of coercive control
and financial abuse, with a view to strengthening these systems to better protect
victim survivors of gender-based violence. Importantly, there is a strong focus
in this audit on how these systems can be improved without creating
unintended consequences, so that they are safe and work as intended for all
Australians.
7.70 The committee also understands that the approaches taken in this audit includes
incorporating the views and experiences of people who face additional and
intersecting barriers when interacting with these systems, including First
Nations people.
7.71 The committee is of the view that including the NDIS in this audit would be of
considerable value to the participants experiencing, or at risk of experiencing,
gender-based violence in the Scheme.
115
Recommendation 8
7.72 The committee recommends that the Australian Government include the
National Disability Insurance Scheme in its audit of Australian Government
systems, identifying areas where they may be weaponised by perpetrators of
family and domestic violence, and strengthening these systems to improve
safety for victim-survivors and prevent further abuse.

Coalition's additional comments
1.1 Coalition members reaffirm the longstanding bipartisan support for the
National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS/Scheme). The Scheme represents
one of the most significant social reforms undertaken in Australia and has
transformed the lives of hundreds of thousands of Australians with disability
and their families.
1.2 Coalition members recognise the profound difference the Scheme has made
since its inception and acknowledge the advocacy of the disability community
that led to its establishment. As noted throughout the committee’s report, the
NDIS plays a vital role in enabling Australians with significant and permanent
disability to live with dignity, independence and greater choice.
1.3 However, the long-term success of the NDIS depends not only on its intent, but
on its sustainability, integrity and effective administration.
1.4 Coalition members consider that the NDIS has now entered a critical phase in
its development. As the Scheme matures and participant numbers continue to
grow, governments must ensure that reforms are carefully designed, clearly
communicated and implemented in a way that maintains public confidence in
the Scheme.
1.5 Coalition members are therefore concerned that several major reform initiatives
currently being pursued by the Government lack sufficient detail, transparency
and implementation clarity.
1.6 These concerns relate particularly to the Government’s proposed targets for
reducing annual growth in the Scheme, the Thriving Kids initiative and broader
Foundational Supports framework, and ongoing concerns regarding fraud and
integrity within the NDIS.
Sustainability of the Scheme and growth targets
1.7 Coalition members recognise that ensuring the long-term sustainability of the
NDIS is one of the most significant policy challenges facing the government.
1.8 The Scheme has grown rapidly since its establishment. Originally expected to
support around 410 000 Australians,1 the NDIS now supports over 760 000
participants and continues to expand.2
1.9 The Government has stated its intention to reduce annual growth in the Scheme
to between five and six per cent over the medium term.
1 Disability Care and Support, Productivity Commission Inquiry Report Volume 1, No. 54, 31 July 2011.
2 NDIA, The NDIS in each state (accessed 6 March 2026).
117
118
1.10 Coalition members acknowledge the importance of moderating the rate of
growth in Scheme expenditure. However, Coalition members note that growth
in the NDIS last year was approximately 10 per cent, meaning the proposed
target represents a substantial reduction.
1.11 Despite the significance of this objective, Coalition members remain concerned
that the Government has not yet articulated a clear pathway for achieving this
outcome. At present, beyond the proposed Thriving Kids initiative and
Foundational Supports framework, the Government has provided limited detail
about the specific policy settings that will deliver the proposed reduction in
growth.
1.12 Reducing growth from double-digit levels to five to six per cent annually is an
ambitious policy objective. Achieving it will require careful reform design,
transparent modelling, and sustained cooperation across all levels of
government.
1.13 Coalition members are concerned that without a clear implementation strategy,
there is a risk that the Government’s growth targets may become aspirational
rather than achievable. Given the scale of public investment involved, Coalition
members emphasise that any long-term strategy to moderate growth in the
Scheme must be supported by clear policy design, transparent reporting and
realistic implementation timelines.
Thriving Kids and Foundational Supports
1.14 Coalition members recognise the policy rationale behind the Government’s
proposal to build a broad Foundational Supports framework for children with
developmental delay and autism.
1.15 The Chair’s report cites statements that around half of new entrants to the NDIS
are children under nine years old, entering the Scheme under the early
intervention stream. Coalition members acknowledge that developing
alternative pathways for children with mild to moderate developmental delay
may assist in ensuring the NDIS remains focused on supporting people with
significant and permanent disability.
1.16 If designed and implemented effectively, a well-functioning Foundational
Supports system could help provide earlier, more appropriate assistance to
families while reducing pressure on the Scheme.
1.17 However, Coalition members remain concerned that significant elements of the
proposed model remain unclear. In particular:
(a) the implementation model for Foundational Supports remains largely
undefined;
(b) the design and delivery of the program appears likely to rely heavily on
state and territory systems;
119
(c) consultation with states and territories prior to the announcement of the
program appears to have been limited. This is even more concerning given
they have essentially been tasked with the specific design and
implementation of the scheme within their jurisdictions; and
(d) there is a lack of clear responsibility and accountability if the Scheme does
not meet its intended goals.
1.18 On Foundational Supports more broadly, the Federal Government has still not
outlined its plans for the remaining $6 billion of funding allocated to
foundational supports.
1.19 Coalition members are concerned that the current approach risks creating
significant variation across jurisdictions, particularly if states and territories
adopt different implementation models. This may create uncertainty for families
and service providers and could ultimately undermine the objective of
delivering consistent supports for children across Australia.
1.20 Coalition members are also concerned that responsibility for implementation
appears to be shifting towards states and territories despite the initiative being
announced as a major national reform. Without clear governance arrangements,
there is a risk that responsibility for any shortcomings in the system may become
contested between different levels of government.
1.21 Coalition members also seek greater clarity regarding the role of key sectors in
delivering Foundational Supports. For example, the Government has indicated
that schools and early childhood education providers may play an increased
role in identifying and supporting developmental delay. However, it remains
unclear how teachers, schools and other community services will be integrated
into the system and what resources will be available to support these
responsibilities.
1.22 Coalition members emphasise that the success of any Foundational Supports
framework will depend heavily on the detail of its design and implementation.
1.23 Given the scale of the reform proposed, Coalition members consider it essential
that the Government provide greater transparency regarding program design,
funding allocations and governance arrangements.
Fraud and integrity in the NDIS
1.24 Coalition members remain concerned about ongoing reports of fraud and
misuse within the NDIS.
1.25 Maintaining public confidence in the Scheme is essential to its long-term
sustainability. This confidence depends on the assurance that taxpayer funds
are being used appropriately and that safeguards are in place to prevent abuse
of the system.
120
1.26 Coalition members are concerned that the Government and the National
Disability Insurance Agency have been unable to clearly quantify the scale of
fraud within the Scheme. Without a clearer understanding of the magnitude of
fraud and non-compliance, it is difficult for policymakers and the public to
assess whether current enforcement measures are adequate.
1.27 Robust integrity systems are critical not only to protecting taxpayer funds but
also to protecting NDIS participants from exploitation by unscrupulous
providers.
1.28 While the establishment of dedicated fraud investigation capabilities and
integrity taskforces is a welcome step, Coalition members consider that further
transparency regarding the scale and nature of fraud within the Scheme is
necessary. Greater clarity on this issue will help strengthen public trust in the
NDIS and ensure that resources intended to support Australians with disability
are used for their intended purpose.
1.29 While Coalition members note there is currently legislation before the
Parliament to expand the scope of the NDIS to investigate fraud, questions
remain on whether such an approach will ultimately be effective.
Conclusion
1.30 Coalition members support the long-term sustainability of the NDIS by
ensuring timely and appropriate supports are delivered to those who require
them, while strengthening safeguards to address fraud, misuse and the
protection of taxpayer funds.
1.31 Unmanaged cost growth presents a significant risk to the sustainability of the
Scheme itself and may ultimately undermine its capacity to deliver equitable
and effective supports to the participants it is designed to serve.
1.32 Coalition members encourage the Government to provide greater transparency
regarding the pathway for achieving its proposed growth targets, the detailed
design and governance of the Thriving Kids and foundational supports
framework, and the scale of fraud and the effectiveness of integrity measures
within the Scheme.
1.33 Providing this clarity will assist Parliament, the disability community and the
broader public to maintain confidence in the long-term future of the NDIS.
