1. Introduction
1.1 On 30 July 2025, the Attorney-General, the Hon. Michelle Rowland MP, introduced
the Strengthening Oversight of the National Intelligence Community Bill 2025 (the
Bill) into the House of Representatives. The Attorney-General subsequently wrote to
the Committee on 26 August 2025 referring the Bill for inquiry and report.
Purpose of the Bill
1.2 On introducing the Bill, the Attorney-General explained that it is ‘critical’ that the
powers entrusted to national security agencies to detect, disrupt and respond to
threats are ‘balanced with the necessary oversight to ensure that public trust is
maintained and the rule of law is upheld’. While the Attorney-General characterised
the current regime as ‘strong’, she emphasised that ‘as the national intelligence
community evolves to meet the complex and dynamic security challenges it faces,
the oversight framework must move with it’.1
1.3 The Bill amends the Inspector-General of Intelligence and Security Act 1986 (IGIS
Act), the Intelligence Services Act 2001 (IS Act) and other legislation to expand the
jurisdiction of the Inspector-General of Intelligence and Security (IGIS) and this
Committee to include oversight of:
• the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission (ACIC); and
• the intelligence functions of:
o the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre (AUSTRAC);
o the Australian Federal Police (AFP); and
o the Department of Home Affairs.
1.4 The Bill also includes:
• provision for the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Intelligence and Security
(PJCIS) to review proposed reforms to counter-terrorism and national security
legislation, and all such expiring legislation, on its own motion
• provision for the PJCIS to request the IGIS to conduct an inquiry into certain
operational activities of intelligence agencies within its purview
• clarification of the provision allowing the PJCIS to request a briefing from the
Independent National Security Legislation Monitor (INSLM)
1 Hon. Michelle Rowland MP, Attorney-General, House of Representatives Hansard, 30 July 2025, pp. 585–6.

• provision for the IGIS and the Office of National Intelligence (ONI) to provide an
annual briefing to the PJCIS
• a broadening of the own-motion review remit of the INSLM.
Bill structure
1.5 Schedule 1, Part 1 contains the main amendments proposed by the Bill which
comprise amendments to the IGIS Act, the IS Act, the Office of National Intelligence
Act 2018 and the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Law Enforcement Act 2010 to
give effect to the enhanced oversight of the National Intelligence Community (NIC).
1.6 Schedule 1, Part 2 makes amendments consequential to Part 1 to provide for
necessary information disclosure to the IGIS, and to address overlapping jurisdiction
between the IGIS and other bodies.
1.7 Schedule 2 contains provisions to amend the Administrative Review Tribunal Act
2024 in relation to reviews of access to ACIC criminal intelligence assessment
records.
1.8 Schedule 3 contains provisions to amend the Criminal Code Act 1995 to include
exemptions from civil and criminal liability for defence officials (and others) for certain
computer-related conduct.
1.9 Schedule 4 contains amendments to the Independent National Security Legislation
Monitor Act 2010 to broaden the INSLM’s own-motion review powers and to
streamline and consolidate the INSLM’s reporting functions.

History of intelligence oversight reviews
1.16 Intelligence oversight has developed in Australia largely through the conduct of
various Royal Commissions and independent intelligence reviews since the 1970s.
The establishment of an IGIS was recommended in 1984 by the second of two Royal
Commissions undertaken by Justice Robert Hope.4 This recommendation was
implemented through the IGIS Act, which gave the IGIS oversight of the Australian
Security Intelligence Organisation (ASIO), Australian Secret Intelligence Service
(ASIS) and Defence Signals Directorate (DSD; now Australian Signals Directorate,
ASD); and a more limited oversight role in relation to the Office of National
Assessments (ONA; now the Office of National Intelligence, ONI) and the Joint
Intelligence Organisation (JIO; now the Defence Intelligence Organisation, DIO).
1.17 While Hope recommended against the establishment of a parliamentary committee to
inquire into the activities of the intelligence agencies—considering that the
responsible Minister, supported by IGIS oversight, would be sufficiently accountable
to the Parliament5—the Parliamentary Joint Committee on ASIO was established by

1.20 The Flood Review also recommended the expansion of the ‘own motion’ powers of
the IGIS to apply to all agencies under its oversight.9
1.21 These recommendations were implemented, resulting in the formation of the PJCIS
as it now exists and putting in place the oversight responsibilities of both the PJCIS
and the IGIS as they have largely continued to date.
1.22 No further significant recommendations to amend intelligence oversight were made
until the Independent Intelligence Review in 2017 (2017 IIR).10 The 2017 IIR
considered the functions of the six ‘core’ Australian intelligence agencies and their
engagement and relationship with a broader group of agencies—the AFP,
Department of Immigration and Border Protection (now the Department of Home
Affairs), ACIC and AUSTRAC—which along with the original six, constitute the group
now known as the NIC.
1.23 While one of the key outcomes of the 2017 IIR was the establishment of ONI,
replacing ONA and taking on new responsibilities for coordination of the NIC, the IIR
also recommended that the oversight functions of the IGIS and the PJCIS be
expanded to include the whole of the NIC, with each NIC agency to be overseen by
these bodies in whole or in part.

2. Enhancing intelligence oversight
2.1 This chapter discusses the amendments proposed by the Strengthening Oversight of
the National Intelligence Community Bill 2025 (the Bill) that seek to enhance the
oversight of intelligence agency activities and legislation, and outlines the evidence
received in relation to the proposed provisions.
Overview of Australia’s intelligence oversight
framework
2.2 Australia’s intelligence agencies are collectively known as the National Intelligence
Community (NIC). The NIC in 2025 includes the:
• Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission (ACIC)
• Australian Geospatial-Intelligence Organisation (AGO)
• Australian Secret Intelligence Service (ASIS)
• Australian Security Intelligence Organisation (ASIO)
• Australian Signals Directorate (ASD)
• Defence Intelligence Organisation (DIO)
• Office of National Intelligence (ONI).
2.3 Three further agencies are included in the NIC in respect of only their intelligence
functions (as opposed to law enforcement, policy or other functions):
• Australian Federal Police (AFP)
• Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre (AUSTRAC)
• Department of Home Affairs.
2.4 Australia’s accountability and oversight framework for the NIC consists of several
core specialised bodies, independent of the Government, each other and the
agencies they oversee. These include the:
• Independent National Security Legislation Monitor (INSLM)
• Inspector-General of Intelligence and Security (IGIS)
• Parliamentary Joint Committee on Intelligence and Security (PJCIS).
2.5 There are also several other bodies that have review or oversight functions that apply
to the NIC agencies, or to certain NIC agencies:
7
• Australian National Audit Office (ANAO)
• Commonwealth Ombudsman
• Independent Reviewer of Adverse Security Assessments
• National Anti-Corruption Commission
• Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC)
• Parliamentary Joint Committee on Law Enforcement
• other parliamentary committees, including Senate committees conducting
Estimates.
Inspector-General of Intelligence and Security
2.6 The IGIS is a statutory authority, established by the Inspector-General of Intelligence
and Security Act 1986 (IGIS Act). The IGIS undertakes three main activities—
inspections, inquiries and review of complaints—to
assist Ministers in the oversight and review of the activities of each intelligence
agency to ensure that they are legal and proper, comply with ministerial
guidelines and directives, and respect human rights.1
2.7 An additional objective of the IGIS Act is to
assist the Government in assuring the Parliament and the public that intelligence
and security matters relating to Commonwealth agencies are open to scrutiny, in
particular the activities and procedures of intelligence agencies.2
2.8 The inspection and inquiry functions of the IGIS are complementary. The IGIS
undertakes proactive inspections of intelligence agency activities and compliance
incidents, and can also undertake formal inquiries which may be instigated by the
Inspector-General, at the request of the responsible Minister or the Attorney-General,
or in response to a complaint. The Inspector-General provides a report to the
responsible Minister at the conclusion of an inquiry.3
2.9 The complaints function of the IGIS allows a member of the public or a current or
former intelligence agency employee to raise concerns about the activities of an
intelligence agency. The IGIS is also empowered to receive public interest
disclosures about suspected wrongdoing within an intelligence agency. The outcome
of an investigation into a complaint is not generally made public; however, the
individual receives a response to their complaint that includes ‘as much information
about the outcome as possible, in light of security requirements’.4

Independent National Security Legislation Monitor
2.10 The INSLM was established under the Independent National Security Legislation
Monitor Act 2010 to review the operation, effectiveness and implications of counter-
terrorism and national security legislation (as defined).5
2.11 In undertaking this function, the INSLM considers whether laws contain appropriate
safeguards for protecting the rights of individuals, remain proportionate to any threat
of terrorism or threat to national security, and remain necessary.6
2.12 The Committee may refer a matter to the INSLM and the INSLM may decide to take
up the review under their own initiative and report on the outcome. The INSLM also
has discrete statutory review functions in relation to specific legislative provisions,
including certain powers within the Schedule to the Criminal Code Act 1995 (Criminal
Code) and the Australian Citizenship Act 2007.7
Auditor-General
2.13 The Auditor-General is an independent officer of the Parliament8 whose primary
responsibilities include auditing financial statements, conducting performance audits,
and reporting directly to the Parliament and to ministers.9 The ANAO exists to support
the exercise of these functions under the leadership of the Auditor-General.
2.14 The ANAO includes the NIC agencies in its audit program. The 2017 Independent
Intelligence Review (IIR) noted:

independent audit of ASIS financial systems and records by senior ANAO
officers. By conducting such audits, ANAO plays an important oversight role,
providing public assurance that agencies are using public funds appropriately.10

2.114 In practice, certain grounds have emerged on which public interest immunity claims
may potentially be accepted, including prejudice to legal proceedings, law
enforcement investigations, national security or defence; damage to commercial
interests; or unreasonable invasion of privacy. In making any claim for public interest
immunity, however, a relevant minister must set out the specific harm that would be
done to the public interest by the disclosure, and it is a matter for the Committee or
(ultimately) the relevant House of Parliament to determine whether to accept a claim
of public interest immunity.126
2.115 The sub judice convention imposes a restriction on debate of legal matters where
there is a substantial danger of prejudice to proceedings before a court unless there
is an overriding public interest. Generally, it is a matter for the Committee to weigh
the possible risks to justice compared with the overriding public interest.127

3. Operation of the Committee
3.1 This chapter provides an overview of the provisions of the Intelligence Services Act
2001 (IS Act) concerning the composition and operation of the Committee, as well as
the proposed amendments in the Bill governing the Committee’s operations.
Overview of the Committee’s operation
3.2 In addition to setting out the functions of the Committee, the IS Act provides the rules
under which the Committee operates.
3.3 Section 28 provides that the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Intelligence and
Security (PJCIS) is to be established after the commencement of the first session of
each Parliament. The Committee consists of 13 members and must be composed of
at least:
• two Senators who are Government members
• two members of the House of Representatives who are Government members
• two Senators who are non-Government members
• two members of the House of Representatives who are non-Government
members.
3.4 By implication, the other five Committee members may be from either House, and
may be Government or non-Government representatives, although subsection 28(3)
mandates that the majority of the Committee’s members overall must be Government
members.
3.5 Sections 29 and 30 deal with the functions of the Committee, as discussed in
Chapter 2. Section 31 requires the Committee to provide the Parliament with a report
of the activities of the Committee as soon as practicable after 30 June each year.
3.6 The remaining provisions related to the operation of the Committee are set out in
Schedule 1 to the IS Act.
3.7 Part 1 of Schedule 1 contains provisions relating to Committee procedure. These
include the Committee’s powers to obtain information and documents, compel
agency heads to appear, and to receive and publish evidence. Part 1 includes
provisions limiting the provision of information, including clause 1 which provides that
the Committee cannot require a person or body to disclose ‘operationally sensitive
information’ or information that would or might prejudice Australia’s national security
or the conduct of Australia’s foreign relations, and clause 4 which enables the
Minister to issue a certificate restricting or preventing the provision of ‘operationally
41
sensitive’ information to the Committee.1 This Part also includes clause 7, discussed
in Chapter 2, restricting the Committee from disclosing certain information in any
report to Parliament.
3.8 Part 2 of Schedule 1 establishes specific offences for failing to provide evidence or
providing false or misleading evidence to the Committee; interfering with or harming
witnesses connected with their evidence to the Committee; publishing or disclosing
certain evidence or documents received by the Committee ‘in a review conducted in
private’; and disclosing Committee information without authorisation.
3.9 Part 3 of Schedule 1 makes various provisions for the administration of the
Committee, while Part 4 provides for the establishment of subcommittees.
3.10 Certain provisions of Schedule 1 make a distinction between public and private
proceedings of the Committee, using the language of ‘a review conducted in private’.2
Under clause 20, the Committee is not permitted to conduct proceedings in public
unless approved by relevant ministers. For ‘a review conducted in private’, subclause
20(3) provides for the Committee to give directions as to the persons who may be
present.
3.11 Clause 22 of Schedule 1 prescribes the obligations of the Committee in relation to
the protection of documents. Subclause 22(1) requires the Committee to have
‘arrangements acceptable to all of the agency heads’ for the security of information
and records held by the Committee. Subclause 22(2) requires documents with a
‘national security classification’ to be returned as soon as possible once the
Committee has examined them.3

5. Committee comments and
recommendations
5.1 The Strengthening Oversight of the National Intelligence Community Bill 2025 (the
Bill) represents the most significant reform to oversight arrangements for Australia’s
intelligence agencies since the creation of the Inspector-General of Intelligence and
Security (IGIS) and the Parliamentary Joint Committee on the Australian Security
Intelligence Organisation in 1986.
5.2 Robust and effective oversight is critical to maintaining public confidence in the
intelligence community. The Committee strongly supports the objective of the Bill to
strengthen the operational, parliamentary and legislative oversight of the National
Intelligence Community (NIC) by expanding the functions of the IGIS, the
Parliamentary Joint Committee on Intelligence and Security (PJCIS) and the
Independent National Security Legislation Monitor (INSLM) respectively.
5.3 This chapter sets out the Committee’s comments, and recommendations where
appropriate, on the key elements of the Bill concerning the broadening of the IGIS’s
and PJCIS’s oversight jurisdiction and reforms to the PJCIS’s inquiry powers. This
chapter also includes the Committee’s comments on the Bill’s proposed technical
amendments affecting the operation of the PJCIS and makes a small number of
recommendations for further enhancements.
Defining ‘intelligence functions’
5.4 The Committee acknowledges that the work of NIC agencies evolves constantly in
response to the threats that Australia faces. This is evident, for example, when
considering the increasing role of the Australian Signals Directorate (ASD) in
combatting cyber threats to Australian businesses and critical infrastructure; or new
powers relating to computer and telecommunications surveillance provided to the
Australian Federal Police (AFP) and the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission
(ACIC) by the Surveillance Legislation Amendment (Identify and Disrupt) Act 2021.
5.5 Therefore, when considering NIC agencies that perform distinct policy, regulatory or
law enforcement roles in addition to their intelligence functions, the Committee
supports the assessment of the Comprehensive Review of the Legal Framework of
the National Intelligence Community (the Comprehensive Review) that a ‘functional’
approach to defining intelligence functions for oversight purposes is preferable to one
based on the structure of agencies.
5.6 The Committee notes the unique situation of the Department of Home Affairs among
NIC agencies, as a portfolio department primarily responsible for policy development
57
and delivery, that also has intelligence functions. The Committee agrees that it is
appropriate that the policy development functions of the Department of Home Affairs
are not subject to oversight by the IGIS or the PJCIS. The Committee also
acknowledges that it is an inherent role of the departmental Secretary and the
executive branch of government to determine appropriate structural arrangements for
the Department’s operations.
5.7 In assessing how the intelligence functions of the Department of Home Affairs ought
to be defined, the Committee has given careful consideration to the evidence of both
the Attorney-General’s Department and the Department of Home Affairs. The
Committee also places significant weight on the well-considered commentary in the
Comprehensive Review about the risks of constraining the oversight of intelligence
functions to a certain division within an organisational structure.
5.8 The Committee notes the proposal to define the intelligence functions of the
Department of Home Affairs in executive-made law, in the form of a legislative
instrument, rather than in the primary legislation as is the case with other agencies.
The Committee acknowledges the commitment made by the Attorney-General’s
Department that any such regulations would be subject to consultation and
notification, as well as parliamentary disallowance. However, the Committee
reiterates the long-articulated view of committees of the Parliament, most particularly
that of the Senate Standing Committee for the Scrutiny of Bills, that scrutiny of this
type cannot substitute for the full parliamentary debate and approval required for the
creation or amendment of primary legislation.
5.9 Nevertheless, the Committee recognises that, in this instance, the proposed
regulation would provide flexibility to ensure that oversight jurisdiction in relation to
the Department of Home Affairs can be updated in a timely manner as the scope of
the Department’s intelligence functions change.
5.10 The Committee therefore accepts the Bill’s proposal to define the intelligence
functions of the Department of Home Affairs in regulations. However, in order to
ensure a greater degree of transparency about the definition of the Department of
Home Affairs’ intelligence functions, the Committee recommends that additional
notification and consultation requirements be included.
Recommendation 1
5.11 The Committee recommends that the proposed regulation defining the
intelligence functions of the Department of Home Affairs under section 3A of
the Inspector-General of Intelligence and Security Act 1986, to be inserted by
item 6 of the Strengthening Oversight of the National Intelligence Community
Bill 2025:
• clearly define the functions of the Department of Home Affairs that are
subject to oversight by the IGIS and the Committee; and
• be promptly amended if there are any subsequent relevant administrative
changes in the Department of Home Affairs.
58
The Committee further recommends that the Bill be amended to require that:
• once the regulation is made, a briefing be provided to the Committee and
the IGIS by the Department of Home Affairs on its interpretation of that
regulation; and
• following any future changes to the regulation, the Committee and the IGIS
be provided a further briefing from the Department of Home Affairs on its
interpretation of the new regulation.
Potential overlap and duplication of oversight responsibilities
5.12 The Committee also acknowledges the evidence of the AFP in relation to the
challenges of distinguishing its intelligence functions from its law enforcement
functions. The Committee supports the elements of the Bill’s proposed definition of
‘intelligence function’ for the AFP that exclude distinct law enforcement
responsibilities from the oversight of the IGIS and the PJCIS.
5.13 The Committee has an existing strong and productive working engagement with the
AFP. The Committee is confident that it can continue to work effectively with the
AFP, as well as with the ACIC, AUSTRAC and the Department of Home Affairs, to
ensure that its oversight is appropriately targeted and effective, and to minimise
duplication and overlap with other parliamentary committees and integrity bodies.
5.14 Ongoing communication will be needed to enable the IGIS and the Ombudsman to
appropriately manage their distinct oversight functions, and to ensure these bodies
can learn from one another’s approaches where they have oversight of different
agencies exercising the same powers. The Committee acknowledges the evidence of
the IGIS and the Commonwealth Ombudsman in relation to the ability for those
agencies to communicate on oversight matters relating to the ACIC and AFP beyond
the current 18 month transition period.
Recommendation 2
5.15 Noting the intent of the Strengthening Oversight of the National Intelligence
Community Bill 2025 to provide the Inspector-General of Intelligence and
Security and the Commonwealth Ombudsman discrete oversight
responsibilities, the Committee recommends the Government consider
whether additional measures may be necessary to enable those bodies to
share information relating to their respective oversight of the Australian
Criminal Intelligence Commission and the Australian Federal Police, beyond
the conclusion of the transitional provisions.
Amendments to PJCIS functions
5.16 The Committee supports the Bill’s reforms to the Committee’s own inquiry functions,
which are broadly consistent with the recommendations of the 2017 Independent
Intelligence Review (2017 IIR) and subsequent reviews. However, the Committee
59
makes a small number of comments and recommendations, as set out below, to
ensure that the new provisions are clear and effective.
Parliamentary oversight of the ACIC
5.17 The Committee notes that the Bill would confer on the PJCIS new functions to review
the administration and expenditure of the ACIC, including its annual financial
statements, and to review any matter in relation to the ACIC that is referred to the
Committee by the responsible Minister, the Attorney-General or a House of the
Parliament.
5.18 The ACIC is currently oversighted by the Parliamentary Joint Committee on Law
Enforcement (PJCLE). The Parliamentary Joint Committee on Law Enforcement Act
2010 provides that it is a function of the PCJLE to:
• monitor and to review the performance by the ACIC of its functions, and

Additional technical amendments
5.63 In considering the provisions of the IS Act that govern the operation of the
Committee, the Committee considers there is an opportunity for further amendments
to enhance efficiency and ensure that the law more accurately reflects modern
practices of the Committee and the Parliament. The Committee notes that several of

Conduct of reviews ‘in public’ or ‘in private’
5.64 Three provisions in Schedule 1 to the IS Act: clause 6 (publication of evidence or
documents), clause 9 (offences for publication or disclosure) and clause 20
(Committee proceedings) make reference to ‘a review conducted in private’ in
establishing restrictions, offences or procedures relating to the Committee’s work.
The meaning of a ‘review conducted in private’ is unclear, as the term is used
inconsistently between the provisions. The Committee considers that subclause
20(3) should be amended to replace the word ‘review’ with ‘meeting’. This will resolve
the inconsistency and help clarify the meaning of the language in all three provisions.
5.65 The Committee also recommends that subclause 20(2) of Schedule 1 to the IS Act
be amended to remove the historical requirement for ministerial approval to conduct
reviews in public.
5.66 As presently enacted, subclause 20(2) prohibits the Committee from conducting any
review in public without the approval of four ministers, including the Prime Minister.
The scope of this requirement is presently ambiguous: it may extend only to
circumstances where the Committee proposes to hold a public hearing, or could be
interpreted to apply more broadly to include publishing any submissions, or even
advertising an inquiry on the Committee’s website. In any case, the purpose of
requiring ministerial approval to conduct these activities is unclear, noting there are
other provisions in the IS Act that prevent the Committee from disclosing sensitive
information, compelling the provision of classified evidence, or departing from agreed
security protocols.17
5.67 As discussed above, the Committee’s work and the way it conducts its reviews have
changed significantly since this provision was drafted, which was initially for the
purposes of the Parliamentary Joint Committee on ASIO in 1986.18 At the time, the
Committee’s functions were limited to reviewing matters referred by the responsible
Minister, which almost exclusively related to ASIO’s internal administration. The
Committee completed just 2 inquiries in its first 10 years and did not review its first

18 See Australian Security Intelligence Organization Amendment Act 1986, cl 92F.

piece of legislation until 1999.19 Under such circumstances, it was not unduly
burdensome for the Committee to obtain express approval from the Minister before
taking evidence in public.
5.68 The functions of the PJCIS today bear little resemblance to those of the committee
established in 1986. Under its contemporary remit, the Committee completed more
than 100 inquiries in the past 10 years and this workload is expected to only increase
due to the expanded functions in the Bill. Although certain inquiries – in particular the
annual reviews of administration and expenditure – are still largely conducted in

public evidence because any amendments need to be justified publicly to gain the
support of the Parliament. As a result, almost all Committee inquiries now include
public submissions, and many do not involve any private or classified evidence at all.

5.70 Clause 22 of Schedule 1 to the IS Act provides for the protection of Committee

make arrangements acceptable to all the agency heads for the security of any
information held and any records made by the Committee’. Under subclause 22(2),
as presently drafted, the Committee must also return national security classified
documents to originating agencies ‘as soon as possible after the members have
examined them’.
5.71 The Committee has robust security arrangements in place in relation to the provision
and handing of classified documents. The Committee and its secretariat comply with
the same requirements under the Protective Security Policy Framework as other
agencies of the Commonwealth, and have the necessary infrastructure and
equipment in place to store, handle and dispose of such documents appropriately.
While it is the practice of the Committee to return classified documents to the
intelligence agencies as soon as practicable once they are no longer needed by the
Committee, there are circumstances where the requirement to manually transfer
documents back to the originating agency for disposal may introduce additional
unnecessary security risks.

oversight in relation to these immunities is comparatively weak.
5.89 While certain intelligence agencies—ASD, ASIS and AGO—have a like immunity for
conduct overseas in the proper performance of their functions, each of those
agencies is subject to robust oversight of the use of the powers by the IGIS: an
external oversight body. This oversight includes, but is not limited to, a requirement
to provide written notice to the IGIS when material damage, interference or
obstruction occurs to a computer in Australia. The IGIS is also empowered to certify
any fact relevant to the question of whether conduct was undertaken in the proper
performance of the agency’s functions.
5.90 By contrast, the proposed reporting requirement for defence officials to give notice of
conduct that causes material damage, material interference or material obstruction to
a computer is internal only: to the Chief of the Defence Force and, in some cases,
the Secretary of the Department of Defence.
5.91 The Committee considers it would be more appropriate for there to be an external
reporting mechanism through which the ADF would report any instances of material
damage, interference or obstruction to a computer in Australia for independent
review. The Inspector-General of the Australian Defence Force (IGADF) may be well
placed to receive such notifications and to provide independent oversight of the use
of these provisions. However, the Committee is aware that, unlike the IGIS, the
IGADF does not have broad own-motion powers to inquire into matters of compliance
with legislation and the propriety of the ADF’s activities, except where a complaint
73
has been made by an ADF member or as part of an investigation into the military
justice system or the death of an ADF member.
5.92 Nevertheless, the Committee is also aware of recent recommendations to clarify the
role, functions and independence of the IGADF, stemming from the Twenty-year
Review of the Office of the Inspector-General of the Australian Defence Force and
the Royal Commission into Defence and Veteran Suicide.21
Recommendation 11
5.93 The Committee recommends that, as part of future reforms to the role and
functions of the Inspector-General of the Australian Defence Force (IGADF),
the Government consider empowering the IGADF to consider and
independently inquire into the subject of written notices made under proposed
subsection 476.7(6) of the Criminal Code.
Broadening the INSLM’s own-motion remit
5.94 The Committee supports the Bill’s proposal to broaden the INSLM’s own-motion
remit to the full range of counter-terrorism, national security and intelligence-related
laws that exist on the statute book.
5.95 The office of the INSLM was established in 2010, with support from this Committee,
in order to review the wide-ranging new counter-terrorism laws that had been
introduced since 2001. Many of those laws impinge on a range of traditional rights
and freedoms and it is appropriate that they be under constant review to ensure they
remain necessary, proportionate and consistent with human rights. However, the
pace of legislative reforms to national security law has only increased since 2010.
The Committee has reviewed multiple new legislative regimes in recent years that fall
outside or extend beyond the counter-terrorism sphere but often equally raise issues
concerning the balance between national security and individual rights and
freedoms.22 Moreover, some powers that were initially introduced for counter-
terrorism purposes have now been broadened to be used for other national security
purposes.23 The Committee therefore considers it is appropriate and timely for the
role of the INSLM to be broadened to keep pace with these developments.

5.96 The Committee also supports the Bill’s provisions aimed at streamlining and
modernising the INSLM’s enabling legislation, including clarifying the independence

Conclusion
5.97 The Committee welcomes the increased oversight responsibility for the IGIS, the
PJCIS and the INSLM proposed by the Bill. This Bill realises important and long-
awaited reforms that have been recommended by multiple independent reviews, by
this Committee and by others over a number of years.
5.98 The Committee particularly supports the measures in the Bill to improve and enhance
the efficiency and functioning of the PJCIS itself. The recommendations in this report
are intended to enhance the Bill’s effectiveness in meeting those objectives.
5.99 The Committee strongly endorses the Bill and, following implementation of the
recommendations in this report, recommends that the Bill be passed by Parliament.
Recommendation 12
5.100 The Committee recommends that the Strengthening Oversight of the National
Intelligence Community Bill 2025 be passed by Parliament.
