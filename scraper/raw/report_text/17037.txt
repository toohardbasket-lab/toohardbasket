Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 On 14 May 2026, the Senate referred the Competition and Consumer
Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Bill 2026 (the bill) to the Senate
Economics Legislation Committee for inquiry and report by 18 June 2026.1 On
11 June 2026, the committee was granted an extension of time to report to
25 June 2026.2
1.2 The committee’s report on the bill contains two chapters:
 Chapter 1—provides an overview of the bill and the inquiry process; and
 Chapter 2—considers inquiry participants’ evidence on the bill.
Purpose of the bill
1.3 The bill seeks to amend the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (CCA) to update
the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) with enhanced consumer protections for
‘current and emerging unfair trading practices’.3 In particular, the bill proposes:
 a new general prohibition on unfair trading practices towards consumers;
 stronger protections against drip pricing by requiring transparency of
transaction based charges; and
 protections against subscription practices that are detrimental to consumers
and small business.4
1.4 The Explanatory Memorandum states the above measures are ‘designed to
complement existing ACL provisions, increase consumer trust in the
marketplace and promote fairer trading among businesses’.5 The bill includes
civil penalties to deter unfair trading practices.6
1.5 When introducing the bill, Dr Andrew Leigh MP, Assistant Minister for
Productivity, Competition, Charities and Treasury, outlined the types of
conduct the bill is intended to address:
Australians know exactly what this bill is about because they have lived it.
Trying to decide on an online purchase, they suddenly find themselves

General prohibition on unfair trading practices
1.7 Part 1 of the bill seeks to introduce a general prohibition on unfair trading
practices (general prohibition) ‘in connection with the supply of, or an offer to
supply, goods or services’ to consumers.8 Under the prohibition, unfair trading
practices are defined as conduct that:
 does, or is likely to do, either or both of the following:
− manipulate the consumer; or
− unreasonably distort the environment in which the consumer makes,
or is likely to make, a decision; and
 causes, or is likely to cause, detriment (whether financial or otherwise) to
the consumer.9
1.8 The general prohibition is a ‘principles based test’ intended to capture conduct
which does not ‘neatly fall within the existing prohibitions on misleading
conduct or unconscionability, but which nonetheless exploits behavioural
biases, overwhelms consumers with complexity, or structures choices in a way
that leads people towards decisions they would not otherwise make’.10
Strengthening protections against drip pricing
1.9 Part 2 of the bill seeks to ‘ensure information relating to transaction based
charges is displayed throughout the purchase process’.11 These changes are
intended to:
 strengthen protections against ‘drip pricing’, which is a practice whereby
businesses gradually add fees during the transaction process, obscuring
the total amount payable, and

Context
1.15 While ACL provides broad protections for consumers against unfair trading
practices, recent market developments and, in particular, emerging forms of
online practices by businesses, has resulted in certain conduct that ‘may fall
outside the full reach of the ACL’.18
1.16 Addressing these issues has been on the agenda for several years. In
November 2020, Commonwealth, State and Territory Consumer Affairs
Ministers committed to ‘exploring how a prohibition on unfair trading practices
could be adopted in Australia’.19 In September 2022, Commonwealth, State and
Territory Consumer Affairs Ministers agreed that ‘the Commonwealth would
lead a public consultation on options to address unfair trading practices in the
ACL’.20 This agreement led to two consultation processes:
 in August 2023, the Government sought feedback on ‘options to address
unfair trading practices’. Subsequently, in October 2024, the Government
announced that ‘it would address unfair trading practices by introducing a
general prohibition and specific prohibitions in the ACL’;21 and
 in November 2024, the Department of the Treasury (Treasury) sought
feedback on the design of the general prohibition and other specific
prohibitions, including options to target ‘subscription practices that are
detrimental to consumers and small businesses, and drip pricing.22
1.17 The abovementioned consultation processes identified businesses were
increasingly using practices harmful to consumers, in both online and offline
contexts, and such practices ‘did not always reach the threshold required to
contravene existing ACL provisions’.23
1.18 As noted in the Explanatory Memorandum, the bill seeks to address the increase
of harmful practices by:
…enhancing protections against unfair trading practices (including
emerging forms of misconduct by businesses, particularly in an online
context), which may cause consumer detriment but may not be clearly

Consumer manipulation or unreasonable distortion of environment (limb 1)

subsection 28B(2)) has two limbs. A person must contravene both limbs to
contravene the general prohibition on unfair trading practices (proposed
subsection 28B(1)).33
1.28 To contravene the first limb, a person must engage in conduct that does, or is
likely to ‘manipulate the consumer’ and / or ‘unreasonably distort[s] the
environment in which the consumers makes, or is likely to make, a decision’,
per proposed paragraph 28B(2)(a).34

Part 2—Drip pricing
1.39 Part 2 of the bill introduces proposed new section 48A to require that a person
who offers to supply a good or service at a displayed base price must also
display specified information regarding any associated transaction charge.47
Applicable goods or services
1.40 Proposed subsection 48A(1) provides that the requirement to display
transaction based charge information applies to goods or services which are
offered for supply are for personal, domestic or household use or consumption.48
1.41 An offer to supply incorporates offers made through advertising, marketing and
promotion, including ‘television advertisement, poster, or in an electronic form
such as on a website or app’.49 However, the Explanatory Memorandum states
the ‘display requirements are not enlivened through a verbal offer to supply, as
the base price would not be displayed’.50
1.42 Proposed subsection 48A(4) provides an exception to the requirement to display
transaction based charge information where the offer of supply is made
exclusively to a body corporate.51
Base price
1.43 The bill specifies that the ‘base price’ is:
An amount payable by a purchaser for a supply of goods or services is a
base price for the goods or services if it includes an amount payable for the
goods or services themselves.52
Transaction based charge
1.44 The bill specifies that a ‘transaction based charge’ is:
A charge (or part of a charge) of any description is a transaction based

Part 3—Subscription contracts
1.49 Part 3 of the bill introduces proposed Division 4A—Subscription contracts to
ACL to protect ‘against subscription practices that are detrimental to consumers
and small business’ by:
 requiring a person that offers to supply goods or services under a
subscription contract to disclose key information about the contract when
making the offer;
 establishing a framework to require a person who supplies goods or
services under a subscription contract to notify subscribers of key
information at key times while a contract is in effect;
 requiring a person who supplies goods or services under a subscription
contract to ensure there is an easy and straightforward way for
subscribers to end a subscription contract with minimal steps, and a way
to end the contract online in certain circumstances.57
1.50 The bill provides that contract is a subscription contract if it meets one of four
sets of requirements relating to the period of the contract (being an indefinite
period, fixed period, initial free period or an initial discount period) and is not
an otherwise excluded contract.58
1.51 Several types of contracts are excluded from being a subscription contract,
including, for example, a lease or a contract of hire purchase (see proposed
section 48C).59 For instance, a supplier would be required to disclose how a party
to the contract can end the contract.60
1.52 Further, Division 4A ‘imposes information disclosure requirements on a person
that offers to supply goods or services under a subscription contract’ (per
proposed section 48D).61
1.53 Proposed Division 4A is subject to the existing compliance and enforcement

Chapter 2
Views on the bill
2.1 This chapter considers inquiry participants’ evidence on the Competition and
Consumer Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Bill 2026 (the bill). In general,
inquiry participants supported the objective of addressing unfair trading
practices that affect Australian consumers. Several inquiry participants strongly
supported the bill’s consumer protection measures.
2.2 However, inquiry participants also raised potential issues in the bill, including:
 the clarity and breadth of provisions that establish the general prohibition
on unfair trading;
 the application of certain provisions related to drip pricing;
 the application of certain provisions related to subscription contracts; and
 the suitability of the bill’s implementation provisions.
2.3 The chapter concludes with the committee’s view and a recommendation.
General prohibition on unfair trading practices
2.4 As outlined below, inquiry participants expressed a range of views regarding
the bill’s proposed general prohibition on unfair trading practices (general
prohibition). These views ranged from support from consumer advocates for
the protections offered by the general prohibition to concerns from industry
representatives regarding the clarity of key provisions and the impact this could
have on businesses.
2.5 Inquiry participants also expressed a range of views on whether the protections
of the general prohibition should be extended to small businesses.
Support for the general prohibition on unfair trading practices
2.6 Several inquiry participants provided evidence on the importance of the general
prohibition to enhance consumer protections against unfair trading practices,
particularly in the online environment where some practices are not adequately
captured under Australian Consumer Law (ACL).
2.7 For instance, the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC)
submitted that the introduction of a general prohibition is ‘critical to improving
protections against harmful practices that are not adequately addressed by
existing provisions of the ACL’.1 The ACCC said harmful conduct:

under the existing provisions of the ACL.

Committee view
2.144 The committee welcomes the opportunity to examine the Competition and
Consumer Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Bill 2026.
2.145 The committee recognises that the bill contains substantial measures to improve
consumer protections, including by establishing a general prohibition on unfair
trading practices and by enhancing consumer protections in relation to drip
pricing and subscription contracts. These are significant changes to the
Australian Consumer Law that are intended protect Australian consumers from

new and emerging unfair trading practices, particularly where such practices
occur in the online environment.
2.146 The committee recognises the need for the changes proposed in the bill. More
than ever, unfair trading practices occur in an online environment that can be
highly complex, lack transparency and can potentially affect a large number of
Australian consumers. The consumer harms associated with unfair trading are
significant and it is vital that the Australian Consumer Law extends to the range
of conduct that causes these harms.
2.147 The committee also recognises that Australian Consumer Law must strike a
careful balance between providing robust protections for consumers while not
creating an excessive regulatory burden for the vast majority of businesses that
trade fairly. This balance contains an inherent tension between principles-based
laws that may provide broader and more flexible consumer protections, and
more prescriptive laws that may offer clearer and more targeted conduct
requirements. These are complex considerations and finding the right balance is
made more challenging by broad application of the Australian Consumer Law
to all businesses which supply goods or services to consumers.
2.148 The committee notes that many inquiry participants expressed support for the
objectives of the bill. Further, several inquiry expressed strong support for the
specific measures in the bill which, in their view, would significantly improve
protections for Australian consumers in ways that are not currently available
under the Australian Consumer Law. The committee shares these views and
considers that, on the whole, the changes proposed in the bill will be a marked
improvement to Australia’s consumer protection laws.
2.149 At the same time, the committee also notes that evidence provided by many
inquiry participants highlighting potential issues with the application the bill’s
measures. For instance, many submitters expressed concern about the clarity of
key provisions in the proposed general prohibition. Submitters also raised
issues regarding how the bill’s provisions in relation to drip pricing and
subscription contracts would apply in practice.
2.150 Given the significance and scale of the changes proposed in the bill, the
committee considers that a degree of uncertainty is a normal part of the policy
process. Nonetheless, the committee is cognisant that a lack of certainty could
be a challenge for many businesses, particularly where significant changes to
business systems are needed to comply with the bill’s new measures. As such,
the committee has made recommendations intended to further clarify the bill’s
measures and aid businesses to adapt to these important consumer reforms.
2.151 The committee considers the bill’s extended implementation period is
appropriate to allow adequate time for the development of regulatory guidance
and to allow time for businesses to make any adjustments necessary to comply
with the new requirements. Additionally, the committee supports the bill’s
51
provision for a statutory review of the proposed amendments, two years after
commencement, as an important mechanism to assess the efficacy of the reforms.
2.152 Further, the committee notes that a number of inquiry participants expressed a
range of views regarding whether the protections afforded under the general
prohibition should extend to small businesses. The committee appreciates the
differing views in relation to this matter, but notes the core objective remains to
provide appropriate and timely unfair trading protections for Australia’s small
businesses. As such, the committee is pleased to note that, on 3 June 2026, the
Treasury commenced public consultation on unfair trading practices
protections for small businesses. Following consultation, the committee
encourages the Australian Government to prioritise implementation of these
important reforms.
2.153 On balance, the committee considers the bill is a significant and necessary
improvement to the Australian Consumer Law. The committee anticipates the
consumer protection measures contained in the bill will benefit many
Australians and positively contribute to Australia’s fair trading regime.
2.154 The committee commends the Australian Government for pursuing these
reforms.
Recommendation 1
2.155 The committee recommends that the Australian Government review the
evidence received during the committee’s inquiry and assess whether any
amendments should be made to proposed section 28B of the bill, or additional
information provided in a revised Explanatory Memorandum, to more clearly
articulate the types of conduct that the general prohibition on unfair trading
is intended to address.
Recommendation 2
2.156 The committee recommends that the Australian Government consider
amending proposed subsection 28B(6) of the bill to include a broader range
of examples of conduct that may be unfair trading practices.
Recommendation 3
2.157 The committee recommends that the Australian Competition and Consumer
Commission prioritise development of regulatory guidance to clarify issues
raised by inquiry participants regarding compliance with the bill’s
provisions. The committee considers regulatory guidance should be released
as soon as practical following passage of the bill to maximise the time
businesses has to make any necessary changes to their systems and practices.
