Chapter 1
Committee Report
1.1 On 28 November 2024, the Senate referred the provisions of the Treasury Laws
Amendment (Fairer for Families and Farmers and Other Measures) Bill 2024 (the
Bill) to the Senate Economics Legislation Committee (the committee) for inquiry
and report by 30 January 2025.1
1.2 The bill contains a range of measures, including:
 changes that strengthen the enforcement regimes of various industry codes,
including the Food and Grocery Code and Unit Price Code;
 expanding the minister’s and regulator’s ability to set and enforce
preexisting product safety and information standards from other
jurisdictions;
 increasing the revenue cap for Housing Australia;
 updating the charities and not-for-profits (NFPs) that have Deductable Gift
Recipient status;
 providing measures for the transfer of government employees between
agencies following the cessation of the Modernising Business Registers
Program (MBR program); and
 other minor changes to simplify language and amend errors in the wording
of various pieces of Treasury legislation.
Conduct of the inquiry and passage of the bill
1.3 On 28 November 2024, the Senate referred the provisions of the Treasury Laws
Amendment (Fairer for Families and Farmers and Other Measures) Bill 2024 to
the Senate Economics Legislation Committee for inquiry and report
by 30 January 2025. Later that day the bill was passed by both houses of the
Parliament with no amendments.2
1.4 Due to the passage of the bill, the committee did not invite written submissions
or hold public hearings for this inquiry.3 This report therefore provides a
summary analysis of the bill as referred to the committee.

Overview of the bill
1.5 The bill contains six schedules:
 Schedule 1 facilitates the return of Australian Securities and Investments
Commission (ASIC) staff working for the Australian Taxation Office (ATO)
as a part of the now ceased Modernising Business Registers (MBR) program.
This measure ensures that staff returning to ASIC maintain the terms and
conditions of their employment in line with the same framework as existing
ASIC staff.4
 Schedule 2 makes changes to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010
(Competition and Consumer Act) and Australian Consumer Law to allow
for more streamlining in setting and enforcing Australian product safety
standards and information standards. The bill provides more flexibility for
Commonwealth Ministers to set safety and information standards,
particularly in line with similar international product safety standards if the
product has already been approved for use under those frameworks.5
 Schedule 3 aims to increase the maximum balance on the Housing Australia
Special Account legislated by the Housing Australia Act 2018. The bill
amends the cap from $1 billion to $4 billion, allowing Housing Australia to
retain more revenue credited to the special account to implement the
agency’s social and affordable housing programs. These measures were
announced in the 2024-25 Budget.6
 Schedule 4 makes additional amendments to the Competition and
Consumer Act, to increase maximum penalties for breaches of the Food and
Grocery Code and the Unit Pricing Code. This will strengthen the
enforcement regime of the industry codes, thereby deterring anti-compliant
and anti-competitive behaviour in the supermarket industry. This Schedule
implements elements of the government's response to the Independent
Review of the Franchising Code of Conduct and the Independent Review of the
Food and Grocery Code of Conduct released in May and June of 2024
respectively.7
 Schedule 5 amends the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (ITA Act 1997) to
update the deductible gift recipient list.
 Schedule 6 makes minor and technical changes to various laws within the
Treasury portfolio, updating legislative references, simplifying provisions
and reducing red tape.8

Schedule 1 – Amendments relating to the cessation of registries modernisation
program
1.6 The MBR program, established in 2018-19, aimed to consolidate Commonwealth
business registry services administered across the ATO and ASIC. Under the
MBR Program, the Australian Business Register and 31 business registers
administered by ASIC were to have been consolidated onto a centralised
platform administered by a newly established Registrar within the ATO.
Following Machinery of Government (MoG) changes in 2021, ASIC registry
employees were transferred to the ATO to assist in the program.9
1.7 Following an independent review in August 2023, the government announced
it would cease the MBR program, pursuing its modernisation objectives through
other programs and initiatives.10
1.8 As part of the government’s decision to unwind the MBR program, Schedule 1
of the bill makes amendments to the Australian Securities and Investments
Commission Act 2001 (ASIC Act), allowing for the return of ASIC employees,
who have been working for the ATO, and whose employment had been under
the Public Service Act 1999 (Public Service Act) and Fair Work Act 2009 as a part
of the now-ceased MBR program.11
1.9 Under current legislation, ASIC employees are not employed under the Public
Service Act, instead being employed under the ASIC Act since 2019. Schedule 1
of the bill would ensure that equivalent employment terms and conditions of
returning ASIC staff are retained upon their return to ASIC. 12
1.10 Specifically, the bill amends the ASIC Act to allow for staff to be transferred to
ASIC by a written determination from the Public Service Commissioner, and to
be employed by the ASIC Chairperson under a written agreement. The bill also
specifically includes ‘transferred staff’ as a category of a staff member under the
ASIC Act.13
1.11 Schedule 1 of the bill also makes amendments to the Corporate Collective
Investment Vehicle Framework and Other Measures Act 2022, to adjust provisions
relating to the registry record of Corporate Collective Investment Vehicles.
Under the changes, these recording measures outlined in the Explanatory
Memorandum (EM) will come into effect from 1 July 2026.14

Schedule 2 – Regulation of safety standards and information standards
1.12 Schedule 2 of the bill amends the Competition and Consumer Act 2010, including
Australian Consumer Law as set out in Schedule 2 of the Act. The bill
streamlines the setting and enforcement of Product Safety Standards and
Information Standards, particularly if these products have already been subject
to equivalent or more strict standards overseas. The amendments also enhance
the enforcement of these standards by expanding the power of the regulator to
request information and documentation, and strengthening enforcement
mechanisms, particularly with more flexible compliance methods including
civil penalties.15
1.13 The Assistant Minister for Competition, Charities and Treasury, the
Hon Dr Andrew Leigh MP (the Minister), stated in his second reading speech:
… Australian Consumer Law does not easily allow for overseas standards
to be recognised alongside Australian standards. Instead, the current
provisions lead to duplicative retesting and relabelling to adhere to the
relevant Australian standard, even when the product already complies with
an overseas standard which offers an equivalent or a better level of
consumer protection.16
1.14 Schedule 2 of the bill would enact four mechanisms to further streamline the
approval and enforcement of Product Safety Standards and Information
Standards. They include:
 expanding the Minister’s ability to make product safety standards and
information standards. This expands their current ability to declare certain
preexisting standards prepared and already approved by Standards
Australia Limited or other prescribed associations in the regulation;17
 allowing for safety and information standards to incorporate preexisting
materials and instruments, such as equivalent international standards;18
 revising and updating the requirements for nominating alternative
compliance methods for safety standards, enhancing the regulator’s ability
to request producers and suppliers to nominate alternative compliance
methods for standards. This mechanism will also add civil penalties for
failure to comply with the regulator’s directive. The bill would then add
further penalties for non-compliance once an alternative method of
compliance has been nominated by the producer;19 and

Schedule 3 – Increasing the cap on the Housing Australia Special Account
1.16 Schedule 3 of the bill makes changes to the Housing Australia Act 2018 (Housing
Australia Act), to increase the legislated cap of Housing Australia’s Housing
Australia Special Account (HASA).22
1.17 Under section 47D(1) of the Housing Australia Act, the HASA has a maximum
balance cap of $1 billion. Under this legislation any funds that Housing
Australia acquires that exceed the cap are to be returned to the Consolidated
Revenue Fund.23
1.18 The bill proposes amendments to the Housing Australia Act, to raise the
legislated cap for the HASA from $1 billion to $4 billion. This increase of the cap
removes funding constraints for Housing Australia to undertake its programs
to improve housing supply and affordability.
1.19 In the 2024-25 Budget, the government allocated additional credit to Housing
Australia to support the Affordable Housing Bond Aggregator (AHBA)
program, increasing it from $1 billion to $4 billion. This allocation was formally
legislated in the Appropriation Act (No. 2) 2024-25.24
1.20 The bill facilitates that allocation, making those funds accessible to Housing
Australia for the AHBA. Additionally, this increase to the cap further facilitates
the upcoming first rounds of the Housing Australia Future Fund Facility and
the National Housing Accord Facility.25

Schedule 4 – Industry Codes
1.21 Schedule 4 of the bill proposes amendments to part IVB of the Competition and
Consumer Act, increasing the civil penalties for contraventions of the Food and
Grocery Industry Code and Unit Pricing Code.26
1.22 This proposed change implements aspects of the government’s response to the
Independent Review of the Franchising Code of Conduct published in May 2024, and
the Independent Review of the Food and Grocery Code of Conduct, published in June
2024.27
1.23 Schedule 4 expands the civil penalties for breaches of the Food and Grocery and
Unit Pricing codes.28 The bill enacts a maximum pecuniary penalty of $50,000 to
individuals not a part of a body corporate, for those found to be in contravention
of the industry codes.29
1.24 Additionally, the bill provides an updated penalty regime for corporate bodies
who are found to be in contravention of the industry code, including the greater
of:30
 $10 million;
 the value of the benefit obtained directly from the contravention of the
industry code, if it can be determined in court; and
 if a value cannot be determined in court, 10 per cent of the adjusted
turnover of that body corporate during the 12 month period before the
contravention occurred.

Coalition Senators’ additional comments
1.1 In response to Schedule 4—Industry codes, Coalition Senators note that, despite
the Albanese Government’s efforts to strengthen the Mandatory Food and
Grocery and Unit Pricing Codes, these initiatives remain heavily delayed or
deliver insufficient disincentive to anticompetitive behaviour.
The Food and Grocery Code
1.2 The Albanese Government has confirmed that its Mandatory Food and Grocery
Code will not come into force until 1 April 2025.
1.3 This will be nearly 10 months after the Government announced it would accept
all the recommendations of the Emerson Report, including making this Code
mandatory—something the Coalition has been calling for since late 2022.
1.4 Infringement notice penalties under the Code, although raised by the
Government in December, are still capped at $198 000.
1.5 Treasury officials gave evidence at the most recent Senate Estimates hearings in
November 2024 that other recommendations of the Emerson Report, among
them an anonymous complaints mechanism and Code Supervisor, would not
be operational until the Mandatory Food and Grocery Code is implemented.1
Unit Pricing Code
1.6 During the same Senate Estimates hearing, Treasury also confirmed that
consultation on the Unit Pricing Code, announced by Prime Minister Anthony
Albanese in October 2024, would not begin until the first quarter of 2025.2

competition package.

avenue for suppliers;

Coalition Senators’ findings
1.9 The Albanese Government’s delayed response is a case of too little, too late, and
betrays a distracted Government that has lost touch with the urgent need for
cheaper groceries among consumers, paving the way for new entries into the
supermarket sector, and improved opportunities and protections for suppliers.
1.10 This inactivity is taking place against a backdrop of twin cost of living and cost
of doing business crises, fuelled by Labor’s economic mismanagement and
misguided priorities, and has left stakeholders waiting unnecessarily for relief—
including over the last Christmas and summer school holidays.
1.11 Food stress remains endemic, with the 2024 Foodbank Hunger Report revealing
2 million Australian households, or 19 per cent, had experienced severe food
insecurity in the previous 12 months.3
1.12 Meanwhile, court action has been taken against Coles and Woolworths by the
Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) for alleged
anti-competitive activity.
1.13 There are also legitimate concerns that the timing of the upcoming Federal
election risks blowing out delays further still.
1.14 The Albanese Government’s current policy features insufficient disincentive for
anti-competitive conduct, especially among larger operators.
1.15 It was telling that the ACCC told a hearing of the Senate Cost of Living
Committee last year that key barriers to new supermarket entry, including high
establishment costs and logistics, were not addressed by the Government’s
initiatives.4
