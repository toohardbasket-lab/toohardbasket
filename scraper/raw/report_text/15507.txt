Advisory Report on the Royal Commissions Legislation
Amendment (Protections for Providing Information) Bill 2026
March 2026 | Canberra
Background and referral
On 8 January 2026, the Prime Minister announced that a Royal Commission on Antisemitism
and Social Cohesion would be established, to be led by the Hon Virginia Bell AC SC, in
response to the terrorist attack at Bondi Beach on 14 December 2025. The Royal
Commission was asked to complete its report by the end of 2026. Additionally, the
previously-announced independent review into Australia’s federal law enforcement and
intelligence agencies, being conducted by Mr Dennis Richardson, would become part of the
Commission and be required to report by 30 April 2026.1 Letters Patent to establish the
Royal Commission were issued on 9 January 2026.
In Australia, Royal Commissions are the highest form of inquiry on matters of public
importance. A Royal Commission has broad powers to gather information to help with its
inquiry, including powers to summon witnesses and to request individuals or organisations to
produce documents as evidence.2 It is an offence to not comply with notices issued by a
Royal Commission without a reasonable excuse.3
Despite these powers, some people, due to the nature of their work in intelligence and
national security matters, are subject to strict secrecy provisions under a range of laws
prohibiting them from disclosing certain information. Previous reviews have identified such
restrictions may stymie the work of a Royal Commission.4
The Royal Commissions Legislation Amendment (Protections for Providing Information) Bill
2026 (the Bill) proposes changes to the Royal Commissions Act 1902 and the Criminal Code
Act 1995 to establish a framework for the provision of intelligence or operationally sensitive
information to a prescribed Royal Commission. The Bill would provide an immunity from
secrecy provisions where a person provides information to such a Royal Commission
(voluntarily or under compulsion), as long as the provision of information is consistent with
an arrangement entered into between a Commissioner of the Royal Commission and the
heads of the relevant Australian intelligence entities or the Commonwealth in relation to
intelligence or operationally sensitive information, respectively. In relation to a prescribed
Royal Commission, the Bill would also clarify that where a person is compelled to provide
operationally sensitive or intelligence information to a Royal Commission, it is not a
reasonable excuse to fail to provide that information because it would breach a secrecy
provision.

Additionally, the Bill creates a new defence to the general secrecy offence in Division 122 of

The Bill was introduced into the House of Representatives on 2 March 2026, where it passed
on the following day. The Bill is now before the Senate for consideration.
On 4 March 2026, the Attorney-General wrote to the Parliamentary Joint Committee on
Intelligence and Security (PJCIS) referring the Bill for urgent inquiry and report. A copy of the
Attorney-General’s letter is at Attachment A.
The PJCIS adopted the inquiry on 5 March 2026 and held public hearings on 5 and 6 March
2026. The Committee received written submissions from the Attorney-General’s Department,
the Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion and the Human Rights Law
Centre. The Committee also received responses to written questions from a range of other
government agencies. Copies of these submissions and transcripts of the public hearings
are available to view on the Committee’s website.5
In her submission to the Committee, Commissioner Bell confirmed that the Royal
Commission had been consulted on the Bill’s development. She told the Committee that
access to detailed information from intelligence and law enforcement agencies is ‘essential
to effectively discharging paragraph (c) of the Royal Commission’s terms of reference’,
which concerns the circumstances surrounding the antisemitic attack at Bondi Beach.6
Commissioner Bell argued that the ‘urgent progression of these amendments is necessary to
support the Royal Commission’s work’ and that:
The inability to access, or further delay in accessing, information currently protected
by secrecy offences is impeding the Royal Commission’s work, in particular the
preparation of the Interim Report that the Commission is required to deliver by 30
April 2026.7
Committee Comment
The Committee notes that the current Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social
Cohesion responds to the antisemitic shootings at Bondi Beach on 14 December 2025,
which is now considered to be the worst terrorist attack ever to have taken place on
Australian soil. The Royal Commission’s work is both urgent and important.
Whilst secrecy provisions are an important part of the national security legislative framework,
the Committee is concerned that the work of the Royal Commission, and potentially other
future Royal Commissions, is currently impeded by this framework. For Royal Commissions
to be effective, witnesses need to know that they are protected from breaching secrecy
provisions when they provide information.
The Committee considers that the proposed amendments appropriately balance the strong
public interest in prescribed Royal Commissions having unimpeded access to information—
and those providing that information being protected from prosecution—with the equally
important public interest in ensuring intelligence and operationally sensitive information is
protected from public disclosure.

The Committee encourages relevant agencies to be active in ensuring their staff are
informed of their ability to provide information to the Royal Commission, and to provide
trusted and secure channels through which such information can be provided.
The Committee acknowledges that the Human Rights Law Centre, while supporting the Bill,
raised areas in which other amendments could be considered to ensure persons providing
information to Royal Commissions are protected, including in relation to information other
than intelligence and operationally sensitive information. The Committee considers that the
Attorney-General’s Department will have an opportunity to engage with those matters in a
more considered way during future reforms, in particular its upcoming comprehensive reform
package addressing secrecy offences across the Commonwealth statute book.8
The Committee thanks witnesses for their prompt engagement with this inquiry. The
Committee is satisfied that urgent passage of the proposed amendments is needed to
enable the Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion to continue, and to be
completed in a timely manner.
Recommendation
The Committee recommends that the Senate pass the Royal Commissions Legislation
Amendment (Protections for Providing Information) Bill 2026 as soon as possible.

Additional comments from Coalition Members and Senators of the
committee
The protection of Australians and the defence of the nation are among the Commonwealth’s
most serious responsibilities. Legislation affecting Australia’s national security architecture,
including the secrecy frameworks that protect intelligence officers, sources, methods and
operationally sensitive information, must be subject to rigorous parliamentary scrutiny.
The Parliamentary Joint Committee on Intelligence and Security (PJCIS) is the Parliament’s
principal specialist committee for the scrutiny of intelligence and national security matters
referred to it under the Intelligence Services Act 2001. It performs a vital role in building
confidence among elected representatives in the integrity and effectiveness of our
intelligence and security agencies.
Over many years, the PJCIS has contributed to sound and durable legislative outcomes under
governments of both political persuasions, working in a cooperative and bipartisan manner.
That reflects a basic principle: national security legislation should be carefully tested and
improved through proper parliamentary processes, not placed beyond scrutiny by the
executive.
It is therefore regrettable that Coalition members of the PJCIS have been compelled to
provide additional comments in relation to the Royal Commissions Legislation Amendment
(Protections for Providing Information) Bill 2026 (the Bill).
We do so not to frustrate the legitimate objectives of this legislation, objectives which the
Coalition supports, but to place on the public record our serious concerns about the way the
Albanese Government handled the Bill’s introduction and initially resisted, then curtailed,
appropriate parliamentary scrutiny.
This concern does not arise in isolation.
In 2023, Coalition members observed, in relation to the National Security Legislation
Amendment (Comprehensive Review and Other Measures No. 2) Bill 2023, that the
Government’s conduct had been “irregular, rushed and contrary to the good conduct of a
national security inquiry of this importance.”
In 2024, Coalition members made similar observations in their dissenting report on the
Cyber Security Legislative Package 2024, noting that “the condensed inquiry timeframe is
insufficient to properly scrutinise such highly complex and consequential legislation.”
Most recently, in January 2026, Coalition members warned that the Government’s
compression to less than a week of the inquiry into the Combatting Antisemitism, Hate and
4
Extremism Bill 2026 “neutralised the Committee’s capacity to compel evidence, to probe
contested claims, and to test the Bill against operational realities.”
Regrettably, the way in which the present Bill was handled suggests this was not an
aberration, but part of a broader pattern in the Albanese Government’s treatment of the
PJCIS. Coalition members are concerned that the PJCIS’s role as a parliamentary safeguard is
being routinely curtailed, undermining the transparency and accountability that national
security decision-making demands.
Our concern is reinforced by the Attorney-General’s public comments before the referral
occurred. In an interview with ABC Radio National on 27 February 2026, the Attorney-
General, Michelle Rowland, stated:
“We don’t think there is a need for a separate parliamentary inquiry.”
The Coalition’s position was clear. If the PJCIS was to properly discharge its scrutiny function
in relation to this legislation, an inquiry was required.
That position was correct.
This Bill is not minor, technical or merely machinery in nature.
As the Bills Digest explains, the Bill amends the Royal Commissions Act 1902 to create a
framework providing immunity from liability under certain Commonwealth secrecy
provisions where a person provides intelligence or operationally sensitive information to a
Royal Commission consistent with relevant Arrangements. It also amends the Criminal Code
Act 1995 to create an additional defence to certain offences in Division 122 of the Criminal
Code where information is communicated for the primary purpose of providing it to a Royal
Commission and the person reasonably believes the information is relevant.
Legislation of this kind plainly warranted parliamentary scrutiny.
This Bill goes to the heart of the powers available to Royal Commissions to engage with
Australia’s intelligence and security agencies. It will immediately affect the powers available
to the Hon Virginia Bell’s Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion. It is
therefore disappointing that, although the Government announced the legislation on 26
February 2026 and stated that secrecy-offence reform had been underway "for some time",
the Bill was only referred to the PJCIS for urgent inquiry by letter of 4 March 2026 - two days
after introduction. The Bill should have been referred immediately so that the PJCIS could
properly commence its work.
In answer to a Question on Notice, the Attorney-General's Department confirmed that the
Bill was not even finalised until 27 February 2026 - the day after it was publicly announced.
The fact that the Government announced legislation and then waited a further five days
before referring it to the PJCIS, makes the compressed timeframe for scrutiny even more
difficult to justify.
This is not a mere procedural complaint: it goes to the heart of parliamentary accountability.
The PJCIS exists so that difficult, technical and/or sensitive proposals can be tested before
5
they become law. When the Government curtails that scrutiny, it diminishes the Parliament’s
capacity to perform its role and reflects a troubling attitude towards the PJCIS’s statutory
function, treating scrutiny as an inconvenience rather than a safeguard.
A further concern was the timing of the PJCIS’s access to the Arrangements contemplated by
the Bill, which are central to the practical operation of the proposed scheme.
As set out in the Attorney-General’s letter to the Chair of the PJCIS dated 4 March 2026:
“…the proposed amendments would provide immunity from secrecy provisions where
a person either voluntarily provides, or is compelled to provide, information to a
Royal Commission. For those protections to apply, the disclosure must occur
consistently with either:
• an arrangement entered into between a Commissioner of a Royal Commission
and the heads of the relevant intelligence entities, in relation to intelligence
information; or
• an arrangement entered into between a Commissioner of a Royal Commission
and the Commonwealth, in relation to operationally sensitive information”.
In the case of the Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion, there are two
such Arrangements.
Proper scrutiny required the PJCIS to examine the Arrangements alongside the Bill itself. That
did not occur in any meaningful timeframe.
As Senator Cash observed during the public hearing, the PJCIS received the Arrangements
only shortly before the hearing commenced on 5 March 2026.
Mr Chris Taylor of the Australian Strategic Policy Institute, who gave evidence on 6 March
2026, indicated he had only become aware of the Arrangements from the previous evening’s
testimony and had to search for them on the Royal Commission’s website. Mr Taylor was
also the only external witness able to appear given the inquiry’s extremely short notice.
Those matters underscore the extent to which the PJCIS’s consideration of the Bill was
unnecessarily truncated. Where the operation of legislation depends on supporting
Arrangements of this kind, those materials should be available in sufficient time to permit
proper examination before evidence is taken.
The need for these reforms was not newly discovered in 2026.
In December 2022, the then Attorney-General, Mark Dreyfus, asked the Attorney-General's
Department to undertake a review of Commonwealth secrecy provisions. The Government
released the final report in November 2023.
Recommendation 7 of that report recommended improving protections for individuals
providing information to Royal Commissions by amending the Royal Commissions Act 1902
to establish a framework clarifying the application of secrecy offences and protective
security requirements, and by amending section 122.5 of the Criminal Code Act 1995 to
include an additional defence where information is communicated for the purposes of a
6
Royal Commission. The Bills Digest records that Recommendation 7 in part reiterated
Recommendation 6 of the interim report of the Royal Commission into Defence and Veteran
Suicide.
It was also cited to the PJCIS that this legislation was required to address gaps in the
Telecommunications (Interception and Access) Act 1979 concerning electronic surveillance
material - gaps that were anticipated to be addressed through the introduction of a new
Electronic Surveillance Act. Work on that legislation has been underway since at least 2023,
yet no Bill has been introduced.
In short, the core reforms in this Bill were identified well before the present Royal
Commission encountered the difficulties to which the Government now points.
In answer to another Question on Notice, the Attorney-General’s Department confirmed
that the Government formally agreed to implement Recommendation 7 on 21 November
2023. The Government therefore agreed over two years ago to enact the very reforms now
contained in this Bill yet took no legislative action until the Royal Commission encountered
practical difficulties in early 2026. That is not a matter of competing priorities, it is a failure
of legislative follow-through on the Government’s own stated commitments.
The answers to Question 5 on notice further confirmed that not only the
Telecommunications (Interception and Access) Act 1979 but also the Surveillance Devices Act
2004 have prevented or delayed the production of certain material to the Royal Commission.
The involvement of two separate electronic surveillance statutes underscores the breadth of
the legislative gap that the delayed Electronic Surveillance Act was intended to address.
The urgency with which the Government now seeks to advance this Bill is, at least in part, a
product of its own failure to respond in a timely way to recommendations made through its
own review process.
Coalition members therefore place on record both our support for the passage of the Bill
and our serious concern at the way the Government handled it. We record our serious
concern at what has become a pattern of constraining and curtailing the work of the PJCIS.
National security legislation should not be rushed through the Parliament on the assumption
that scrutiny is dispensable. The role of the PJCIS is fundamental, not ornamental.
Governments are not entitled to sideline or diminish that role simply because proper
scrutiny proves inconvenient. When they attempt to do so, the Parliament should say so
plainly.
Clear guidance to agency staff
An important practical issue remains:
• how current and former officials, employees, officers, affiliates, agents and other persons
with relevant information will become aware of the new pathway, understand the scope
and limits of the protections; and
• know how to engage with the Royal Commission safely and lawfully.
7
The Bill's effective operation depends on whether relevant personnel understand how the
framework works in practice: what the lawful pathway is, what conditions must be met,
what secure channels are to be used, and what protections apply.
The Bills Digest makes clear that the broader immunity mechanism in the Royal
Commissions Act 1902 depends on disclosure occurring consistently with the relevant
Arrangements and that the proposed section 6PG provides for those Arrangements to be
published as a notifiable instrument. Publication is a necessary starting point, but it is not
sufficient on its own. The Bill creates two distinct protective mechanisms that operate
differently, and personnel must understand the practical significance of each.
Proposed section 6PD of the Royal Commissions Act 1902 provides the broader immunity
where the statutory conditions are met. Schedule 3 of the Bill amends section 122.5 of the
Criminal Code Act 1995 to create an additional, narrower defence in relation to certain
offences in Division 122 where information is communicated or dealt with for the primary
purpose of providing it to a Royal Commission and the person reasonably believes the
information is relevant.
Those distinct mechanisms should be clearly explained to relevant personnel.
The answers to a further Question on Notice reveal a critical further dimension. Where a
person discloses intelligence or operationally sensitive information outside the
Arrangements, the full Schedule 1 immunity is unavailable.
The importance therefore of the Arrangements and the Bill they support cannot be
overstated. Clear internal guidance is not a peripheral administrative matter. It is essential to
ensuring that voluntary disclosure occurs lawfully, securely and with confidence and to
minimising the risk of uncertainty, inconsistency or informal workarounds in the handling of
sensitive material before it is received by the Royal Commission.
Recommendation 1
Coalition members recommend that the head of each relevant intelligence entity, as set out
in the Parties Clause to the Arrangements, in consultation with the Attorney-General’s
Department and the Royal Commission, issue clear written guidance to all staff and, where
applicable, other personnel, as soon as practicable, setting out:
1. the ability of a person to engage voluntarily with the Royal Commission in accordance
with the Arrangements;
2. the requirement to comply with the Arrangements and to use secure authorised
channels, and the consequences of acting outside the Arrangements - including potential
exposure to criminal liability under agency-specific secrecy laws and loss of the immunity
provided by the proposed section 6PD(3);
3. the protections available under the proposed section 6PD and related provisions where
the statutory conditions are met;
8
4. the distinction between the broader immunity mechanism under the Royal Commissions
Act 1902 and the narrower Criminal Code defence in Schedule 3;
5. the legal advice and assistance mechanisms available to persons considering
engagement with the Royal Commission, including, the National Legal Aid service

Coalition members note Mr Chris Taylor’s evidence that practical issues may still arise in the
handling of documents and classified material before they are received by the Royal
Commission.
Mr Taylor emphasised the importance of agencies providing secure channels through which
staff may engage with the Royal Commission, so that sensitive material does not leave
classified systems or become exposed through informal workarounds. Coalition members
regard this as an important implementation matter.
Further PJCIS review
Coalition members also note that the Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social
Cohesion is required to provide an interim report by 30 April 2026.
In answer to a Question on Notice, the Attorney-General’s Department provided no
commitment to any formal review of the Bill’s operation following the interim report, stating
only that it “will continue dialogue” and will “consider any recommendations.” That
response falls well short of the structured parliamentary oversight this legislation warrants
and reinforces the need for the PJCIS to conduct a formal review.
Coalition members consider that the practical operation of the Bill and the associated
Arrangements should be revisited after the interim report has been delivered. Parliamentary
scrutiny should not end with the passage of legislation where operational experience may
reveal further issues. Such a review would enable the PJCIS to assess whether the scheme
has operated as intended, including in relation to voluntary disclosures, secure handling
pathways, agency communication to staff, and any unintended operational consequences
and whether any further changes are required.
Coalition members regard this as a prudent follow-up measure given the compressed
timeframe within which this legislation was considered.
Recommendation 2
Coalition members recommend that, following delivery of the Royal Commission’s interim
report, the operation of the Bill and the associated Arrangements be reviewed by the PJCIS
9
to assess whether the scheme has operated as intended and whether any further legislative
or administrative changes are required.
Scope of the Bill’s protective framework
The answers to Questions on Notice, arising from a submission by the Human Rights Law
Centre, identified a significant policy question about the scope of the Bill’s protective
framework that Coalition members consider warrants further attention.
As the Human Rights Law Centre observed in its submission of 5 March 2026, the Bill’s
immunity mechanism applies only to intelligence information and operationally sensitive
information. A person who holds other information subject to Commonwealth secrecy
provisions, information that may nonetheless be directly relevant to the Royal Commission’s
inquiry receives no equivalent immunity. Such a person may still face criminal liability under
sector-specific secrecy offences if they disclose that information voluntarily and can only rely
on the narrower Schedule 3 Criminal Code defence, which places an evidential burden on
the person relying on the defence.
The Human Rights Law Centre gave the example of a Department of Education employee
who holds information relevant to the Royal Commission’s inquiry but subject to federal
secrecy offences relating to childcare. That employee, if they chose to disclose voluntarily,
may face potential criminal liability without the benefit of the Schedule 1 immunity, even
though they are seeking to do the same thing, in the public interest, as an intelligence
official who would have full protection. In answer to Questions on Notice, the Attorney-
General’s Department acknowledged this but stated that the Bill was “targeted” to address
identified issues with intelligence and operationally sensitive information and that existing
compulsory notice powers remain available for other types of information.
The Department did not directly address the policy justification for treating persons holding
different categories of secret but relevant information differently when both are seeking to
assist a Royal Commission in the public interest. Coalition members consider that this is a
matter of genuine policy concern, particularly given the broad remit of the Royal
Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion and the potential relevance of non-
intelligence information across a wide range of Commonwealth agencies.
The issue is likely to be even more significant for future Royal Commissions without a
national security focus, where the absence of any equivalent immunity could deter voluntary
engagement entirely.
Recommendation 3
Coalition members recommend that the Government review whether the immunity
framework in Schedule 1 to the Bill should be extended to cover information subject to
Commonwealth secrecy provisions beyond intelligence information and operationally
sensitive information, and report to the PJCIS on that review within six months of
commencement of this Act.
