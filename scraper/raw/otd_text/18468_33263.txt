Australian Government response to the
Senate Select Committee on PFAS (per and
polyfluoroalkyl substances) ’Inquiry into the Extent,
Regulation and Management of PFAS’ Final Report
OCTOBER 2026
Overview
On 22 August 2024, the Senate appointed the Select Committee on PFAS (per and
polyfluoroalkyl substances) (the Committee), to conduct an Inquiry into the Extent,
Regulation and Management of PFAS. The Committee delivered its final report on
19 November 2025. The final report makes 47 committee recommendations; 25 chair
recommendations and Labor Senators made a further 7 recommendations.
The Australian Government welcomes the Committee’s final report and the opportunity
to respond to its 79 recommendations.
The Government responded in November 2025 to the three recommendations made by
the Committee in its interim report.
The Government also acknowledges the Joint Standing Committee on Foreign Affairs,
Defence and Trade 2022 Inquiry into PFAS remediation in and around Defence bases
resulting in 12 recommendations focused on the investigation and communication of
PFAS and support for PFAS affected communities.
The 2022 and 2025 reports each covered:
• Communication of government activities around PFAS, especially for PFAS-
affected communities.
• Coordination between levels of government to investigate and fund the
remediation of PFAS contamination.
• Consideration and funding of research into the health effects of PFAS exposure.
Given the comprehensive nature of the 2025 Inquiry report and the similarity in themes
arising, the Government considers that this response also appropriately addresses the
recommendations from the 2022 inquiry.
PFAS are a diverse group of over 4,700 chemicals that have a wide range of chemical
structures. This means that different PFAS have different properties and risk
characteristics. In Australia, PFAS have been used for a long time in a wide range of
consumer products and industrial applications, including certain firefighting foams.
The Government acknowledges PFAS is a significant environment, health and cultural
concern for many communities across Australia. PFAS use and contamination is a
complex and evolving subject, and the Government is committed to taking a
transparent, scientific and risk-proportionate approach to their control.
The Government thanks the Committee for its diligence and thoroughness in inquiring
into PFAS in Australia. The Government also thanks those organisations, community
groups and individuals that made submissions or appeared at hearings for this inquiry.

Recommendation 1
11.37 The committee recommends that Food Standards Australia and New Zealand
(FSANZ) reviews the Tolerable Daily Intakes for PFAS against the latest international
research and regulatory settings and updated Australian Drinking Water Guidelines,
and presents a report to the Australian Parliament—no later than October 2026—
which outlines the findings of the review.
11.38 As part of this review, FSANZ should reconsider the need to establish regulatory
limits on PFAS in food, taking into account a holistic quantification of population
PFAS exposure.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
The Australian Government considers health‑based guidance values should be
reviewed when substantive new scientific evidence emerges, rather than on a fixed
schedule. FSANZ will re‑evaluate the PFAS Tolerable Daily Intakes (TDIs) if credible new
evidence indicates the existing values are no longer appropriate.
The TDIs for PFAS were established by FSANZ following a comprehensive toxicological
evaluation of laboratory animal and human studies, and were agreed by the Australian
Health Ministers’ Advisory Committee in 2017. FSANZ continues to monitor and assess
emerging scientific evidence on the health effects of PFAS. Studies underpinning the
updated ADWGs found no evidence to justify a change to the TDI values established in
2017. FSANZ remains confident the current values are appropriate for dietary risk
assessment purposes.
FSANZ also considers total dietary exposure to PFAS through the Australian Total Diet
Study program. These assessments consistently show PFAS exposure from food for the
general Australian population is low and does not indicate a need to establish
regulatory limits in foods.

Recommendation 3
11.78 The committee recommends that the Australian Government considers
providing subsidised PFAS blood testing, enhanced health and cancer screening, and
targeted mental health support for those people with the highest levels of PFAS
exposure, including some firefighters, people exposed at work, and residents of

The Australian Government agrees in principle to this recommendation.

The Population Based Screening Framework sets out the principles of screening

An estimated $8.4 billion is being invested in mental health and suicide prevention

Recommendation 8
11.117 The committee recommends the Australian Government:
• works with the states and territories to ensure that workers are protected from
unnecessary PFAS exposure in all states and territories;
• reviews relevant Commonwealth work health and safety legislation,
regulations, and official advice with the aim of ensuring a consistent approach
to occupational PFAS exposure—and actively encourages states and
territories to do the same;
• amends the Australian Code for the Transport of Dangerous Goods by Road &
Rail to classify PFAS as a 'dangerous good'; and
• develops a publicly available online resource to provide state regulators,
employers, and employees with information about PFAS as a workplace
hazard, including how to initiate and progress remediation of contamination.
11.118 Where necessary, work health and safety regulations should also be amended
to extend provisions to employees of private companies working on PFAS
remediation, such as for Defence.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
Work health and safety laws (WHS) place a primary duty of care on persons conducting
a business or undertaking (PCBU), such as employers, to eliminate or minimise WHS
risks to the extent reasonably practicable. This includes risks arising from PFAS
exposure in the workplace.
6
WHS laws are largely harmonised through the model WHS laws adopted by most
jurisdictions. The Government works with jurisdictions, unions and employers through
Safe Work Australia (SWA) to ensure the model WHS laws provide a balanced and
nationally consistent framework to safeguard Australian workers and workplaces.
While not specifically listed, the transport of PFAS is already captured under the
National Transport Commission’s Australian Code for the Transport of Dangerous
Goods (ADG Code) when it meets the classification criteria as an environmentally
hazardous substance.
Recommendation 9
11.119 The committee recommends the Australian Government:
• works with relevant state and territory regulators to ensure that all veterans
and firefighters exposed to PFAS— including members of the Australian Army
Fire Service and Retired Firefighters' Group—are considered and eligible as
part of any remediation, research, testing, and support programs; and
• includes Airservices Australia Emergency Vehicle Technicians in its workers
compensation scheme for firefighters, by amending the Safety, Rehabilitation
and Compensation Act 1988 (Cth) (SRC Act).
The Australian Government agrees in principle the first point of this recommendation
as it relates to veterans.
Veterans should be included in any forthcoming research, testing, and support
programs and DVA will continue to liaise with relevant authorities to advocate for their
inclusion in the PFAS specific programs.
DVA already provides coverage for medical conditions arising from Defence service
under the Military Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 2004 (MRCA), including
presumptive arrangements for certain defined conditions for Defence firefighters where
relevant criteria are met.
It should be noted, current presumptive arrangements were not implemented as a
response to concerns about PFAS exposure, but in response to concerns about other
chemical exposures. Where a causal connection between PFAS exposure and medical
conditions is definitively established, this will be incorporated within DVA’s liability
frameworks.
The Australian Government notes the second point of this recommendation.
Workers’ compensation in Australia is administered separately by Commonwealth,
states and territories under their respective schemes. The Commonwealth framework
7
SRC Act is informed by SWA’s Deemed Diseases in Australia List, which refers to
firefighters as an occupational group. Any extension of presumptive coverage to other
occupations such as emergency vehicle technicians must be supported by
epidemiological research.
Airservices Australia (ASA) employees are covered under the SRC Act. Employees
classified as ‘firefighters’ including those whose firefighting or related duties form a not
insubstantial part of their duties have access to relevant presumptive provisions.
Recommendation 10
11.120 The committee recommends the Australian Government works with state and
territory work health and safety regulators to develop a national regulatory framework
which enables state and territory WorkSafe agencies to issue notices and progress
regulatory responses to PFAS exposure in Australian workplaces.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
WHS is legislated and regulated separately by the Commonwealth, state and territories.
WHS regulators participate in the Heads of Workplace Safety Authorities (HWSA) forum
to promote consistent regulatory and enforcement approaches across Australia and
New Zealand.
Inspectors can issue compliance notices where they reasonably believe a WHS law has
been breached, including improvement notices, prohibition notices, non-disturbance
notices, and infringement notices.
Recommendation 11
12.23 The committee recommends the Australian Government:
• fast-tracks the deployment of PFAS-free firefighting foams to replace all legacy
PFAS-containing foams;
• moves to quickly replace all legacy firefighting equipment that still utilises or
stores PFAS-containing foams, and ensures the safe disposal of existing
stocks;
• bans or severely restricts the use of firefighting foams containing PFAS
by Commonwealth entities; and
• works through cross jurisdictional bodies to achieve an urgent national ban on
the use of PFAS-containing firefighting foams, including by private sector
entities, major hazard facilities and B Class foam extinguishers in Australia.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
8
Consistent with the National PFAS Position Statement, the Australian Government
supports reducing PFAS use wherever practicable, including transitioning away from
uses that may cause irreversible or long-term environmental harm.
The Industrial Chemicals Environmental Management Standard (IChEMS) agreed by all
Australian Governments establishes nationally consistent standards and controls for
managing environmental risks from industrial chemicals and promotes the use of safer
alternatives.
Under the IChEMS, national risk management standards have been established to
protect the environment from over 1500 different PFAS. A ban on the import,
manufacture and use has been applied to 5 groups and apply to around 780 individual
substances whether on their own, in mixtures or in articles, including when used in
firefighting foams.
Jurisdictions are at various stages of implementing the IChEMS. Queensland, NSW, ACT
and Victoria already have legislation in place to enforce the standards within
their jurisdictions. The Australian Government expects compliance with these
standards and is working with relevant Australian Government agencies to give effect to
border controls.
Recommendation 12
12.26 The committee recommends that the Australian Government provides funding
for toxicological studies of fluorine-free foams so as to ensure that legacy PFAS-
containing foams are not replaced by the next harmful products.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
Any new industrial chemicals (not listed on the Australian Inventory of Industrial
Chemicals) introduced (imported or manufactured) to Australia to replace PFAS
ingredients of foams would be subject to the authorisation requirements in the
Industrial Chemicals Act 2019. Under these requirements, funding of toxicological
studies is the responsibility of the chemical introducer.

Recommendation 13
12.30 The committee recommends that the Australian Government works with states
and territories to establish a fund and taskforce to support the remediation of
severely PFAS-contaminated sites, including current and former fire stations (and
surrounds), firefighting training facilities, and nearby water catchments that have
been impacted, and ensures the availability of uncontaminated drinking water at all
fire stations.
12.31 To complement this initiative, the Australian Government should establish an
expert tripartite panel comprised of government, private sector and unions, to:
• advise on reducing PFAS exposure risk to workers;
• advise on effective remediation of PFAS-contaminated sites;
• advise on new and established techniques and technologies; and
• encourage further innovation through supporting site remediation research
and development.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
The Government is committed to working with the states and territories through Heads
of EPA Australia and New Zealand (HEPA) to strengthen nationally consistent
approaches to PFAS contamination and remediation, recognising that remediation
requirements vary by site and jurisdiction.
The Intergovernmental Agreement on a National Framework for Responding to PFAS
Contamination supports coordinated, risk-based responses that protect the
environment and human health.
The Environmental Health Standing Committee (enHealth) brings together Australian
state and territory health departments, the National Health and Medical Research
Council (NHMRC) and HEPA to provide advice and make recommendations to the
Australian Health Protection Committee and through it, to Health Ministers on
environmental health matters including water quality and assessment of associated
public health risks.
Worker protection from PFAS exposure is supported through state and territory work
health and safety (WHS) laws. These laws place a primary duty of care on PCBUs, such
as employers to ensure workers not exposed to health and safety risks to the extent
reasonably practicable. This includes risks arising from exposure to PFAS in the
workplace.
The Department of Defence (Defence) and Airservices Australia administer remediation
programs for sites under their jurisdiction, while state-level environment protection
authorities oversee state-level remediation.
10
In December 2021, DCCEEW received $12.6 million over four years to support
consistent contamination management standards and expert advice for complex sites,
across the Commonwealth estate. This supported the establishment and ongoing
delivery of an environmental contamination advice function, within the National EPA to
assist agencies in dealing with contamination across the Commonwealth estate.

Recommendation 15
12.41 The committee recommends that the Australian Government funds and
coordinates a strategic national PFAS environmental monitoring program to track
contamination levels and trends in the Australian environment, including key
waterways and native wildlife species, and publish emerging data.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
The PFAS National Environmental Management Plan, coordinated by the National EPA,
provides nationally consistent guidance for the planning and design of environmental
monitoring, and is regularly updated to reflect new scientific evidence. States and
territories are responsible for undertaking monitoring, assessment and analysis within
their jurisdictions and refer to the PFAS National Environmental Management Plan as a
framework for these activities.
The Commonwealth undertakes monitoring on Commonwealth land through existing
programs, including the Department of Defence’s PFAS Investigation and Management
Program (for Defence properties) and Airservices Australia’s National PFAS
11
Management Program (for airports) which operate in accordance with national and
state guidelines.

drinking water supply to the residents of the Jervis Bay Territory (JBT), including the
Aboriginal community at Wreck Bay, with a new water pipeline connecting JBT to the
Shoalhaven City Council’s water distribution network.

13
Shoalhaven City Council-owned Vincentia Reservoir and the Stoney Creek Reservoir in

Testing of Jervis Bay Territory drinking water is undertaken by Shoalhaven City Council to

Recommendation 20
12.89 The committee recommends that Australian Government agencies involved in

communities.

Northern Territory Government, and key organisations to progress these commitments.

The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
The ADWGs provide nationally recognised settings for safe drinking water, including for
PFAS and other contaminants, and support risk-based, site-specific monitoring and
treatment based on local conditions. Responsibility for drinking water testing rests with
water suppliers, while state and territory governments establish the monitoring
requirements and standards for drinking water suppliers within their jurisdictions.

Recommendation 1
1.46 That the Australian Government considers the need to establish periodic reviews
of the Tolerable Daily Intakes for PFAS and other chemicals of concern.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
The Australian Government considers that reviews of health-based guidance are best
informed by scientific evidence, rather than fixed review timeframes. FSANZ actively
monitors and assesses emerging scientific PFAS research and will review the TDIs if
credible new evidence indicates the current values no longer remain health protective.
FSANZ has assessed new evidence since the values were established in 2017, including
reviews of immune system effects and studies supporting updates to the ADWGs, and
found no basis for revising current values.
FSANZ will continue to monitor international assessments including the scheduled
2027 review by the Joint United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization and the
World Health Organization Expert Committee on Food Additives, when considering
future action.

Recommendation 6
1.66 In addition to Recommendation 5, the Australian Government should:

Recommendation 7
1.103 That the Australian Government considers restricting or phasing out uses of
PFAS in consumer products where PFAS is not essential to the product and is readily
replaceable. Regulation facilitating these restrictions or phase outs should also
consider and endeavour to mitigate against the risk of substitutions for other PFAS-
like chemicals or potentially hazardous alternatives.
The Australian Government notes this recommendation.
Consistent with the National PFAS Position Statement the Australian Government
supports reducing PFAS use wherever practicable, including transitioning away from
uses that may cause irreversible or long-term environmental contamination.
The Industrial Chemicals Environmental Management Standard (IChEMS) establishes
national standards for managing industrial chemicals, including PFAS, and applies
restrictions and prohibitions where appropriate. As the IChEMS expands it will support
the identification and adoption of safer alternatives while reducing environmental risks
from high concern chemicals.
