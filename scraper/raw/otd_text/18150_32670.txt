Australian Government Response to the
Parliamentary Joint Committee on Law Enforcement report:
Law Enforcement Capabilities in relation to Child Exploitation
September 2026
1
Australian Government response to the Parliamentary Joint Committee on
Law Enforcement report on Law enforcement capabilities in relation to
child exploitation
Every child and young person has the right to be safe from child sexual abuse. It is our
collective responsibility to ensure that strong frameworks are in place to create safe
environments for children in all settings. The Australian Government is committed to
preventing and responding to child sexual abuse and protecting children and young people
from abuse and harm more broadly. Australia is well placed to deliver on these through the
National Strategy to Prevent and Respond to Child Sexual Abuse 2021-2030, a 10 year
whole-of nation framework that provides a coordinated and consistent approach addressing
child sexual abuse in all settings.
Preventing and responding to child sexual abuse and exploitation requires whole-of-
government and whole of society efforts. The law enforcement agencies tasked with
investigating these matters collaborate domestically, internationally and across all sectors,
supported by the necessary powers – and appropriate checks and balances – to protect
children in Australia and around the world.
Online child sexual exploitation and abuse remains a global challenge, exacerbated by
evolving technologies. Australia has a world leading framework to address this, underpinned
by robust and technology neutral criminal offences and complementary regulatory schemes
which are regularly reviewed to ensure they remain fit for purpose. Australia has taken
significant action in our efforts to prevent child sexual exploitation and abuse and support
victims and survivors, including through national support services and a wide array of
awareness raising and education measures.
The Government thanks the Committee for its work on these important issues.
The Government provides the following responses to the Committee’s recommendations.
Recommendation 1
The committee recommends that the Australian Government regularly monitor whether the
criminal offence framework adequately covers predatory conduct in relation to child abuse
material and child exploitation, and, if inadequacies are identified, prioritise legislation to
remedy them.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government continually monitors the operation of its laws to ensure they keep pace with
technological developments, offending trends, operational needs and societal expectations.
The Criminal Code Act 1995 (Cth) (the Criminal Code) includes a robust framework of
Commonwealth offences relating to child sexual abuse and child abuse material. States and
territories have responsibility for matters related to child sexual abuse within their
jurisdictions.
Under measure 13 of the First Commonwealth Action Plan of the National Strategy to
Prevent and Respond to Child Sexual Abuse (2021-2030), the Attorney-General’s
2
Department is currently reviewing Commonwealth child sexual abuse offences, assessing
their adaptability to modern trends and consulting with relevant agencies on reform options.
Further, in August 2022 the then Meeting of Attorneys-General (now the Standing Council of
Attorneys-General) endorsed the Work Plan to Strengthen Criminal Justice Responses to
Sexual Assault 2022 – 2027 (the Work Plan). Under the Work Plan, jurisdictions are taking
collective and individual action to improve the experiences of victims and survivors of sexual
assault in the criminal justice system. Under the Work Plan, the Attorney-General’s
Department and Australian Institute of Criminology have conducted a comprehensive review
of sexual assault and child sexual abuse legislation across the Commonwealth, states and
territories. This included consultations with victims and survivors and expert stakeholders
and explored inconsistencies in the legislative landscape. The final report was published by
the Australian Institute of Criminology in October 2024.
Recommendation 2
The committee recommends that the Australian Government ensure that data is recorded
that reflects the total effective sentence handed down to child sex offenders, and
continually monitor the appropriateness of these sentences.
The Government notes this recommendation.
The Commonwealth Director of Public Prosecutions (CDPP) captures sentencing data that
attaches to each charge for which an offender is sentenced, and has amended internal
procedures to ensure total effective sentences are recorded on its case management system
following a sentence judgment.
It is a matter for the judiciary to determine an appropriate sentence, considering the nature of
the offending and the personal circumstances of the offender, while considering the
maximum sentence and any mandatory minimum sentence provisions which may apply.
As required by the Crimes Legislation Amendment (Sexual Crimes Against Children and
Community Protection Measures) Act 2020 (SCAC Act), the Attorney-General’s Department
undertook a review of the first three years of operation of certain amendments to Part IB of
the Crimes Act 1914 (Cth). These amendments included mandatory minimum sentences for
some child sex offences and repeat offenders, as well as presumptions in favour of
cumulative sentences and actual terms of imprisonment. The review report was tabled in
Parliament on 21 August 2024. It found that the SCAC Act had achieved its objectives in part
(noting that the full impact of the amendments was unlikely to have been realised in the
three-year review period, particularly given the impact of COVID-19 on the court system).
The review made three recommendations for minor technical legislative amendments to
certain provisions introduced by the SCAC Act to clarify certain ambiguities or unintended
consequences in relation to their application. These recommendations are under consideration
by Government.

Recommendation 3
The committee recommends that the Australian Government consider permitting the
submission of community impact statements to a court, including the following statements
supported by the Australian Federal Police and the Australian Centre to Counter Child
Exploitation:
• statements from victim-survivors depicted in material found in the offender’s
possession, including where the offender is not responsible for producing,
procuring or soliciting that material directly from the victim; and
• community impact statements (also known as group impact statements) on behalf of
victim-survivors depicted in child abuse material available online, none of whom
are necessarily depicted in the material in the offender’s possession.
The Government notes this recommendation.
The Government supports a range of initiatives that ensure the voices of victims and
survivors are heard throughout the court process, and to improve supports available for
victims and survivors. The Attorney-General’s Department continues to engage with agencies
that have an interest in protecting victims and survivors of child sexual abuse, including
Commonwealth agencies and state and territory law enforcement, prosecutorial bodies and
justice agencies to ensure victims and survivors are supported and empowered, particularly
through the criminal justice system.
The National Strategy to Prevent and Respond to Child Sexual Abuse 2021-2030 is a
whole-of-nation framework to establish a coordinated and consistent approach to preventing
and better responding to child sexual abuse in all settings. The Government is progressing a
range of measures to support and empower victims and survivors of child sexual abuse,
including in relation to access to justice. Measure 9 of the First National Action Plan of the
National Strategy aims to enhance legislative protections for vulnerable witnesses giving
evidence in criminal proceedings for child sexual abuse-related Commonwealth offences.
The Crimes Amendment (Strengthening the Criminal Justice Response to Sexual Violence)
Act 2024 passed Parliament on 29 November 2024. The Act strengthens protections for
vulnerable persons in Commonwealth criminal proceedings by enhancing safeguards and
implementing trauma-informed measures that better support vulnerable persons when
appearing as witnesses or complainants in criminal proceedings.
Further, through the Work Plan, the Government is committed to taking action to improve the
experiences of victims and survivors of sexual assault in the criminal justice system. In line
with Priority 2 of the Work Plan (building justice sector capability to better support and
protect victims and survivors), the Attorney-General’s Department is undertaking a scoping
study to identify options for providing greater access to witness intermediary schemes. These
schemes provide access to trained professionals who support witnesses, including
complainants, to give their best evidence in Commonwealth criminal proceedings.
The scoping study, which is expected to be completed in 2026, is examining whether there
are opportunities to establish a new standalone Commonwealth witness intermediary scheme,
4
or whether there are opportunities to improve access for Commonwealth witnesses to existing
state and territory witness intermediary schemes.
Recommendation 4
The committee recommends that the Australian Government ensure that an appropriate
ministerial council regularly considers coordination and legislative challenges facing
Commonwealth, state and territory law enforcement in relation to child exploitation.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government will continue to use existing governance arrangements and ministerial
forums to ensure there is appropriate coordination and consideration of matters relating to
child sexual abuse and exploitation. This includes the forums set out below.
Beyond these governance arrangements, the Government continues to engage with state and
territories, law enforcement bodies, industry and civil society on an ad hoc basis through
roundtables and workshops to collectively discuss and identify opportunities and challenges
on the issue of child sexual exploitation and abuse.
Standing Council of Attorneys-General
The Standing Council of Attorneys-General (SCAG) is a quarterly meeting of
Attorneys-General from the Australian Government, state and territories, and the
New Zealand Minister of Justice. The purpose of SCAG is to implement a national and
trans-Tasman focus on maintaining and promoting best practice in law reform. A strategic
priority for SCAG is responses to child, family, domestic and sexual violence.
As an example, at the SCAG meeting on 14 November 2025, all Attorneys-General endorsed
an Agreement to deliver National Working with Children Check (WWCC) Reform. The
Agreement reflects a coordinated commitment by the Australian, state and territory
governments to deliver a national approach to WWCCs based on mutual recognition,
strengthening child safety by addressing inconsistencies within and across WWCC schemes.
Police Ministers Council
The Police Ministers Council comprises the Minister for Home Affairs, the police ministers
from every Australian state and territory and the New Zealand police minister. The Council
fosters a coordinated and collaborative approach to law enforcement priorities across all
jurisdictions to progress national policing initiatives and achieve improved law enforcement
policy outcomes for Australian and New Zealand citizens. The Council’s current strategic
priorities include preventing and responding to child sexual abuse.

Recommendation 5
The committee recommends that the Australian Government prioritise and pursue efforts,
in collaboration with state and territory governments, to:
• enable the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission and the Australian
Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre to access the National Child Offender
System; and
• enable the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre to access the
National Police Reference System.
The Government agrees in-principle with this recommendation.
The Government acknowledges the importance of the National Child Offender System and
National Police Reference System to strengthen Commonwealth, state and territory law
enforcement capabilities.
The National Child Offender System allows Australian police to record and share child
offender information. It directly enables police in each state and territory to manage key
information to meet their requirements under respective child protection legislation. It
consists of:
• the Australian National Child Offender Register, which allows authorised police
officers to register, case manage and share information about registered persons, and
• the Managed Person System, which holds information on alleged offenders who are
charged but not convicted, or after an offender’s reporting obligations have been
completed.
The Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade also has access to information from the
Australian National Child Offender Register for the purposes of performing functions under
the Australian Passports Act 2005 (Cth).
However, because the National Child Offender System is operated by state and territory
agencies in accordance with their respective legislation, legislative changes are likely
required by state and territories in order to facilitate access by the Australian Criminal
Intelligence Commission (ACIC) and the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre
(AUSTRAC). The Attorney-General’s Department will continue to engage with jurisdictions
to discuss legislative reform.
As Australia’s financial intelligence unit, AUSTRAC’s access to national policing
information, including through access to the National Criminal Intelligence System (NCIS),
is vital for effective intelligence generation. AUSTRAC’s inability to directly access or ingest
NCIS data greatly limits the effectiveness of its financial intelligence function, including its
efforts to counter child exploitation. AUSTRAC continues to work with the ACIC to gain
NCIS access. The ACIC is progressing work to enhance information sharing across the law
enforcement, intelligence and regulatory domains. AUSTRAC is a member of the ACIC-
chaired NCIS Steering Committee which endorsed activities to support NCIS onboarding and
prioritisation of AUSTRAC’s NCIS access at its March 2026 meeting.

Recommendation 6
The committee recommends that the Australian Federal Police and the Commonwealth
Director of Public Prosecutions:
• adopt, as far as is practicable, procedures and technologies that minimise exposure
to child abuse material while still ensuring that investigations are properly
conducted and courts are properly informed about the severity of offending; and
• work with state and territory authorities to share best practice and improve the use
of such procedures and technologies in all Australian jurisdictions.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government acknowledges the importance of adopting practices to minimise exposure to
child abuse material in the course of investigations, as well as sharing best practice with state
and territory authorities.
Emerging technology and current procedures
Science and technology-enabled capabilities are central to the way the AFP delivers its
operations. These capabilities are applied responsibly to anticipate and respond to emerging
threats, supporting more effective policing while maintaining public confidence and trust.
Through engagement with the private sector and other stakeholders, the Australian Centre to
Counter Child Exploitation (ACCCE) actively considers emerging technology and how it can
be used in the fight against child abuse material. The ACCCE assesses emerging technology
utilising AFP guidelines to ensure transparency and compliance with ethical and privacy
requirements.
The Australian Victim Identification Database (AVID), hosted by the ACCCE, provides a
central database of child abuse material and related media for cross jurisdictional law
enforcement purposes, underpinned by an international standard for the categorisation of
child abuse material. This provides a supported online system allowing real-time updates and
reduces investigator exposure to duplicate child abuse material media. The database aims to
improve law enforcement opportunities to locate and rescue child victims of sexual
exploitation and more effectively identify offenders.
ACCCE platforms are designed to include a range of embedded user wellbeing features to
reduce investigator exposure to distressing material, support psychological health, and
improve operational efficiency when handling sensitive and illegal digital content, including
child abuse material. These platforms provide several content minimisation and controlled
exposure mechanisms to support workplace health and operational sustainability.
Artificial Intelligence for Law Enforcement and Community Safety (AiLECS) Lab is a
collaboration between the AFP and Monash University. It is an Australian-first research lab
investigating the ethical use of artificial intelligence in law enforcement and community
safety. The AiLECS Lab was conceived as a model and platform for artificial intelligence
research related to law enforcement on an international scale.
7
Through agreement between the AFP and CDPP, AFP child exploitation investigators will
conduct a manual identification of an agreed number of child abuse images and videos per
device for prosecution purposes. This reduces the exposure of AFP child exploitation
investigators to child abuse material seized during investigations.
The CDPP also proactively reviews its procedures to balance the wellbeing of investigators
and prosecutors while ensuring courts are properly informed about the severity of offending
when presenting evidence in court for trials or sentence hearings. In June 2023 and
September 2024, the CDPP updated two of its internal Practice Group Instructions which deal
with the use of sample images of child abuse material in sentencing proceedings for child
exploitation matters, and work health and safety issues arising from dealing with child abuse
material.
The first Practice Group Instruction provides guidance to prosecutors on procedures to ensure
that courts are properly informed about the severity of the offending, while limiting exposure
to the material, wherever possible. The second Practice Group Instruction sets out
instructions to prosecutors aimed at limiting the exposure of CDPP staff to child abuse
material wherever possible, including by reducing the chance of inadvertent exposure to the
material, and by carefully monitoring staff file loads so as to ensure that any individual
prosecutor does not deal with an overwhelming amount of child abuse material as part of
their individual practice.
Collaboration with states and territories
The AFP is a member of the Australia and New Zealand Child Protection Working Group
(formerly Operation Griffin). This working group aims to address victim-based crime
investigating the exploitation and abuse of children nationally and internationally that have a
direct impact on Australia and New Zealand. The working group, to which all state and
territory law enforcement contribute, is used as a forum for exchange of best practice and
emerging trends.
The AFP has developed the Online Child Exploitation Investigations Program, which
provides participants with the knowledge, skills and capability to successfully conduct
complex, sensitive and/or protracted online child sexual exploitation investigations. This
program is available for state and territory police working in Joint Anti Child Exploitation
Teams to attend.

Recommendation 7
Recognising the extreme nature of the content associated with child exploitation
investigations, the committee recommends that the Australian Centre to Counter Child
Exploitation prioritise efforts to share best practice with Joint Anti Child Exploitation
Teams and other law enforcement agencies regarding:
• support for officers’ health and wellbeing; and
• training to ensure that officers are well-equipped to perform their duties.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government is committed to supporting investigative officers and sharing best practices
with other law enforcement agencies.
The AFP continues to share best practice and lessons learned from its health and wellbeing
initiatives with state, territory and international law enforcement partners through
interjurisdictional forums such as the Australia and New Zealand Child Protection Working
Group and the Virtual Global Taskforce.
The ACCCE prioritises the wellbeing of members working in child protection. The ACCCE
has developed nine Health and Wellness Principles, which serve as an aspirational guide for
how the ACCCE addresses health and wellbeing. These principles have been informed by
workforce feedback, advice from international partners, and guidance from internal support
services.
The nine principles are designed to complement existing AFP governance.
The ACCCE Training Team is responsible for developing and facilitating training for AFP
investigators working in Child Protection and Joint Anti Child Exploitation Teams. Some of
the training programs and workshops facilitated by the ACCCE Training Team to better
equip officers perform their duties include:
• AFP Child Protection Induction Workshop, which provides members a key overview of
the ACCCE, investigations and prosecution of child exploitation offences and offender
typology
• AFP Online Child Exploitation Investigations Program, which provides participants with
the knowledge, skills and capability to successfully conduct complex, sensitive and/or
protracted online child sexual exploitation investigations, and
• AFP National Interviewing Vulnerable Persons Program, which equips investigators with
the necessary skills and knowledge to plan, prepare for and conduct Evidence in Chief
Interviews with vulnerable persons/children, including witnesses and suspects.
State and territory law enforcement agencies have successfully participated in a number of
these training programs and courses in recent years.
9
The ACCCE has developed and delivered three levels of Victim Identification
training. This includes an online awareness training program for first responders and law
enforcement who come across child abuse material in the course of their duties, to improve
understanding of the victim-centric approach to online child sexual abuse investigations and
improve collaborative victim identification efforts. This framework also includes foundation
training for new victim identification members within both the AFP and state and territory
law enforcement agencies, and a specialist victim identification training program, for more
experienced victim identification members of the AFP and state and territory law
enforcement agencies.
The AFP will continue to engage with Joint Anti Child Exploitation Teams and state and
territory law enforcement agencies through existing forums to share best practice, trainings
and supports.
Recommendation 8
The committee recommends that the eSafety Commissioner apply pressure to technology
companies to ensure that they apply robust procedures and technologies to detect child
abuse material and report it to law enforcement, regardless of whether end-to-end
encryption is used.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government acknowledges the pivotal role technology companies have in detecting child
abuse material. Ensuring there are appropriate measures to ensure technology companies
work with law enforcement is key to strengthening Australia’s ability to combat child abuse
material.
The eSafety Commissioner, Australia’s independent online safety regulator, has a range of
powers under the Online Safety Act 2021 (Cth) (the Online Safety Act). The Online Safety
Act allows the Commissioner to compel the takedown of illegal or harmful content, including
child sexual abuse material, to require reporting from regulated online services to improve
transparency and accountability, and ensure improvements in online safety through industry
codes and standards.
The Online Safety Act also enables the Minister for Communications to set Basic Online
Safety Expectations (BOSE) for certain services. The Online Safety (Basic Online Safety
Expectations) Determination 2022 includes expectations that companies will take reasonable
steps to:
• proactively minimise the extent to which material or activity on a service is unlawful
or harmful (section 6).
• minimise the extent to which class 1 material (including child sexual exploitation and
abuse material) is provided on their service (section 11).
Under the Online Safety Act, the eSafety Commissioner may issue notices requiring online
service providers to report their compliance with the BOSE. Failure to comply with a
reporting notice may result in civil penalties up to $910,000. The eSafety Commissioner may
also issue public statements regarding a service’s compliance or non-compliance with the
BOSE.
10
As of April 2026, the eSafety Commissioner has issued 35 notices to service providers on
their compliance with the BOSE. Many of these notices focussed on the steps companies are
taking to detect child sexual exploitation and abuse, including on their end-to-end encrypted
services, with transparency reports published on the findings obtained to date.
In 2023, the eSafety Commissioner registered 6 enforceable industry codes (the Unlawful
Material codes) to regulate illegal content across six online sectors. Under the Social Media
Services Code, higher risk social media providers are required to deploy systems, processes
and/or technology to automatically detect and flag known child sexual abuse material, and
implement techniques that detect, disrupt and/or deter both known and first-generation child
sexual abuse material. Social media service providers are also required to report child sexual
abuse material to law enforcement within 24 hours or as soon as reasonably practicable, if
there is a serious and immediate threat to the life or physical health or safety of an adult or
child in Australia. These requirements are enforceable. eSafety is monitoring compliance and
will take enforcement action to ensure industry participants comply with their obligations.
In addition, in June 2024, eSafety registered mandatory industry standards for relevant
electronic services (including online messaging, text/video chat, email, dating, gaming and
enterprise communication) and designated internet services (websites, apps and other online
services such as online file/photo storage which do not fall within the other categories). The
Unlawful Material standards require providers to implement systems, processes and
technologies to effectively disrupt and deter end-users of the service from using the service to
create, offer, solicit, access, distribute, or otherwise make available, or store child sexual
abuse material. These obligations apply to services that use end-to-end encryption but do not
require those services to do anything that would break or weaken encryption.
The standards also require providers to report child sexual abuse material to an enforcement
authority as soon as practical if they become aware of it and consider there to be an
immediate threat to the life or physical safety of a person in Australia.
Where a provider fails to meet a direction to comply with an industry code, or to comply with
an industry standard, the eSafety Commissioner can take enforcement action including
seeking a civil penalty of up to $54.6 million.
The Government has committed to legislate a digital duty of care for online services
operating in Australia – a key recommendation of the independent statutory review of the
Online Safety Act. The duty of care will place obligations on services to exercise due
diligence and take reasonable steps to prevent reasonably foreseeable online harms, including
child sexual exploitation and abuse. In line with the review recommendations, the duty of
care will require services to encompass safety by design and conduct ongoing cycles of risk
assessment, mitigation, review and reporting across their systems.

Recommendation 9
The committee recommends that the Australian Centre to Counter Child Exploitation or, if
necessary, another appropriate law enforcement body:
• monitor the number and quality of reports of child abuse material being made by
technology companies in order to identify any deficiencies in detection processes,
particularly where end-to-end encryption may be associated with the deficiencies;
and
• if deficiencies are identified, liaise with the eSafety Commissioner about improving
the detection processes applied by technology companies.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The AFP holds serious concerns about the widespread rollout of end-to-end encryption
(E2EE) across major communication platforms and applications in a way that limits the
ability of communications service providers to proactively identify criminal behaviour and
harm on their own platforms and report it to law enforcement, and prevents targeted access
by law enforcement under lawfully issued warrants for the purpose of investigating serious
crime and preventing further harm to victims.
In 2026 over 93% of the AFP’s lawfully intercepted content across all crime types was
unintelligible due to encryption. Without implementing appropriate safeguards, the
widespread implementation of E2EE by major technology and social media companies has
the potential to reduce the AFP’s ability to investigate reports made to the ACCCE.
In 2023, the AFP renewed its Memorandum of Understanding with the National Center for
Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC), which is a non-profit corporation whose mission
is to help find missing children, reduce child sexual exploitation and prevent victimisation.
The renewed MoU allows for a continued bilateral partnership between the two organisations
and formalises the provision of AFP financial support to NCMEC’s program of work. This
includes its efforts to prevent child exploitation by operating the CyberTipline. Amongst
other things, NCMEC’s CyberTipline allows US-based electronic service providers to report
suspected child abuse material, which is forwarded to the AFP where relevant.
The AFP, particularly through the ACCCE, maintains a close working relationship with
eSafety and regularly exchanges data regarding online reports relating to online child sexual
exploitation.
Recommendation 10
The committee recommends that the Australian Government and eSafety Commissioner, as
part of their work on the Roadmap for age verification and complementary measures to
prevent and mitigate harms to children from online pornography and industry codes for
class 2 material, respectively, consider options for age assurance or age verification of
children using social media, online games or other online platforms to ensure that
age based protections are effective.
12
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government is committed to minimising the harm that comes from children accessing
content online that is not appropriate for them – including online pornography. Age assurance
or age verification is used across a range of areas to uplift online safety.
In the 2024-25 Budget, the Government provided $6.5 million to conduct a trial of age
assurance technologies to protect children from harm online, including on social media, and
age-restricted content such as pornography. The final report of the Age Assurance
Technology Trial was published on 31 August 2025, available at
infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/publications/age-assurance-technology-trial-final-
report. The social media minimum age was passed on 29 November 2024 and came into
effect on 10 December 2025, 12 months after Royal Assent. The social media minimum age
requires certain social media platforms to take reasonable steps to prevent Australians under
16 from having an account.
The Government is focussed on the effective implementation of the Online Safety Act,
including the creation of industry codes and standards to keep all Australians safe online.
The Unlawful Material Codes and Standards which deal with illegal and seriously harmful
content, discussed in response to Recommendation 8, are phase 1 of this process. In 2025,
eSafety completed phase 2 of the process by registering 9 industry codes (the Age-Restricted
Material Codes) to limit children’s access to age-inappropriate content such as online
pornography. The codes apply to eight sections of the digital industry – including
pornography sites and app stores as well as sites where children may unintentionally come
across pornography like social media and gaming sites. Under the codes, certain services that
host or facilitate access to age-restricted material (such as online pornography), need to take
measures to prevent children’s access, including use of age assurance measures. These codes
are all now in force.
Like the Unlawful Material codes and standards discussed in Recommendation 8, the eSafety
Commissioner can take enforcement action against breaches of directions to comply with the
Age-Restricted Material codes, including seeking a civil penalty of up to $54.6 million for a
company.

Recommendation 11
The committee recommends that the Australian Government consider options for requiring
major technology companies operating in Australia to have an appropriate number of trust
and safety staff based in Australia.
The Government agrees in-principle with this recommendation.
It is the Government’s clear expectation that companies operating in Australia have
appropriate levels of trust and safety staff available to support Australian users. Appropriate
resourcing and investment in human and technological interventions is critical to a service’s
ability to safeguard users from material and activity that is unlawful or harmful.
As outlined in the response to Recommendations 8 and 10, the eSafety Commissioner has
registered industry codes and standards for social media services, hosting services, app stores,
internet service providers, equipment providers, search engines, relevant electronic services,
and designated internet services, covering both class 1 material, including child sexual
exploitation material, and class 2 material, including online pornography. These codes and
standards contain enforceable requirements for relevant services to have adequate trust and
safety personnel for the purposes of ensuring compliance with the codes and standards.
The Online Safety (Basic Online Safety Expectations) Determination 2022 includes relevant
expectations and examples of reasonable steps, including that service providers should action
reports and complaints from end-users within a reasonable period of time, and have
appropriate staff, systems, tools and processes to facilitate timely responses.
Recommendation 12
The committee recommends that the Australian Government commission further research
into the links between contact and non-contact child sex offending to better inform how
law enforcement and other agencies detect, investigate, and prevent this crime.
The Government notes this recommendation.
The Government acknowledges the importance of continued research to build evidence on
trends and changes in relation to the risk, extent and impact of child sexual abuse
victimisation and offending in Australia. The Government draws on a range of domestic and
international research including from the Australian Institute of Criminology and academia,
to inform policy and law reform.
Under the National Strategy to Prevent and Respond to Child Sexual Abuse 2021-2030, the
Attorney-General’s Department is developing a Child Safety Research Agenda (the Agenda).
The Agenda will coordinate and drive national research to prevent and respond to child
sexual abuse through:
• building evidence on the risk, extent and impact of child sexual abuse victimisation
and offending, and
• linking government and non-government stakeholders with researchers.
Key stakeholders, including Commonwealth, state and territory governments, relevant
14
advisory and working groups, child safety researchers, law enforcement and international
counterparts have been consulted during the development of the Child Safety Research
Agenda. Stakeholder engagement has sought insights on implementation, existing research
programs and agendas, and emerging research needs.
Recommendation 13
To inform the implementation of prevention services that target adults who have sexual
thoughts about children, the committee recommends that Australian Government
commission research into strategies for ensuring such services are targeted and effective.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government is committed to supporting implementation of prevention services that
target adults with sexual thoughts about children, and is working to progress a number of
initiatives.
Research
As mentioned in the response to Recommendation 12, the Attorney-General’s Department is
developing the Child Safety Research Agenda to develop and deliver a national agenda to
coordinate and drive national research to prevent and respond to child sexual abuse.
Offending Prevention Service
The Attorney-General’s Department has recently launched Australia’s first national child
sexual abuse offending prevention service, Stop It Now! Australia, under the National
Strategy to Prevent and Respond to Child Sexual Abuse 2021-2030. The design of the
Offending Prevention Service is based on research conducted by the Attorney-General’s
Department, including work commissioned from Lonergan Research, engagement with
domestic and international experts and extensive stakeholder consultation.
The objective of the Offending Prevention Service is to protect children from sexual abuse by
intervening before an individual offends or re-offends. It provides advice and guidance to
people who are worried about their own sexual thoughts and behaviours towards children or
young people, or have concerns about someone else’s behaviour. This advice and guidance is
accessible via a national helpline and website that community members can use
anonymously: stopitnow.org.au.
Recidivism Reduction Pilot
Under the National Strategy, the Attorney-General’s Department has also conducted a
scoping study and is progressing a procurement process to secure a service provider(s) for a
national pilot service to prevent child sexual abuse reoffending. The pilot will work with
child sexual abuse offenders after they have been released from prison, to promote
accountability and prevent further harm. This program will work alongside mandated
requirements, such as parole conditions, to offer a strong, evidence-based approach to
managing offenders once they have been released back into the community. The Australian
Institute of Criminology has been engaged to design and deliver an evaluation of the
effectiveness of the pilot service.

Recommendation 14
Notwithstanding the current ‘One Talk at a Time’ public information campaign, the
committee recommends that the Australian Government implement ongoing efforts to
increase public awareness of the risks of child exploitation, particularly among parents,
including how to reduce and respond to these risks.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Government is committed to increasing public awareness and education of the risks of
child sexual abuse among all adults, including parents and carers. The Government
recognises that improving awareness and understanding of child sexual abuse, including how
to reduce and respond to these risks, will help to prevent abuse by challenging
misconceptions, reducing shame and stigma, and creating safe environments for children and
young people.
The ‘One Talk at a Time’ campaign, a measure under the National Strategy to Prevent and
Respond to Child Sexual Abuse 2021-2030, is Australia’s first national campaign aimed at
preventing child sexual abuse. It was launched by the Government on 22 October 2023 and
aims to protect children and young people from sexual abuse by helping adults (including
parents, kin and other carers) understand that child sexual abuse is preventable and
encouraging them to have ongoing, proactive, preventative conversations with children,
young people and other adults in their lives. ‘One Talk at a Time’ is intended as a long-term,
multi-phase communication effort aimed at promoting behavioural and cultural change to
protect children and young people in Australia.
To support the ‘One Talk at a Time’ campaign, the Government has developed a range of
educative tools and resources to support adults to learn more about child sexual abuse,
including signs and indicators, grooming and online safety, and to help them to have
preventative conversations. Development of these resources has been an ongoing effort
involving collaboration with child safety experts and organisations to ensure resources are
effective, culturally safe, trauma-informed and accessible.
In addition to the ‘One Talk at a Time’ campaign, the Government continues to identify other
innovative ways to reach an audience of technology consumers, non-government
organisations and the broader Australian community in relation to the risks of child sexual
abuse, including online. On 30 August 2024, the Attorney-General’s Department launched
The Shadows of the Web – Protecting our children in the digital world. This evidence-based
documentary was funded by the Attorney-General’s Department under the National Strategy
to Prevent and Respond to Child Sexual Abuse 2021-2030 and aims to inform an audience of
technology consumers, the Australian community and international partners on the escalating
threat of child sexual exploitation and abuse.

Recommendation 15
The committee recommends that the Australian Government, as part of its planned reforms
of the Classification Scheme and in consultation with the eSafety Commissioner, consider
options for the Classification Scheme to better convey the risks of computer games that
allow interaction with other players or the uploading of user-generated content.
The Government agrees in-principle with this recommendation.
The Government is committed to supporting improvements to the National Classification
Scheme (the Scheme) to accurately convey the risks of computer games. Australians rely on
the Scheme to make informed media choices, especially when it comes to the content being
accessed by the children in their care.
Successive reviews have found that the Scheme has not kept pace with the way Australians
access media content, particularly the rapid growth in digital and online content and the
emergence of new content sharing platforms and interactive features, or with evolving
community standards.
In March 2023, the Government announced a two-stage reform process to modernise the
Scheme. The Government undertook a public consultation process on proposed updates to the
classification guidelines from March to May 2026, including the guidelines for the
classification of computer games. Proposed updates aim to ensure the guidelines are fit for
purpose in addressing areas of community concern, including around online interactivity and
sexually explicit content.
The Government also brought forward a legislated review of the Online Safety Act as part of
a holistic assessment of the framework. The final report of the review of the Online Safety
Act was provided to Government on 31 October 2024. The review was conducted by Ms
Delia Rickard PSM and includes a recommendation to decouple the Online Safety Act from
the Scheme. On 14 April 2026, the Government released its response to the Report of the
Statutory Review of the Act. As an immediate priority, the Government has committed to
reforms that incentivise harm prevention, including implementing a digital duty of care. The
duty of care will place obligations on service providers to proactively and effectively manage
the risk of harms from the use and misuse of their platforms and services – something that
they are best placed to do.

Coalition dissenting recommendations
Recommendation 1
Coalition members of the committee recommend that, in line with the eSafety
Commissioner’s recommendations in the Roadmap for age verification and complementary
measures to prevent and mitigate harms to children from online pornography, the
Australian Government implement and evaluate a trial of age assurance technologies and
the use of digital tokens in the Australian context.
The Government agrees with this recommendation.
The Roadmap for Age Verification, released in August 2023, made several recommendations,
including that eSafety continue to implement the Online Safety Act, and that age assurance
technologies be piloted in Australia before seeking to mandate them.
In the 2024-25 Budget, the Government provided $6.5 million to conduct a trial of age
assurance technologies to protect children from harm online, including on social media, and
age-restricted content such as pornography. The final report of the Age Assurance
Technology Trial was published on 31 August 2025, available at
infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/publications/age-assurance-technology-trial-final-
report.
Recommendation 2
Coalition members of the committee recommend that the Australian Government work
with states and territories to implement a national public register of child sex offenders.
The Government notes this recommendation.
The Government is committed to protecting children from sexual abuse and other harms. This
includes working with states and territories to consider the most effective way to manage
convicted child sexual abuse offenders living in the community.
Any national scheme to publicly release information about registered child sexual offenders
can only be implemented with the support of the states and territories, who manage the
relevant data and have laws prescribing its disclosure.
The Government is progressing nationally agreed reforms to WWCCs under the SCAG
Agreement to Deliver National WWCC Reform.
The establishment of the National Continuous Checking Capability (NCCC), led by the
ACIC, will enable ongoing, near‑real‑time monitoring of national criminal history
information for WWCC holders. Once fully implemented and adopted by all states and
territories, the NCCC will close existing information gaps by ensuring that all relevant
criminal history, including both Commonwealth and state and territory offences, is identified
promptly rather than only at the point of application or renewal.
19
National mutual recognition of negative WWCC decisions is a critical safeguard to prevent
individuals who pose a risk to children from exploiting differences between state and territory
systems. Under this reform, a person who is refused or has their WWCC revoked in one
jurisdiction will be unable to obtain or hold a WWCC in another, closing a significant
loophole that previously enabled forum‑shopping.
Together with national mutual recognition of WWCCs, underpinned by meaningful
consistency in automatic exclusion criteria, risk assessment frameworks and information
sharing across jurisdictions, these reforms will strengthen safeguards for children and deliver
a more nationally consistent approach to worker screening.
The Government will continue to work with states and territories to identify and support
evidence-based initiatives to enhance child safety in the community and prevent offender
recidivism.
